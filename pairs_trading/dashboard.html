Statistical arbitrage · Indian equities 47 stocks · 1,081 pairs · 2012–2021

Can you build a pairs trading strategy on Nifty 50 stocks?

Yes — cointegrated pairs genuinely exist inside the index, and a walk-forward strategy on them makes money out of sample. But the edge is thin, it shrank every year of the sample, and it dies at roughly 80 basis points of round-trip cost.

The short answer. Screening all 1,081 pairs each quarter, trading the 10 most strongly cointegrated ones on a 60-day rolling z-score, and charging 10 bps per leg per side produces a Sharpe of 0.54 on 4.4% volatility over seven out-of-sample years — a 2.3% CAGR unlevered, 5.3% levered to a 10% vol target. It has almost no market beta (0.04) and a maximum drawdown of 8.8% against the index's 38%.

It also fails to clear a 95% significance bar (bootstrapped Sharpe CI −0.21 to +1.29), and the classic textbook version of the same strategy — a fixed formation-period z-score, as in Gatev et al. — loses money on this universe. What works here is narrow, and knowing which knob matters is most of the answer.

Construction

How the strategy is built

Everything below is walk-forward. Pairs are chosen on a three-year formation window and traded on the quarter that follows it — the trading window never contributes to its own pair selection, hedge ratio or entry thresholds.

  1. Screen every pair on log pricesAll 1,081 combinations of 47 stocks. Keep pairs with log-price correlation > 0.70, then run an Engle–Granger regression in both directions and keep the orientation with the stronger residual stationarity.
  2. Filter on mean-reversion qualityADF p-value < 0.05 on the residual, Ornstein–Uhlenbeck half-life between 5 and 60 days, at least 12 mean crossings per formation year, and a positive hedge ratio. Typically 60–230 of the 1,081 pairs survive.
  3. Trade the 10 most stationaryRank survivors by ADF p-value, take the top 10, equal-weight the capital. Each pair holds ₹1 of the dependent leg against ₹β of the independent leg, scaled so gross exposure per pair is constant.
  4. Signal on a 60-day rolling z-scoreEnter when |z| > 2.0 (short the rich leg, long the cheap leg), exit at |z| < 0.5, stop out at |z| > 3.5, force-close after 60 days. The rolling window is trailing-only — this single choice is what separates a profitable strategy from a losing one.
  5. Re-screen every 63 trading daysPairs decay. Re-running the screen each quarter replaces broken relationships; open positions close at the boundary, which is why 31% of trades exit on the window edge rather than at the target.
  6. Charge 10 bps per leg, per side40 bps per completed round trip across both legs — brokerage, STT, exchange charges, impact and slippage for a Nifty 50 name. The short leg assumes stock futures, since cash-market shorts cannot be carried overnight in India.
Out-of-sample result

Seven years of walk-forward equity

The strategy compounds quietly and independently of the market. It is not a competitor to owning the index — it is an uncorrelated, low-volatility return stream that only becomes interesting with leverage or as a diversifier.

The binding constraint

Transaction costs decide everything

At zero cost this is a Sharpe-1.1 strategy. Every basis point of friction takes a visible bite, and the whole edge is gone at 20 bps per leg per side.

Why this matters more here than in a US study. Average holding period is 15 days and the book turns over roughly 60 round trips a year across 10 pairs. Indian large-cap STT plus impact on a two-leg trade realistically lands in the 25–60 bps round-trip range — inside the profitable zone, but not by a wide margin. Any execution sloppiness, and the strategy is a coin flip.
Signal design

One parameter separates profit from loss

Fourteen configurations, all run over the same out-of-sample period. The split is not subtle: every variant that calibrates the z-score on the formation window loses money, and every variant that uses a trailing rolling window makes it.

Why static z-scores fail

A spread's mean drifts. Calibrating μ and σ on a three-year formation window and then holding them fixed for a year means that by month six a normal spread reads as a 3-sigma event. 66% of static-z trades exit on the stop-loss versus 8% of rolling-z trades.

Why rolling betas also fail

Letting the hedge ratio float on a 120-day window (variant C) sounds more adaptive and scores −0.74. A drifting β re-anchors the spread continuously, which destroys the very stationarity the screen selected for. Fix β at formation; let only the z-score adapt.

Fewer pairs, not more

Concentrating in the 5–10 most stationary pairs beats diversifying across 20–40. The ADF ranking carries real information: pairs ranked 10–40 add trades and costs without adding edge.

Structural finding

The opportunity set is shrinking

This is the most durable result in the study and the one least dependent on strategy design: the number of Nifty 50 pairs that pass a cointegration screen fell by roughly two-thirds between 2015 and late 2020.

190 pairs passed, 17.6% of the universe. Median half-life 24 days.

Dec 2020

61 pairs passed, 5.6% of the universe — the low of the sample.

Of all survivors

13% were same-sector. Most cointegration in the index is cross-sector and economically accidental.

Trade anatomy

How the edge is actually earned

A high hit rate with a payoff ratio below one — many small wins funding fewer, larger losses. That is the normal signature of a mean-reversion book, and it is also why the stop-loss policy matters so much.

Reading the result honestly

What would stop me deploying this as-is

It is not statistically significant

A 2,000-draw bootstrap puts the Sharpe's 95% interval at −0.21 to +1.29, with an 8.2% chance the true Sharpe is at or below zero. Seven years and 420 trades is simply not enough to distinguish 0.54 from noise.

The configuration was chosen in-sample

The 14 variants and the 15 grid cells were all evaluated on the same out-of-sample period. The honest headline is the distribution of that family — median Sharpe around 0.45 — not the best cell in it.

Survivorship bias

The universe is the Nifty 50 as constituted at the end of the sample, back-filled to 2012. Stocks that were in the index and got dropped are absent, which flatters both the strategy and the benchmark.

Execution assumptions

Fills are at the closing price on the signal day, both legs, with no borrow cost, no futures roll cost, no basis risk between the cash and futures leg, and no capacity limit. Each of those is a real deduction.

The data ends in 2021

The freely available dataset used here stops at 31 December 2021. The declining-cointegration trend implies the most recent four years are the hardest, and they are exactly the ones not tested.

Two constituents are missing

HDFCLIFE and SBILIFE listed in late 2017 and cannot support a three-year formation window early in the sample, so the universe is 47 rather than 49 names.

Where I would take it next. Three changes have the best chance of moving this from marginal to deployable: widen the universe to the Nifty 200, where far more genuine same-sector pairs exist and the cointegration decay is less severe; replace the pairwise screen with a multi-asset cointegration basket so the hedge is a portfolio rather than one stock; and model execution against actual stock-futures spreads rather than closing prices, because on these numbers execution quality is the strategy.

Method. Engle–Granger two-step cointegration on log adjusted closing prices; Augmented Dickey–Fuller with one lag and a constant on the residual; half-life from an AR(1) fit of the residual's first difference. Walk-forward with a 756-day formation window and 63-day trading windows. All performance is net of 10 bps per leg per side unless stated.

Data. Daily OHLCV and adjusted closes for 47 Nifty 50 constituents, 2 January 2012 – 31 December 2021, 2,465 trading days, from a public NIFTY 500 dataset on GitHub. Benchmark is an equal-weight buy-and-hold of the same 47 names over identical dates.
