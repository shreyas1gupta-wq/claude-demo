The Link That Got Stronger

The ENSO record carried an honest debt: three published countings of "how often does El Niño convert to an Indian drought" that refused to agree — 71%, 44%, 47% — flagged in the register as unreconciled, with a defended range of "roughly 45-70%" and a promise to compute the real number when the data landed. The data landed. The number is 56%, the flag is resolved, and the era split rewrote where the chain's fragility actually lives: not in the sky, but in the buffers underneath it.

PART 1

Why three countings could not agree

Each published counting used a different El Niño definition (an ONI threshold, an India-specific indicator, an onset-year cross-reference), a different span, and a different drought list. That is not sloppiness — it is what happens when a question lives in prose instead of code. The register's response was characteristic: print all three, flag the irreconcilability with a [VERIFY], defend only the range, and register EN-D2 — the contingency table re-derived against the live record once authenticated data existed. The debt sat there, visible, for exactly as long as it had to.

PART 2

One definition pair, fixed before the table

Two mirrors passed the two-pass gauntlet: the NOAA ONI table (1950 through mid-2026, five anchors including the 1997 peak matched to the digit: +2.40) and the IITM-shape all-India monsoon series (1872-2016, five anchors including the Great Famine's 604mm and a full-sample mean of 848.2mm — the canonical scale, which is how you authenticate units). Then, before any table was computed, the definitions were committed: an El Niño year is one whose monsoon-season ONI average reaches +0.5; a deficient year is one below 90% of the series' own long-period average; the verification bar was the record's own defended range, 45-70%. No degrees of freedom left at run time. The result: sixteen El Niño years, nine deficient — 56.2%, inside the bar — against a 14% base rate in all other years. Lift 4.1x. The first link is real, and it now stands on one reconciled counting instead of three irreconcilable ones. Robustness came free: a second, CPC-label-based El Niño definition agrees in 94% of years, and swapping the LPA window moves nothing.

PART 3

The era split rewrites the fragility story

The cases chapter had documented falling drought severity across the Green-Revolution and liberalization boundaries and warned that a pooled base rate would overstate modern transmission. The computed split says something sharper: the METEOROLOGICAL link has, if anything, strengthened — 29% conversion in 1950-69, but 67% versus a 5% base rate in 1991-2016, a 13x lift. What weakened across those boundaries was never the sky; it was the transmission BELOW the sky — irrigation, buffer stocks, the policy response that kept 2014-15's twin deficits out of headline CPI. The chain conditioner's design — read each realized link in order, never jump from the ENSO state to a trade — was right for a better reason than the desk knew when it registered it. And 1997 stands confirmed as the great save: a super-El Niño, no deficiency, the year that teaches why link one alone licenses nothing.

PART 4

What this buys, and what it doesn't

H55 remains exactly what it was: a Tier-C candidate. Its promotion test (EN-D1 — sector returns conditioned on realized rainfall deficits and food-CPI spikes, purged, era-split) still waits on data this environment cannot pull. What changed is the quality of the ground under it: the base-rate exhibit is now computed, reconciled, era-split, and wired to an annual re-print — the 2023-24 El Niño's monsoon enters the table the day the IMD primary lands. The census stands at 134 consumed looks. A flag raised honestly, carried visibly, and resolved by pre-registered computation is the register working exactly as designed — the debt was never the problem; unrecorded debts are.
