PART 1

What "reserve currency" actually means

Four different jobs travel under one name: the currency trade is invoiced in, the currency banks fund in, the currency smaller countries anchor to, and the currency central banks hold reserves in. The dollar dominates all four today, but each at a different percentage and with different stickiness. Why does status persist? Network effects: liquidity begets liquidity — you invoice in dollars because everyone else does, which deepens dollar markets, which makes the next contract dollar-denominated too. Economically this is a coordination game with multiple equilibria: the incumbent persists long after its fundamentals peak, and when transitions come they can be nonlinear. That one sentence contains both this lesson's discipline (drift is the base case) and its humility (thresholds can't be timed).

PART 3

The 2022 story is in gold — and COFER can't see it

When Russian FX reserves were frozen in 2022, the measurable response was not a currency switch — it was central banks buying gold, the one reserve asset that is nobody's liability and nobody's jurisdiction. COFER measures FX only, so the decade's most important reserve shift is invisible in Fig 1 — a textbook lesson in knowing what your data cannot say. The monograph's data chapter found the honest measurement problems too: the World Gold Council's purchase estimates can exceed IMF-reported figures by an order of magnitude in a given quarter (estimated unreported buying — the China question), and India's own signal is the cleanest free proxy we found: the RBI moved its gold home — the domestically-held share of its gold jumped from ~38% to ~77% between 2023 and 2026. Our seat therefore pairs two legs that must fire together: COFER drift AND gold accumulation — so no single narrative can trade alone.

PART 4

How orders actually turn — the sterling lesson

The only complete modern transition took roughly four decades and two world wars. The revisionist history matters: the dollar first overtook sterling in reserves in the mid-1920s — multipolarity was normal for decades, sterling partially recovered in the 1930s, and the pound's final wind-down (the sterling area, capital controls, the 1949 and 1967 devaluations) ran into the 1960s. Two lessons cut opposite ways: transitions are glacial (against the doom cycle) — and once war finance and capital controls crack the incumbent's credibility, shares can move faster than consensus expects (against complacency). The monograph scores today's preconditions honestly: the incumbent runs the fiscal arc of Lesson 5, but no challenger yet offers deep, open, rule-of-law markets at scale — and the 1971–1980 case shows an incumbent can survive its own crisis of confidence when it restores real rates (Volcker), which is precisely the r-vs-g hinge the debt monograph watches.
