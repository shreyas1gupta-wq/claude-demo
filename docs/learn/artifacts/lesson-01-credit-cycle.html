PART 0

How to think before you compute

Five ideas, learnable in five minutes, that decide whether everything after them is science or self-deception. Every one of them is wired into the repo as a rule the code enforces, not a good intention.

0.1 — Cycles are states, not clocks

The word "cycle" smuggles in a promise: that the thing repeats on schedule. Almost nothing in markets does. What repeats is the sequence — leverage builds, fragility accumulates, something breaks, balance sheets repair — while the duration of each act varies wildly. So we never ask "what month of the cycle is it?" We ask "where in the sequence are we?" — and we answer with a measured number.

Definition · state variable

A quantity, computed only from data available today, whose level tells you where you are in a process — like a fuel gauge, not a calendar. Our credit state will be a number in [−1, +1]: −1 ≈ post-bust repair, +1 ≈ boom fully mature.

Rule · the clock test

Before treating any cycle as periodic, count the completed periods you can actually observe in your market. Fewer than four? Then the "period" is a story, not a parameter — use the state, never the schedule. (India's credit record post-1991 contains one, maybe two completed down-legs. One or two. The verdict writes itself.)

0.2 — The Slutzky–Yule trap: smoothing invents waves

In 1927 Eugen Slutzky showed something disturbing: take pure random noise — coin flips, no cycle in it whatsoever — and run a moving average through it, and the result looks compellingly wavy. Your eye will find peaks, troughs, even a "period." Every smoothed chart you will ever see, including ours, carries this hazard. Try it yourself:

0.3 — Whatever works, decays

McLean & Pontiff (2016) re-tested 97 published return predictors: performance fell ~26% after the original sample ended and ~58% after publication. Markets learn. So every edge in our model must carry a written survival argument — a reason it persists even though it is public — or take an explicit numeric haircut. The credit cycle has one of the strongest survival arguments in finance; that argument is Part 1's punchline.

0.4 — Look-ahead: the cardinal sin

A backtest commits look-ahead when any number computed "as of March" secretly used April's data. It is the most common way quantitative research lies to its author, and it is often invisible — buried inside a filter, a percentile, a revised data series. Our defense is structural: every transform in the repo is expanding-window — at each date it may use history up to that date and nothing else — and a test literally truncates the future and checks the past doesn't change. In Part 4 you will see look-ahead on a chart: the same filter run honestly and dishonestly, and how different the honest one looks.

0.5 — No magic numbers, and evidence has tiers

Any specific constant ("buy when the gap > 9%") is a claim that needs its own evidence — and is usually just noise that flattered one historical sample. We use ranks ("where is today versus all of history so far?") instead of thresholds, pre-register parameter grids instead of picking single values, and write down every hypothesis before testing it — because re-testing rejected ideas until one "works" manufactures false discoveries. Finally, not all evidence deserves equal weight:

Tier A

≥30 observed episodes; enough to fit and cross-validate on our own market's data. Full rights: may size positions.

Tier B

4–30 episodes, or fewer with ≥10 cross-country analogues. Parameters frozen at inception, borrowed from the global panel. The credit cycle lives here.

Tier C

Narrative, thin, or unverifiable. May only reduce risk, never add it — a rule you'll see enforced in code in Part 4.

PART 1

What credit is, and why it cycles

No prior finance knowledge assumed. Three balance-sheet facts, one psychological fact, and the boom–bust machine assembles itself.

1.1 — Credit, from zero

Credit is borrowed purchasing power: a loan, a bond, a mortgage. Two facts most people never internalize. First, every loan is simultaneously someone's asset (the lender's) and someone's liability (the borrower's) — so "total credit" measures how interconnected and obligated an economy has become. Second, when a bank lends, it doesn't hand over someone else's savings; it creates a deposit — new money — with a keystroke. Bank lending expands the money supply on the way up and contracts it on the way down. That is why credit is the economy's accelerator pedal, in both directions.

The two ratios we will actually use

Credit / GDP — total bank+NBFC lending divided by national income: "how indebted is the economy relative to its ability to pay?"

Credit / Deposits (CD ratio) — lending divided by the deposit base funding it: "how stretched is the banking system's own balance sheet?" India's monthly series runs from 1969; its long-run range is roughly 52% to 80%.

1.2 — Minsky's ladder: stability breeds fragility

Hyman Minsky's insight, ignored for decades and canonized after 2008: calm itself is destabilizing. After years without defaults, lenders and borrowers both extrapolate. Minsky described the drift as three rungs of borrower:

Hedge finance

Income covers interest and principal. Sober. Dominant early in an expansion.

Speculative finance

Income covers interest only; principal must be rolled over. Depends on markets staying open.

Ponzi finance

Income covers neither; the plan is to sell the asset at a higher price. Depends on the boom itself continuing.

The longer the calm, the larger the share of speculative and Ponzi borrowers — invisibly, because rising collateral prices make everyone look creditworthy. Fragility is built in the boom and only revealed in the bust.

1.3 — The feedback loop, both directions

More credit is extended
→
Asset & collateral prices rise
↓
Standards ease; loan-to-value looks safe
←
Lenders look smart; risk models say "calm"

Run it forward: self-reinforcing boom. Now reverse one arrow — income disappoints, or rates rise — and the marginal (Ponzi) borrower fails first, their collateral is sold, prices fall, which impairs the next borrower's collateral, which forces more selling. The reverse loop has an amplifier the forward loop lacks: forced selling. This asymmetry is why busts are faster than booms, and why Jordà, Schularick & Taylor titled their famous paper "credit bites back": recessions that follow credit booms are systematically deeper and slower to heal, whether or not a banking crisis erupts.

1.4 — Why this survives being known

Here is the question Part 0 demands we answer: this mechanism was described by Minsky in the 1970s and by Kindleberger in 1978. Every central banker has read them. Why hasn't the signal been arbitraged to dust?

Survival argument · the strongest in our whole model
The bias sits inside the credit suppliers, and the trade against them is one almost no institution can hold.

Behavioral leg: Baron & Xiong (QJE 2017) showed that after large credit expansions, bank shareholders go on to earn deeply negative returns — and don't demand compensation in advance. The people extending the credit neglect the crash risk they are creating. Knowing about Minsky doesn't immunize you against being paid quarterly to ignore him.

Limits-to-arbitrage leg: the trade that would correct it — shorting the banking system for years, paying carry the whole way, wrong until you're suddenly right — is career-ending at almost any fund. Knowledge does not create capacity.

Consequence for us: the credit state is publishable without dying. The McLean–Pontiff decay that kills stock-picking signals doesn't mechanically apply. This is why L10 anchors the slow end of our ladder.

1.5 — What the signal is, and is not

One honest limitation, stated now so nothing later oversells it: credit conditions forecast the distribution of what comes next — crash probability, drawdown depth, forward risk premia — not the date. On a cycle whose down-legs arrive years apart, timing error is measured in years. That is why, in our architecture, the credit state adjusts permissions (how much leverage we may run, how much hedge we must carry) and never places a directional bet. We do not short booms. We carry less sail into mature ones.

PART 2

Reading the evidence like a quant

The credit-cycle literature rests on two workhorse tools: regression and AUROC. Learn them once, from zero, on toy data you can hover — then the real results table stops being a wall of numbers.

2.1 — Regression, from zero

A regression is a machine for answering: on average, when X is higher by one unit, how much higher is Y? Below are 40 synthetic points generated from a rule we chose ourselves — Y = 2 + 0.8·X + noise — so we know the truth the machine is trying to recover. "Ordinary least squares" (OLS) picks the one line minimizing the summed squared vertical misses. Hover any point to see its residual: the part of Y the line cannot explain.

Three habits when reading any regression claim: (1) slope + uncertainty — a slope without a confidence interval is a rumor; (2) in-sample vs out-of-sample — fitting the past is easy, predicting the future is the test; (3) persistent regressors bias slopes — when X barely moves month to month (like a credit ratio), standard formulas overstate significance, which is why our pre-registered designs specify the Stambaugh correction and Newey–West errors. You don't need the formulas yet; you need to know the traps have names and our designs name them.

2.2 — AUROC, from zero

Crisis prediction isn't "predict Y" but "rank the dangerous years above the safe ones." The standard score is AUROC. Take every historical year, labeled crisis-within-3-years or not, and look at the indicator's value in each group. AUROC answers: if I draw one crisis year and one safe year at random, what's the probability the indicator was higher in the crisis year? 0.5 = coin flip, useless. 1.0 = perfect separation. 2.4 — The India haircut

Every number above is a pooled estimate across many countries and a century. India contributes little of that sample, has different institutions (state-owned banks, financial deepening from a low base), and offers us one-to-two observed down-legs. So the contract requires the haircut be stated, not hidden:

Our working prior — assumption, marked [A]
Pooled AUROC 0.83–0.85 is treated as an upper bound; the working India prior is 0.65–0.75.

Still comfortably better than a coin flip; honest about pooling dilution and thin domestic data. It stands until the first purged, India-conditioned estimate (design R1, Part 5) replaces it — and if that estimate's lower confidence bound falls below 0.55, the seat's weight gets cut. The prior is falsifiable and scheduled for its own execution. That is what makes it science.

PART 3

India's credit history, 1992 → 2026

The domestic record is short but instructive — and it teaches two data-handling lessons no textbook covers.

1992–2002
Post-liberalization NPA cleanup

New recognition norms bite; public-sector-bank gross NPAs grind from ~19% to ~12%. The system starts the era already deleveraging.

2003–2008
The great credit boom

Credit growth peaks at 25–30%/yr — infrastructure and real estate surge. Reported NPAs fall to ~2%, which the boom itself makes look sustainable. Minsky's calm, textbook edition.

2008–2011
GFC pass-through, then re-acceleration

Stimulus re-inflates lending before the prior boom's quality is known. The next bust is seeded here.

2011–2015
Twin Balance Sheet problem

Over-leveraged corporates and stressed banks, both hiding it: true stressed loans ≈12% while reported numbers stay serene.

2015–2018
AQR recognition shock

The RBI's Asset Quality Review forces honesty: GNPA leaps 5.1% → 11.2%. Data lesson #1: this is a measurement break, not a credit event — the loans were already bad. A naive model reads 2015 as a sudden crisis; a careful one splices the series.

2018
IL&FS / NBFC crisis

A shadow-lending funding freeze — invisible in bank-only credit data. Data lesson #2: our credit aggregate must be bank + NBFC, or the fastest-growing fragility never shows up.

2020
COVID

Credit collapse, then forbearance masks true quality for years. Policy-shaped throughout — resists clean classification as a market-driven down-leg.

2021–2024
Unsecured-retail boom

Credit accelerates 16 → 20%/yr, tilted to unsecured personal loans; the CD ratio reaches ~80% — highest since 2005; household debt climbs 26% → 42% of GDP.

Nov 2023
The regulator blinks first

RBI raises risk weights on unsecured retail and NBFC lending by 25pp — the composition signal confirmed by the institution with the best data.

2024–2026
Today

GNPA at a decadal best (2.15%) — remember 2003–08: reported loan quality is a lagging comfort. The CD ratio remains elevated. We are not forecasting; we are locating.

Clock-test verdict
One, at most two, completed down-legs. The credit cycle enters our model as a Tier-B state variable with parameters pooled from the global panel — never as a domestic clock, never with a fitted regime-switching model.

(TBS → AQR → IL&FS reads best as one prolonged episode; COVID is policy-shaped. Two, charitably. The clock test needs four.)

PART 4

Building the state variable — with our actual runs

Everything below ran in our repository. The economy is synthetic — we engineered a boom (months 200–320) and a bust (320–400) so the truth is known — because an estimator that can't recover a truth we planted has no business touching real rupees.

4.1 — The laboratory economy

4.2 — Detrending: why the standard tool is banned here

To say "credit is above trend," you must estimate the trend. The textbook tool — used by the BIS for its official credit-gap — is the Hodrick–Prescott (HP) filter, which fits a smooth curve through the whole series. James Hamilton's 2018 paper, bluntly titled "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter," documented two disqualifying sins. First, it manufactures spurious cycles — Slutzky's trap (Part 0.2) industrialized: HP output looks cyclical even when fed noise. Second, the end-point problem: the trend estimate is least reliable at the end of the sample — which is precisely where you trade — and it silently revises its own past as new data arrives, so the history you backtest on is a history that never existed in real time.

Hamilton's replacement is almost embarrassingly simple — a regression (Part 2.1!):

ratiot = α + β₁·ratiot−h + β₂·ratiot−h−1 + β₃·ratiot−h−2 + β₄·ratiot−h−3 + gapt
Forecast today's ratio from what it was h months ago (and 3 adjacent lags). The part the forecast misses — the residual — is the gap. h sets the horizon: our demo uses h=24 months; production pre-registers a grid of 16–24 quarters (design R4).

Read it plainly: "knowing where the ratio stood two years ago, where would we expect it today — and how far above that expectation are we?" A boom is, operationally, credit being persistently above where its own history said it should be.

4.3 — The look-ahead lesson, made visible

That regression can be fit two ways, and the difference is the deepest lesson in this entire lesson. Full-sample mode fits the coefficients once, on all 480 months — so the "gap" at month 250 was computed with knowledge of month 480. Expanding mode refits at every date using only data up to that date — what you could actually have known. Same equation. Watch what changes:

A confession, on the record. We wrote the test for this module asserting the gap would "rise through the boom and fall through the bust" — the omniscient shape. The run falsified us: that's the orange line's shape, and only hindsight produces it. Under the contract's rules, the falsification is logged in the verification register, the test was rewritten to the measured shape only after it held on 10 of 10 random seeds, and the monograph records the original wrong claim. This is Part 0 operating on its own authors — which is the point of Part 0.

The redesign that falls out: the expanding gap is our impulse input (it detects acceleration and, most violently, the turn), and boom maturity must come from somewhere else — the CD ratio, next.

4.4 — Percentiles instead of thresholds

How high is "high"? Any fixed number is a magic number (Part 0.5). Our answer: at each date, rank today's value against all history up to that date. "The CD ratio is at its 97th percentile since 1969" needs no tuning, adapts as history accumulates, and cannot peek forward — the rank at month 250 uses months 1–250, full stop. (Our no-look-ahead test literally deletes the future and checks the past ranks don't move.)

4.5 — The composite, and a rule enforced in arithmetic

The state combines the two percentiles (rescaled to [−1,+1]) with pre-registered weights. A third input — composition, the share of new credit flowing to the riskiest categories, like 2021–24's unsecured retail — is Tier C: real enough to respect, too thin to trust. Part 0's rule said Tier C may only reduce risk. In code, that is one line: the composition term passes through max(contribution, 0) — since higher state = less risk permission, a hot reading can push the state up (toward caution) but can never pull it down (toward risk-taking).

4.6 — What the test suite certifies

Five properties, each frozen only after holding across ten random economies: the gap fires in the build-out; the gap collapses below −0.05 at the turn; percentiles are range-bound and provably unchanged when the future is deleted; the CD percentile saturates >0.85 late-boom; the state sits ≥0.3 higher in the boom than at the turn and in the aftermath; and the clamp's two guarantees. Run them yourself: python3 -m pytest tests/test_credit_cycle.py. The full phase table lives in research/cycles/01-credit-cycle-DEMO.md.

4.7 — The trail: a number becomes a phase (added 2026-09-01, principal's directive)

A scalar state hides something your eye catches instantly on a sine wave: where the value came from. A credit state of 0.6 reached from 0 → 0.2 → 0.4 is a boom still building; the same 0.6 reached from 1 → 0.9 → 0.8 → 0.7 is a boom unwinding — and the unwind is the dangerous part (busts are faster than booms; that asymmetry is Part 1.3's forced-selling amplifier). This path-dependence has a name — hysteresis — and a standard operational form: the business-cycle clock used by Eurostat and the OECD for their leading indicators. Plot the state's level against its velocity (how fast it's changing), and every cycle becomes a loop through four quadrants:

recovery

level low, rising — repair is ending; the notation is 0.30U

boom

level high, rising — 0.60U: fragility building, not yet revealed

slowdown

level high, falling — 0.60D: same level, opposite meaning; the turn

downturn

level low, falling — deleveraging running its course

The epistemics tax, paid in advance. Extra dimensions are extra ways to fool ourselves: four quadrants slice our already-thin history into thinner cells. So the rules, registered before any data: quadrant boundaries are deterministic (slope sign × level midpoint), never fitted; the smoothing/dead-band choices live on pre-registered grids; and no traded rule may branch on quadrant or age until three pre-registered hypotheses pass — H66 (at matched levels, is falling-from-high really worse? tested on the pooled century panel), H67 (are the labels stable out-of-sample, or a knife-edge?), H68 (does time-in-quadrant carry information — duration dependence?). Until then the phase is computed, logged, and displayed — like here — but the book ignores it.
PART 5

From state to book — and the honest frontier

5.1 — What the state actually moves

L10 feeds the macro-credit block, capped at 20% of the overall regime score. Through that channel, a maturing boom (state rising toward +1) does four quiet things: leverage permission decays toward 1.0×; the hedge floor rises one bucket earlier than it otherwise would; tail-sleeve entries slow; and the quality-sleeve floor binds. A post-bust state does the reverse. Nothing here shorts a boom or calls a top — Part 1.5's limitation, respected in the plumbing.

5.3 — The three boxes

Known today

The mechanism, the pooled effect sizes, the survival argument, the construction — and a module proven to recover engineered booms and busts in real time, with its one wrong assumption caught and logged.

Awaits data

Every India-specific coefficient: AUROC, drawdown slopes, the horizon h, the weights, τ½. All have pre-registered experiments waiting for the Phase-0 pulls.

Never knowable

The date of the next turn. The seat is built so that not knowing it is survivable — that is the entire design.
