Knowing Your Counterparty

The last band of the atlas is the fastest — and speed is where a desk must know exactly who sits on the other side of the trade. The closing entry is three refusals, each argued to its strongest form first: a real premium we decline because collecting it means racing market-makers; a calendar pattern that died in public forty years ago; and a layer of genuine structure we rule out of scope rather than pretend to trade. With these three addresses filed, every row of the Cycle Atlas has its verdict.

PART 1

A real premium we refuse to collect

One-month cross-sectional reversal — buy last month's losers, sell last month's winners, within a liquid universe — is not a data-mining artifact. It is the return to PROVIDING LIQUIDITY: when someone must sell a falling stock now, whoever takes the other side gets paid for immediacy and inventory risk, and that payment spikes exactly when markets are stressed. The mechanism survives being known (someone must always be paid to warehouse risk). So why refuse it?

Because the premium is paid PER TRADE, and the strategy is nothing but trades: turnover runs over a hundred percent a month, India's securities transaction tax alone takes 20 basis points per round trip, and every transaction-cost study in the literature puts reversal first in line to die net of costs. Worse, collecting immediacy premia at daily speed means competing with colocated market-makers whose whole business is this trade. The register's verdict: a Tier-C FLAG with zero return budget — reversal may inform the TIMING of entries the book already wants (don't chase a name that just spiked), and design MR1 defines the only path that could ever unfreeze more: net-of-cost survival in both half-samples plus the decay haircut, graded when stock-level data lands. Even that pass would cap it at "flag".

The counterparty question
Every speed game has a professional on the other side. A weekly-cadence desk wins by not showing up to races it cannot finish.

Against an HFT market-maker in the immediacy trade, we lose. Against no one in a monthly rebalance of slow states, the plumbing map (Lesson 34) is enough.

PART 2

The pattern that died in public

The weekend effect — negative average Monday returns — was documented by French in 1980 on decades of US data, celebrated, harvested… and then it vanished. The data-snooping bootstrap literature later showed why it was always suspect: test enough calendar splits and something "works" by construction. It is the same lesson the register has now taught itself three times with its own money-free prints — the CW3 omnibus null on months, GS1's festival months ranking worst, and here the band's last calendar claim: day-of-week and expiry-day drift carry no mechanism that survives costs at our scale. REJECT, with no trial spent — proving a priced-at-zero negative on daily data would cost more than the claim was ever worth.

PART 3

Out of scope is not a verdict on truth

Intraday structure — the U-shaped volume curve at open and close, auction effects — is REAL, mechanism-backed microstructure. The register neither rejects it nor trades it: this is an end-of-day, weekly-cadence program by design, and execution algorithms inherit standard participation logic. The distinction matters enough to be its own verdict type: a REJECT says "we tested or reasoned this to death"; OUT OF SCOPE says "true or not, it is not this program's game" — and writing it down prevents the silence from ever being read as ignorance, or worse, as an invitation.
