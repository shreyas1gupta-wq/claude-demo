Registered to Die

The atlas's final candidate is the strangest case in the register: a cycle that is almost certainly REAL — semiconductors have swung through more than ten booms and busts since 1976, better sampled than anything else we've examined — attached to a market where it almost certainly doesn't matter. The entry exists to measure that mismatch, and its design is registered with the desk's prior written on the label: we expect this test to fail.

PART 1

A real cycle, for once

Most "cycles" in this atlas failed the clock test — five pre-registered clock trials, five fails. The semiconductor cycle is different: a high-fixed-cost industry where capacity takes years to build responds to demand like the classic cobweb model — shortages trigger fab investment, fabs all arrive together, glut follows, investment stops, the shortage returns. Add double-ordering through the supply chain (customers over-order in shortages, cancel in gluts — the bullwhip), and you get the industry's famous ~4-year swing, visible in the WSTS billings series across five decades. By observed-period count, this could actually pass the clock test that Kitchin, Juglar, and the 18-year property clock all failed.

PART 2

Existence is not transmission

And none of that is the question. The question is whether the chip cycle reaches THIS book — and India's investable "tech" is a services industry. TCS, Infosys, and HCL sell enterprise IT projects and BFSI outsourcing; their revenue cycle follows US corporate IT budgets, not fab utilization. When both fell together (2001, 2008), the common cause was the macro shock — a channel the ladder already owns through L9, the global-cycle seat. And the most recent year is the sharpest divergence on record: fiscal 2026 delivered one of the largest semiconductor upgrades in WSTS history in the same twelve months that Indian IT services stagnated — the chip boom and the services book moving in OPPOSITE directions, exactly what a no-transmission prior predicts. The entry's null hypothesis is exactly that: the semiconductor state adds NOTHING to what L9 already carries.

The design, with its prior on the label
H59-D1: WSTS billings state vs NIFTY IT relative returns, incremental over L9 — registered with the desk's stated expectation that it fails.

Why register a test you expect to fail? Because a measured death blocks the costume. "Semis are cyclical, NIFTY IT is tech, therefore rotate on the chip cycle" is a pitch the desk will hear again; the answer should be a print, not a shrug. A candidate registered to die is still knowledge.

PART 3

The dated reopening clause

One honest wrinkle: India is trying to become a fabrication economy — fab and OSAT incentives, announced projects. If a listed Indian fab ever reaches index membership, the transmission question REOPENS with a genuinely new mechanism, and the entry says so with a dated revisit trigger checked at the annual loop. That is how the register treats change: not by predicting it, but by writing down in advance what event would change the verdict. A second honest unknown is stamped on the cycle itself: the 2023–26 AI-datacenter buildout is one half-swing of history, and nobody gets to call a regime change from inside it.

With H59 filed, every candidate the atlas surfaced (H53–H59) has an entry, a design, or both. The candidate ledger closes; only the capstone remains.
