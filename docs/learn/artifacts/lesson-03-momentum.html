PART 1

The fact that broke efficient markets

Rank every stock by its return over the past year (skipping the most recent month — that bit reverses). Buy the top tenth, short the bottom tenth, hold a month, repeat. That mechanical recipe — no accounting, no forecasts, nothing but past prices — is WML (winners minus losers), and Jegadeesh & Titman's 1993 demonstration that it earned large, persistent profits in a century of US data is still the single most embarrassing fact for the idea that prices instantly reflect everything. PART 2

Why it exists — four engines, one survival argument

No single story explains momentum; four reinforcing ones do, and they disagree usefully about what would kill it:

Slow news diffusion

Information spreads gradually (Hong-Stein): the first move is incomplete, so it continues. Predicts momentum is strongest where attention is thinnest — small, under-covered names. Dies if information gets fast.

The disposition anchor

Holders sell winners too early and cling to losers (Shefrin-Statman; Grinblatt-Han): unrealized-gain overhang makes prices under-travel, then drift. Dies only if human loss-aversion dies.

Overconfidence

Investors over-trust their private views and double down on confirmation (Daniel-Hirshleifer-Subrahmanyam): trends overshoot before they reverse. Predicts momentum coexists with long-run reversal — which it does.

Frog in the pan

A stream of small news gets less attention than one big jump (Da-Gurun-Warachka): gradual movers drift more than jumpers. Directly measurable — and a natural fit for our information-discreteness screens.

Survival argument — weaker than credit's, and priced as such
Momentum survives because exploiting it fully requires surviving its crashes — but it decays more than any slow macro state, and we haircut it accordingly.

The trade against momentum's mispricing is career-risky (tracking error, 2009-style disasters), which protects the premium. But this is a crowded, published, fast cross-sectional anomaly: McLean-Pontiff decay applies with full force, factor funds now hold measurable AUM in it (our crowding monitor's input), and our standing forward haircut is 25–35% off literature effect sizes — escalating by rule, never by mood.

PART 3

The crash — an option you didn't know you sold

Momentum's worst months are not when markets fall. They are when markets rebound after having fallen. The mechanism: after a bear market, the loser decile is full of smashed, leveraged, near-distress names. Those stocks now behave like call options on the market's recovery — nearly worthless if things stay bad, explosive if things turn. A momentum book is SHORT those names, so it is short those calls. When the rally comes, the "losers" can double while the winners plod — and WML implodes. Daniel & Moskowitz formalized it; here it is on both our panels, computed by us:

The worst US months on record (Aug 1932 −76.9%, Sep 1939 −53.6%, Apr 2009 −44.9%) all sit inside the guard's state. What the guard can never do is name the month — only the weather.

PART 5

Taming it — constant risk instead of constant rupees

Lesson 2's vol-targeting idea applies with special force here, because momentum's own volatility is even more forecastable than the market's, and its high-vol states are precisely its crash states. An 83% drawdown becomes 29% — not by predicting anything, only by refusing to hold constant rupees through a variance regime. This is the same physics as Lesson 2, pointed at a sleeve instead of a book, and it is why the momentum sleeve reports to the fast-stress machinery rather than living in isolation.

PART 6

Into the book — and the frontier

What this cycle feeds: the L3 composite (12-1 + 6-1 + 52wk-high ranks, cost-netted before anything trades) is the return engine's first sleeve; L4's trend state confirms regime and schedules hedges inside its frozen 0.20 budget; the crash guard throttles the sleeve, reduce-only, in panic states; the crowding monitor watches factor-fund AUM; and the phase machinery's D-quadrant re-entry idea awaits its H66/F7 verdict before any traded rule uses it.
