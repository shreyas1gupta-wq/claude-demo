Only Mechanisms Buy a Test

The register has already killed five long clocks — Kitchin, business, property, commodity, Kondratieff — with pre-registered bars. Band 4 turns the same knife on the SHORT clocks: the calendar. Twelve months make twelve free hypotheses, and a desk that tests all twelve will "discover" one. So the rule here is stricter than a p-value: a month buys a test only by naming its mechanism first. Two months qualified. One failed. One passed — and was still refused budget.

PART 1

The trap, demonstrated on purpose

Calendar patterns are the oldest data-mining trap in finance: mechanism-free, infinitely re-testable, and manufactured for free by noise (the Slutzky–Yule effect — smooth any random series and apparent cycles appear). Test 12 months at the 5% level and you expect ~0.6 false positives; test day-of-week, half-of-month, and holidays too and a "discovery" is guaranteed. The Contract bans fixed-period calendar cycles outright (§8) unless a mechanism is named.

Trial CW3 ran the omnibus test — all twelve months of Indian market returns at once, Kruskal–Wallis — with the interpretation rule pre-stated before the print: a null confirms the reject; a significant print STILL doesn't promote any month, because that's what a 12-way comparison does. The print: p = 0.354. No calendar structure. And the sweep obligingly produced its own bait: November ranks #1 in volatility, December #1 in median return. Both stay logged and un-interpreted — that refusal is the whole lesson.

Definition · omnibus test

One joint test across all groups ("do the twelve months differ at all?") instead of twelve separate ones. It spends the entire 5% error budget once, where twelve separate tests spend 5% each and quietly become a 46% chance of at least one false alarm.

PART 2

The two months that named a mechanism

February — the Union Budget. India is the rare market whose single largest annual policy reveal sits on a legally fixed date (last day of February until 2016, February 1 since 2017 — both inside the same month). Timing is law; direction is unknowable (Lesson 32's 3/8). The claim is therefore about VOLATILITY, not return: trial CW1.

April — the fiscal-year end. India's tax year closes March 31. Tax-motivated selling depresses small caps into FY-end and the pressure lifts in April — the same mechanism as the US "January effect" (Ritter, Roll), shifted by the fiscal calendar. Prediction: specifically small-cap, specifically April. Trial CW2, on the size factor (SMB), with the month named before any number was seen.

PART 3

The prints

CW1 failed — and the failure taught the register a theorem it had already met once (the 2025 crowding unwind, invisible in monthly factors): an event-day phenomenon must be tested at event-day resolution. A 1–3 day vol spike drowns inside a month. So the L5 budget window rests on the mechanism and the published daily-resolution India VIX record — our own daily test (CW-D1) waits on the VIX vault, and the monograph says exactly that instead of borrowing a pass it didn't print.

The honest asymmetry
One pre-named month passing at p = 0.020 is evidence. The best of twelve months passing at p = 0.020 would be noise.

Identical numbers, opposite meanings — the entire difference is WHEN the month was chosen. That is what pre-registration is for.

PART 4

Pass, then refuse — again

CW2 joins DL1 and FP1a in the register's strangest quadrant: passes we refuse to promote. The reasons are stated, not vibes: the effect is a small-cap long in the costliest corner of the book (impact plus a 20bp transaction-tax round trip); it delivers one observation per year; and its US ancestor is the canonical DECAYED anomaly — the January effect faded after publication (McLean–Pontiff), and India's version gets that haircut before any sizing talk.

What the pass buys instead: a Tier-C flag and a ledgered paper trade (CW-PT1 — a modeled April small-cap tilt, net of the cost model, graded only after three Aprils). Promotion is a conversation the paper ledger may open in 2029. Not this print.

PART 5

A seat that schedules and cannot point

The L5 module (quant/ladder/calendar_windows.py) is the ladder's only SCHEDULE — not a state estimated from data, but windows built from event calendars: every February, plus election windows around supplied result months (an event list, never an extrapolated clock — nothing is generated beyond announced calendars). Each window carries its trail (start, end, kind, anchor), overlaps count as depth, and consumption is reduce-only vol scheduling by contract.

The design rule is enforced structurally: the module's namespace contains no directional output, and a test asserts it stays that way — test_no_directional_output_exists. The seat can shrink risk into a window; it cannot ever place a bet on one. Rejects close the entry: month-of-year seasonals (CW3's omnibus null, plus the §8 ban) and dividend season (mechanically priced — the ex-date drop approximates the dividend, so there is nothing left to harvest).
