The Debt Mountain

The slowest cycle in the atlas: how governments climb debt mountains in crises and descend them over generations — by growth, by surplus, by default, or by quietly taxing savers through negative real rates. Why 11 of 18 rich countries are still climbing, what that did to investors the last time, and what our book buys as insurance.

PART 1

One equation runs everything

Public debt obeys an accounting identity — not a theory, an identity:

Δ(debt/GDP) = (r − g) × debt/GDP − primary balance + skeletons
r = real interest rate on the debt · g = real growth · primary balance = budget surplus before interest · skeletons = bailouts and off-budget surprises.

Everything in this lesson is about who controls each term and what they do when the mountain gets tall. If r < g, debt melts on its own — the "painless" arc. If r > g, only surpluses or defaults work. And when debt is so large that raising rates to fight inflation would blow up the budget itself, the central bank quietly stops being independent — fiscal dominance (Sargent & Wallace's "unpleasant arithmetic"). The state variable we build is simply: which of these arithmetic regimes are we in? Never: when does it end?

PART 2

The mountains — and how rarely they're descended

DS1 — our census of completed descents
In the fiat era (post-1971), only 4 of 18 advanced economies have ever completed a lasting ≥30-point descent. Eleven of 18 hit their all-time peak in 2020 — the current arc is unresolved almost everywhere.

This is why the atlas calls the entry a state, never a clock, and Tier C: a process completed four times under two different monetary orders supports classification of where we are, and nothing more. Every stronger claim you will ever hear about "the fourth turning of the debt cycle" is narrative wearing a number.

PART 3

Repression — the quiet tax that paid for the last descent

After WWII, debt didn't fall because of austerity heroics. It fell because governments held interest rates below inflation for decades while requiring their own banks, insurers, and pension funds to hold the bonds anyway (Reinhart & Sbrancia called it the "liquidation" of government debt). Read that carefully, because it cuts both ways. Repression halves real equity returns — the seat is not paranoia. But it leaves them positive — so the perma-bear response (sell equities, go full gold-and-bunkers) has historically been the more expensive mistake. This is the rare lesson whose psychology section warns about two opposite operator failures: the frog-boil (normalizing endless negative real rates, holding ever more duration) and the doom cult (the debt chart becomes a religion). The seat's reduce-only clamp is the structural answer to both: it can add insurance, it can never sell the future.

PART 5

India — where the apparatus is standing infrastructure

India is unusual: financial repression here isn't a historical episode, it's plumbing. The SLR — the law requiring banks to hold government bonds — peaked at 38.5% in 1990-91 (with CRR near 15%: over half of every deposit pre-committed to the state) and stands near 18% today: dormant, not dismantled. The 1991 crisis — gold flown to London as collateral — is the founding memory. Post-2003, India ran the growth descent (debt/GDP fell for 15 years without repression), then COVID rebuilt the peak. The L15 inputs for India are therefore concrete: general-government debt level and slope, the repo-minus-CPI persistence gauge, and the captivity composition (who is being made to hold the bonds) — every one measurable from RBI's own free publications, every one specified in the monograph's data chapter.

PART 6

Into the book — insurance, sized to be carried forever

The harvest is deliberately humble: the state conditions the gold floor (40–50% of the gold sleeve's rationale), a slow-debasement tail budget (0.3–0.6% of NAV per year), and a conditional lift when the fiscal-dominance state is ON — all reduce-only against frozen caps, never a timing trade, never an equity exit. The insurance is sized so it can be carried for a decade without fatigue, because DS1's deepest lesson is that these arcs outlast careers. Three designs pre-registered: India's own repression-gauge history, gold's conditional performance in the state (the direct test of the floor's premise), and the annual censoring tracker — the day any major economy completes its descent is the day this seat gets its first fiat-era out-of-sample test.
