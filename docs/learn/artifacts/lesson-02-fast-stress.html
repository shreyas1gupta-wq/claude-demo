PART 1

Volatility, from zero

Volatility is the typical size of a return, ignoring its sign. Take daily returns, square them (killing the sign), average over a window, take the square root — that's realized volatility. Multiply by √252 (trading days in a year) to state it annually: "16% vol" means a typical year wanders about ±16%. One fact makes this number precious: returns themselves are nearly unforecastable, but their SIZE is highly forecastable. The market hides tomorrow's direction and openly advertises tomorrow's temperature.

Clustering — the most robust fact in finance

Why it clusters — mechanism, not mystery

Three engines, all of which say vol is high when the market's machinery is impaired: (1) information arrives in bursts — a crisis is a stream of news, not one headline; (2) forced selling — falls trigger margin calls and redemptions, whose selling causes further falls (the credit-bust amplifier from Lesson 1, running at daily speed); (3) market-makers widen spreads and shrink size when inventory risk rises, so the same order flow moves prices more. And one asymmetry: falls raise future vol more than rallies do (the "leverage effect," French-Schwert-Stambaugh 1987) — which is exactly why a vol spike coincides with the left tail our mandate is bound to cut.

PART 3

Vol targeting — and an honestly contested claim

If size is forecastable, the simplest possible response is: hold less when the forecast is hot. Vol targeting sets your position so the risk stays constant instead of the rupees:

weight_t = min( cap, (target_vol / forecast_vol_t)² )
Position shrinks as squared vol rises (Moreira–Muir's scaling); the cap is our frozen 1.5× gross ceiling. forecast_vol uses only data through yesterday.

Survival argument · why this isn't arbitraged away
De-risking into stress is a risk transformation, not an alpha — and the players big enough to crowd it are structurally forced to do the opposite.

Leverage constraints bind hardest exactly when vol spikes (margin calls force selling at the lows), and daily-liquidity funds must meet redemptions into stress. Our books have no outside redeemers — being able to act on the orange curve of Fig 2 without being forced the other way is one of this desk's few genuine structural edges.

PART 6

The simplicity lane (principal's directive, 2026-09-01)

Your directive: simple, broadly-understood rules — "overbought when price is far above its 50-day average," "gap up over 5%," "volume 3× normal," an economics-backed cycle with no fixed period — deserve consideration, and must not be rejected solely on a t-stat. This is not a concession to the contract; it is aligned with it, for a reason worth learning:

Why simplicity is an anti-overfitting technology
A rule you can say in one sentence has two or three parameters. A rule with two parameters has very few ways to secretly memorize the past.

Every parameter is a dial that could have been tuned to flatter history. The 200-day-average rule and "don't fight a vol spike" have survived decades of being public partly because there is almost nothing in them to overfit. Carver's systematic-trading philosophy and our own two-tier admission (rationale-backed rules face a LOWER evidence bar than statistics-only ones) both encode this. Simplicity is not the opposite of rigor — un-examined complexity is.

Learn one properly: the ATR-extension rule

ATR(14) — Average True Range — is a 1970s-vintage realized-vol measure: the 14-day average of each day's full traded range (high−low, gap-adjusted). It is Fig 4's blue line wearing older clothes. Your rule — "overbought when price sits many ATRs above the 50-DMA" — is then: the price has traveled an extreme multiple of its own typical daily range above its trend anchor. That is a real mechanism: extension that far, that fast, means late-arriving buyers chasing — the crowd most likely to reverse. It is the same object as our overextension/ reversal family, stated in trader's units. The honest treatment: the multiple is a grid ({4, 6, 8, 10}×), never one number; the rule enters as reduce-only (trim entries into extension) first; and India's circuit-limit bands (5/10/20%) interact with your gap thresholds — a 5% gap in a 5%-band stock is a locked circuit, not a signal, so the gap rule must be band-aware. These are exactly the refinements a mechanism survives and a curve-fit doesn't.

The lane's rules (charter: research/register/heuristics-lane.md)

Admission by mechanism

A one-sentence economic story names who is on the wrong side and why they're stuck. Has one ≤2 parameters, both on grids. No mechanism, no card.

Judged by sign, episodes & paper

Sign-consistency across decades and regimes, episode tables, then LIVE paper probation — not t-stat maximization. Statistics inform; they alone don't execute or veto.

Reduce-only first

A heuristic's first job is always subtracting risk (trim, pause, hedge earlier). Adding exposure needs the full funnel.

Rejection needs BOTH failures

A card is rejected only when the mechanism fails scrutiny AND the evidence fails — and the card stays in the register with its reason. Nothing is silently dropped.

And your own decisions: the Stage-2 channel is built for exactly this. Any call you want to make — "this gap-up smells like distribution, trim it" — becomes a timestamped, scored paper trade. Your hit-rate by category is measured; influence follows a published grid; your veto survives any score. You are not asking permission from the statistics — you are keeping score against them, which is how both you and the model learn which of your instincts are edges.
