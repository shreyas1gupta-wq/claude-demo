## PART A — Theory and evidence

### A.1 What an inflation regime *is* — and is not

**Not the same object as inflation.** A CPI print is a monthly number; an inflation *regime* is the
institutional doctrine — the reaction function a central bank, a fiscal authority, and a
wage-setting apparatus are jointly understood to follow — that determines how large a shock has to
be, and for how long it has to persist, before it changes what people expect *next*. IR2's own
result is the cleanest possible statement of the distinction: 81% of country-years stay in the same
inflation state as the year before, pooled across 150 years and 18 countries. Single prints are
noisy; the *state* — high-inflation or low-inflation, credible-anchor or not — is sticky almost to
the point of stubbornness. This is why the design folds the regime into a **persistence** gauge
(trailing 36-month shares, per `partC-data.md` STEP 2) rather than a print-reactive one: IR2's 81%
is the empirical argument for measuring the state, not the observation.

**The credibility literature: regime change as fiscal-monetary regime change.** Thomas Sargent's
*The Ends of Four Big Inflations* (1982, in Robert Hall, ed., *Inflation: Causes and Effects*,
University of Chicago Press for NBER) **[Verified: title, editor, publisher, venue via NBER's own
page and IDEAS/RePEc this session]** studied how Austria, Germany, Hungary, and Poland ended their
early-1920s hyperinflations. His central, and still-contested, claim: what stopped each
hyperinflation was not a gradual tightening of money growth but a **discrete, publicly legible
change of regime** — a new central bank charter, a balanced-budget commitment, often literally a
new currency — that changed the *fiscal* backing of the monetary authority all at once, and
therefore changed expectations discontinuously rather than adaptively. Sargent's paper is the
founding statement of a claim this file leans on without re-deriving: an inflation regime is
jointly a monetary *and* a fiscal commitment, and it changes at **regime speed**, not gradually —
sharply enough that agents update their forecasting model itself, not merely their forecast. The
paper's reception is not uncontested — critics argue the four hyperinflation cases generalize
poorly to the fiat-era, non-hyperinflationary disinflations this monograph actually studies — but
the mechanism claim (fiscal credibility is inseparable from monetary credibility) is the
load-bearing part, not the specific policy prescription. **Grade: Tier C, mechanism/narrative** —
n=4 hyperinflation cases, no attempted numeric fit here or anywhere in this program.

**Volcker as the canonical fiat-era instance.** The 1979–82 US disinflation is this program's own
up-arc-to-down-arc pivot (IR1's own 1982 peak-year marker). The federal funds rate peaked near
**19–20%** in mid-1981; CPI inflation fell from **13.5% (1980) to 3.2% (1983)** — a 10.3-point
collapse in three years — at the cost of the deepest postwar recession to that point, unemployment
peaking at **10.8%** in November–December 1982 **[Verified: rate peak, CPI figures, unemployment
peak, and recession dates via Federal Reserve History and multiple independent summaries this
session]**. Marvin Goodfriend and Robert King's *The Incredible Volcker Disinflation* (*Journal of
Monetary Economics* 52(5), July 2005, pp. 981–1015; also NBER WP 11562) **[Verified: authors,
journal, volume/issue/pages, year via NBER's own page and IDEAS/RePEc this session]** supplies the
credibility mechanics Sargent's earlier paper leaves qualitative: using FOMC transcripts, they show
the real cost of the disinflation traced to **imperfect, not zero, credibility** — long-term
interest rates stayed high and volatile through the early 1980s precisely because markets were not
fully convinced the new regime would hold, forcing the Fed to prove it with sustained tight policy
rather than being believed on announcement alone. Volcker could not simply declare the new regime;
he had to be *tested*, over years, before the market granted it authority — a pattern this file's
Part A.4 finds echoed, on a much smaller scale, in 2022–23. **Grade: Tier B mechanism** (single
episode, but the credibility-testing mechanism recurs across every disinflation this program has
examined, including India's own 2013–16 sequence in A.5 below).

### A.2 Why arcs last decades

Four reinforcing structures explain why an inflation regime, once set, persists for 30–50 years
rather than resetting every business cycle — none alone would produce IR1's 52-year and 38-year
spans; together they do.

**(1) Institutional memory — the generation that remembers.** Ulrike Malmendier and Stefan Nagel,
*Learning from Inflation Experiences* (*Quarterly Journal of Economics* 131(1), 2016, pp. 53–87)
**[Verified: authors, journal, volume/pages, year via Oxford Academic and NBER conference paper
this session]** use over 50 years of Michigan Survey microdata to show individuals overweight
inflation they personally lived through when forming expectations, with an age-dependent updating
rule: the young update hardest, because recent experience is a larger share of their total lived
history. A cohort that came of age during the 1970s carries a durably higher inflation-expectations
prior than one that came of age during the 2010s — which is exactly why a *policy-doctrine* change
(a new central-bank charter, a new institutional consensus) needs a generational turnover, not just
a few quiet years of low prints, to fully embed. This is the direct microfoundation for arcs running
longer than any single business cycle or policy tenure.

**(2) The political economy of disinflation costs.** Every disinflation this file has examined —
Volcker's, India's 2013–16 (A.5) — bought its credibility with a recession or a growth slowdown
paid for by voters and workers, not by the policymakers who benefit from the eventual credibility.
That asymmetry (concentrated near-term pain, diffuse long-run gain) is a standard political-economy
friction against attempting disinflation casually or often; once paid, an incumbent government has
every incentive to defend the resulting low-inflation regime rather than repeat the cost — a second
reinforcing reason regimes, once established, are defended rather than re-litigated each cycle.

**(3) Wage-price institutional structures — indexation's rise and fall.** The 1970s up-arc was
institutionally *reinforced* by formal cost-of-living-adjustment (COLA) wage indexation across major
US union contracts and by Italy's *scala mobile* — mechanisms that mechanically transmitted a price
shock into the next wage round, and the next wage round into the next price round, without requiring
any fresh expectation to form at all. Their post-Volcker/post-1980s dismantling (union COLA coverage
falling sharply through the 1980s; *scala mobile* abolished in stages 1984–92) removed the
mechanical transmission channel itself, not merely the inflation reading — a structural reason the
down-arc, once launched, was self-reinforcing rather than merely expectation-driven.

**(4) The globalization of disinflation, and its contested 2020s reversal.** Kenneth Rogoff's
*Globalization and Global Disinflation* (Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City *Economic Review*
88(Q4), 2003, pp. 45–78, presented at the August 2003 Jackson Hole symposium) **[Verified: journal,
volume/pages, year, venue via the FRB Kansas City paper itself and IDEAS/RePEc this session]**
argues rising trade openness and import competition materially reinforced the 1990s–2000s
disinflation independent of monetary-policy improvement — cheaper tradable-goods competition put a
structural ceiling on domestic pricing power. The contested question for the 2020s is whether this
tailwind has reversed. **Goodhart and Pradhan's** *The Great Demographic Reversal* (Palgrave
Macmillan, 2020) **[Verified: title, publisher, year — already cross-verified in this program's own
`research/dossiers/07-long-waves.md` finding #16, carried forward unchanged]** argue the
low-inflation decades were substantially a one-off effect of a huge, cheap, underemployed global
labor supply (China's internal migration, post-1989 Eastern Europe, rising Western female
labor-force participation) entering the effective world workforce — a shock that cannot repeat and
is now reversing as those cohorts age out and China's working-age population shrinks. Layered onto
this: post-2018 tariff escalation and post-2022 supply-chain "friend-shoring"; a fiscal-dominance
concern `debt-deep` already treats as a live L15 leg (Contract §4/§9's reduce-only Tier-C discipline
applies identically here); and "greenflation" — the energy transition's medium-term investment and
critical-minerals cost pressure, a term the ECB's Isabel Schnabel popularized in March 2022
**[VERIFY: exact coinage attribution and date]**. **Grade: Tier C on the 2020s-reversal thesis as a
whole** — a coherent, multiply-corroborated argument from independent angles, but observed for at
most four years against a 30–50-year arc claim; exactly the "regime-change maximalism" trap
`realrates-deep` G.2 and `gold-deep` G.4 already name for adjacent literatures, and A.4 below scores
honestly rather than resolving it.

### A.3 The measurement literature

**Trend inflation: unobserved-components models.** James Stock and Mark Watson's *Why Has U.S.
Inflation Become Harder to Forecast?* (*Journal of Money, Credit and Banking* 39(s1), 2007, pp.
3–33) introduced the **unobserved-components model with stochastic volatility (UC-SV)** — inflation
decomposed into a slow-moving permanent "trend" and a transitory "gap," with both components'
variances themselves evolving over time **[Verified: paper's existence, UC-SV framing, and JMCB
venue via Princeton's own hosted copy and multiple secondary summaries this session; exact
volume/page numbers — VERIFY]**. Their own multivariate extension (*Core Inflation and Trend
Inflation*, NBER WP 21282; SSRN 2621337) generalizes the same logic across a full price-index
cross-section, treating trend inflation as a dynamic-factor common component **[Verified: existence
and NBER working-paper number via NBER's own page this session; final journal venue — VERIFY]**.
This is the methodological ancestor of IR1's own approach — a rolling 10-year pooled median is a
simpler, non-parametric stand-in for the same idea (separate the persistent state from the noisy
monthly print) — and the reason IR1's note that "single prints don't [persist]" is decades of
literature converging on the same discipline, not this program's own invention. This program
deliberately does **not** replicate Stock-Watson's full UC-SV apparatus: Contract §8's ban on the HP
filter for endpoint-revision reasons applies with equal force to any state-space trend extractor
whose latest-period estimate revises hard as new data arrives — a rolling, backward-looking window
(IR1's own method) is the deliberate, less elegant, more honest substitute.

**Inflation persistence changes across regimes.** A substantial empirical literature finds inflation
persistence — how long a shock to inflation takes to fade — **fell** materially in industrial
economies after they adopted formal inflation targeting, consistent with the theoretical channel
that well-anchored expectations reduce the "intrinsic" (backward-looking, expectations-driven)
component of inflation dynamics **[Verified: general finding and mechanism via multiple independent
academic summaries this session, including ScienceDirect and San Francisco Fed coverage]**. The
finding is genuinely contested, not settled: several rigorous studies find little or no persistence
change once other confounds are controlled, and separating "IT adoption caused the fall" from "IT
adoption coincided with a worldwide disinflation that had other causes too" (Rogoff's own
globalization channel, A.2) is an identification problem the literature has not resolved cleanly
**[Verified: this specific caveat via the same session's search summaries]**. This is directly
relevant to India's own 2016 MPC framework (A.5): the honest claim available to this program is that
formal inflation targeting is *associated with* lower persistence in the cross-country record, not
that India's 2016 framework change is proven to have caused it on its own ten-year sample. **Grade:
Tier B mechanism (cross-country, contested identification), Tier C on any single-country causal
claim including India's.**

**Inflation-expectations anchoring measures, and their availability for India.** The developed-
market toolkit has three legs: household surveys (Michigan, ECB SPF), professional-forecaster
surveys, and market-based breakevens (TIPS-implied, 5-year-5-year-forward). The US 5Y5Y forward
stayed inside a roughly 2.0–2.5% band from 2000 through 2021, spiked to a still-contained **2.67% in
April 2022**, and never crossed 3% through the entire 2021–23 episode — a market-based signal the
regime, not just the print, held **[Verified via Cleveland Fed and multiple independent summaries
this session]**. India has only one of the three legs at meaningful scale: the RBI's own
**Inflation Expectations Survey of Households (IESH)**, running quarterly since **September 2005**
(bi-monthly more recently), surveying ~4,000 households across 12 cities **[Verified: launch date,
cadence, sample size/coverage via RBI's own publication pages this session]**. The market-based leg
is effectively **absent**: government Inflation-Indexed Bonds, launched June 2013 (WPI-linked),
issued monthly only through December 2013, went illiquid as WPI ran negative for 15 straight months
into 2015, and were fully bought back by February 2016 **[Verified: launch date, illiquidity
mechanism, buyback via multiple independent summaries this session]** — no breakeven series exists
for India at usable liquidity today. India's regime-persistence read is therefore **survey-only**,
one full data-leg thinner than the US comparison, a gap to be named whenever an India persistence
claim is made, not assumed away.

### A.4 The 2021–2023 episode as a regime test — scored honestly both ways

This is not a verdict; it is a scorecard, because the episode is genuinely read-both-ways evidence
and this program does not manufacture false resolution (Contract §4's Tier-C narrative discipline
applies with equal force to a live, contested question as to a settled one).

| For "the regime held" | For "the down-arc has ended" |
|---|---|
| **The "transitory" error, corrected inside the regime, not outside it.** The Fed called 2021's inflation surge transitory and held policy near zero through early 2022 even as CPI ran hot — a forecasting failure, not a regime failure; once corrected, the Fed acted **inside** its existing institutional mandate rather than needing a new one **[Verified via multiple independent retrospective summaries this session]**. | **Fiscal arithmetic.** Advanced-economy debt/GDP ratios remain near post-WWII highs; a large, persistent primary deficit is itself a Sargent-style fiscal-regime input to the inflation outlook (`debt-deep`'s own DS1–DS4 already treat this as a live, if Tier-C, L15 leg) — this is a structural argument, not a 2021–23-episode artifact, and it predates and outlasts the "transitory" debate. |
| **The fastest hiking cycle since Volcker, executed without a repeat of 1981's cost.** The Fed raised its policy rate from 0–0.25% to 5.25–5.50% in **17 months** (March 2022–July 2023) — the most aggressive tightening cycle since Volcker's — against CPI that peaked at **9.1% YoY in June 2022 [Verified via multiple independent summaries this session]** — and inflation fell substantially without the 10.8%-unemployment-scale recession Volcker's disinflation required. | **Deglobalization and the energy transition, independently argued (A.2).** Both predate 2021 and both are structural-supply-side, not demand-cyclical — exactly the kind of shock that changes the *regime*, per Sargent's own framing, rather than a print that reverts. |
| **Expectations stayed broadly anchored.** The US 5Y5Y breakeven never crossed 3% even at the episode's peak (A.3); this is the market-based instantiation of IR2's own 81% stickiness finding — a genuine regime-test the anchor passed, in real time, under real stress. | **The credibility-testing mechanism itself (Goodfriend-King, A.1) says a regime is proven only by being tested repeatedly over years, not by surviving one episode.** One successful disinflation without a recession is a single, favorable data point against a Volcker-scale demonstration, not yet its equal. |

**Honest read.** IR2's 81% stickiness argues for the "regime held" column as the base rate, and this
episode's specific evidence (anchored 5Y5Y, no 1981-scale recession) is consistent with that base
rate rather than contradicting it. But n=1 additional test-year against a 30–50-year arc claim is
not enough to retire the "ended" column's structural arguments, several of which (fiscal arithmetic,
demographic reversal) are independently well-evidenced on their own terms and simply have not yet
had enough time to resolve either way — precisely the "regime-change maximalism" pattern this
program's own `realrates-deep` and `gold-deep` monographs warn against extrapolating from a loud,
recent episode. **Grade: Tier C either direction; the honest position is "held so far, under real
stress, with structural pressure still building" — never a verdict.**

### A.5 India — the domestic regime history (double weight)

**Five regimes, mechanically distinct, in roughly 70 years.**

1. **1950s–60s: food/monsoon-driven, pre-institutional.** Inflation in early independent India was
   dominated by monsoon-driven agricultural supply shocks feeding directly into a food-heavy CPI
   basket — there was no independent central-bank inflation mandate to speak of; price behavior
   tracked the harvest, not doctrine.
2. **1970s: oil shock plus deficit financing.** India's **highest recorded annual CPI inflation,
   28.6% in 1974**, followed the first OPEC oil embargo, a severe domestic drought, the fiscal
   aftermath of the 1971 Bangladesh war, and a massive expansion in fiscal deficit financing; CPI
   inflation exceeded 20% in both 1973–74 (20.2%) and 1974–75 (25.2%) **[Verified: figures via
   multiple independent summaries this session]**. Average GDP growth ran near 3.2% against average
   broad-money (M3) growth near 17.7% across the decade — a textbook deficit-monetization signature,
   the Indian instance of IR1's global up-arc.
3. **1980s–2013: chronic high-single-digit inflation — "the inflation tax" era.** A self-reinforcing
   cycle of deficit-induced inflation feeding inflation-induced deficits characterized the 1980s;
   double-digit inflation recurred around the 1990–91 balance-of-payments crisis (aggravated by the
   Iraq-Kuwait oil-price spike, with reserves covering barely three weeks of imports)
   **[Verified: BoP-crisis mechanics via multiple independent summaries this session]**, and chronic
   high-single-digit CPI persisted with no formal nominal anchor for two more decades — functioning,
   this program's own dossiers already flag (D07/D08), as a de facto tax on cash and fixed-income
   savers, a full generation before India's regime change arrived.
4. **2013–16: the disinflation, engineered.** Facing near-double-digit CPI in 2012–13, RBI Deputy
   Governor Urjit Patel's Expert Committee (report **January 2014**) proposed a phased disinflation
   glide path — CPI to **8% within 12 months (by January 2015)** and **6% within 24 months (by
   January 2016)** — ahead of adopting a steady-state **4% ± 2%** target **[Verified: committee
   report timing, glide-path targets via multiple independent summaries this session]**. Then-
   Governor Raghuram Rajan endorsed the path publicly. The disinflation, in fact, **undershot** the
   glide path — CPI fell to 6.8% by June 2014 and 5.6% by September 2014, faster than the committee
   itself expected **[Verified via Business Standard's own contemporaneous coverage this session]**
   — a credibility-building over-delivery structurally similar in kind (if far smaller in cost) to
   Goodfriend-King's Volcker mechanism: a new doctrine proving itself by outperforming its own
   announced path, not merely meeting it.
5. **2016–present: the Monetary Policy Committee / flexible inflation-targeting framework — India's
   own regime change.** The RBI Act was amended in 2016, the Monetary Policy Framework Agreement
   (February 2015) given statutory force, and the six-member MPC held its first meeting **October
   2016**, formally adopting **CPI at 4% ± 2%** as the mandate **[Verified: sequence via this
   program's own `research/dossiers/08-india-mid-cycles.md` I1 and cross-checked independently this
   session]**. This is India's Sargent-style regime change in miniature: a discrete, legislated,
   publicly legible institutional commitment — not a gradual drift toward lower prints.

**Performance, 2016–2026, including both breaches.** Average CPI inflation fell from **6.8% in the
four years before the framework (2012–16) to 4.9% since adoption [Verified via Business Standard's
own retrospective coverage this session]** — directionally consistent with the cross-country IT-
persistence literature (A.3), with the same identification caveat (concurrent global disinflation
forces were also at work). Two formal breaches occurred:

- **2020**: CPI ran above the 6% upper tolerance for **four consecutive quarters** from Q1 2020 —
  mechanically more than the three-quarter statutory trigger — but the government and RBI jointly
  excluded the April–June 2020 reading from the count on the grounds that MOSPI had estimated that
  quarter's data by **imputation**, not direct price collection, under COVID lockdown, terming the
  underlying print "artificial"; no formal failure report was filed **[Verified: quarterly readings,
  imputation rationale, and non-filing via Deccan Herald's own contemporaneous coverage and multiple
  independent summaries this session]**. Read charitably, this is a genuine measurement-integrity
  exception; read skeptically, it is the first instance of the framework's own escape valve being
  used at the first real test — a fact this file states without adjudicating, since Contract §4
  forbids resolving genuinely contested narrative claims into false certainty.
- **2022**: CPI averaged above 6% for three straight quarters (6.3% Jan–Mar, 7.3% Apr–Jun, 7.0%
  Jul–Sep 2022), triggering the statutory failure clause for the **first time since the framework's
  2016 inception**; RBI filed a confidential report to the government (reasons, remedial action,
  time-to-restore estimate) that the government declined to make public, citing statutory
  confidentiality **[Verified: quarterly figures, first-time-triggered status, confidentiality
  decision via BusinessToday and multiple independent summaries this session]**.

**The March 2026 framework review — resolved, during this monograph's own writing window.** The
framework's second five-year review tested four live questions via an RBI discussion paper: whether
the 4% point target should continue; whether it should become a range; whether the ±2% bands should
be adjusted or scrapped; and whether policy should target headline or core CPI **[Verified via
Policy Circle's own coverage this session]**. The Government of India **retained the framework
unchanged** — 4% target, 2–6% band — for a further five years, **1 April 2026 to 31 March 2031**,
per gazette notification dated **25 March 2026** **[Verified: outcome and gazette date via Policy
Circle and Business Standard's own coverage this session]**. Former Governor Shaktikanta Das had
publicly argued against excluding food from the target index, on the grounds that its outsized
basket weight makes it inseparable from lived inflation experience for Indian households
**[Verified via Policy Circle's own coverage this session]** — a position the retained-headline
outcome vindicates. The regime this file documents is not merely historical: it was freshly
re-legislated six months before this monograph was written.

**Monsoon/food-weight structure — and a live rebase that changes the regime reading.** Under the
outgoing CPI base (2012=100), the **food and beverages group carried a 45.86% combined weight**
**[Verified via MOSPI's own published weight table and multiple independent summaries this
session]** — nearly half the index — meaning a bad monsoon, an onion or tomato spike, or a
pulses-supply shock could move headline CPI materially with no demand-side or monetary content at
all. This is precisely why the atlas (line 273, 307) routes food-CPI spikes to a separate,
ENSO-conditioned channel (**H55**) rather than regime information — the kind of noise IR2's own
81%-stickiness logic says to look past. **This structure just changed, materially, mid-design.**
MOSPI released India's first CPI reading on the new **2024=100 base** in February 2026 (part of the
concurrent multi-series 2026 rebase `docs/MASTER_PLAN.md` R0.1b already flags — GDP/GFCF, CPI, IIP
and WPI all rebasing within months of each other), built on the 2023–24 Household Consumption
Expenditure Survey and COICOP 2018; the food and beverages weight **fell to 36.75%** — a roughly
9-point reduction **[Verified: new base year, weight figure, HCES/COICOP methodology via MOSPI's own
press materials and multiple independent summaries this session]**. Design implication, with the
same discipline `docs/MASTER_PLAN.md` applies to every other 2026 rebase: this is a **level break in
the regime-reading input itself**, never fit through — a lower food weight mechanically dampens
headline CPI's monsoon-sensitivity relative to the 2016–2026 track record above, so any future
persistence gauge spanning the February 2026 boundary needs the same splice discipline already
applied to the gold-duty and GDP-base breaks.

**The WPI–CPI wedge — H61's tie-in.** This program's atlas already opens a distinct, separately-
tracked hypothesis (**H61**, `docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` line 319, `docs/masterplan/C-hypothesis-
register.md` line 228) on the wedge between WPI (input/producer prices, Office of Economic Adviser,
DPIIT) and CPI (output/consumer prices, MOSPI) as a margin-cycle lead indicator for cyclical-versus-
defensive sector tilting — explicitly **not** a regime-arc signal, and this file does not
re-purpose it as one. The relevant tie-in here is narrower and purely structural: WPI itself is
scheduled for discontinuation after roughly five years of parallel running on its own new 2022–23
base (per `docs/masterplan/A-data-catalog.md` H2), and both WPI and CPI are rebasing within months
of each other in 2026 — meaning H61's own wedge series inherits the identical splice-discipline
requirement this file's food-weight discussion above already states for CPI alone. The two
hypotheses (H61's margin-cycle read, this file's regime-arc context) share a data-hygiene problem,
not a mechanism; they are not to be merged.

## PART B — What n≈2 arcs permit: the affirmative case for no new seat

### B.1 The information already flows through two existing seats

`docs/DESIGN.md` L15 (line 248) already lists "persistence of negative real rates" as one of its
three Tier-C, no-numeric-authority inputs, built (per `partC-data.md` STEP 2) as the rolling
**trailing-36-month share** of repo-minus-CPI readings that are negative — functionally the same
object IR2 measures at 150-year, 18-country scale: a persistence gauge over prints, not a print
itself. IR2's 81% stickiness is the direct empirical justification: a 36-month window is the right
instrument for a process the JST panel shows changes state only ~19% of the time year-to-year.
Layered onto this, L6 (`docs/DESIGN.md` line 239) already carries the faster-moving
monetary-policy-stance seat (repo path, lagged ~12–24 months, Tier B, n≈4–5 marginal per
`08-india-mid-cycles.md` I1) inside the shared macro-credit block budget (§4.2, ≤20%) — where a
genuine cyclical policy read already lives, at a timescale markets can act on. Between L15's slow
gauge and L6's fast one, the information this file documents has **two existing, budgeted homes**; a
third, arc-timed seat would not add information, it would double-count the same state at a third
latency.

### B.2 Three named rejected uses

**(1) Arc-timing.** No rule may condition a position on "where we are" in a 30–50-year arc. IR1's
own n=2 completed arcs fail the clock test by an order of magnitude (Contract §4 requires ≥4); there
is no defensible way to estimate a turning-point probability from two observations, any more than
GC2's honest null (`gold-deep` A.2) permits timing gold's century anchor from a near-unit-root
series. This forecloses any "the down-arc is X% through its historical length, therefore..." rule
outright.

**(2) A "new 1970s" allocation overhaul.** A.4's own scorecard shows the "regime has ended" case is
genuinely argued but genuinely unresolved after one post-episode test-year; converting that
uncertainty into a wholesale portfolio-mix change — rotating toward the explicit 1970s playbook
(commodities, short duration, structurally higher gold) before the regime question is anywhere close
to resolved — is precisely the doom-allocation pattern `gold-deep` B.4(3) and G.1 already name and
foreclose for the debt-supercycle case; this file names the same failure mode for its own literature
rather than assuming immunity because the mechanism differs.

**(3) Trading inflation prints.** `gold-deep` A.2/A.3 already establishes, from an entirely separate
literature (Erb-Harvey), that gold is an unreliable inflation hedge at every horizon 1–20 years —
the corroborating finding from this file's own side is IR2's 81% stickiness plus A.4's honest read:
individual prints are noisy realizations of a sticky state, and a signal that reacts to the print
rather than the state is trading noise, not regime information. A CPI-surprise trading overlay would
also duplicate `sleeves.yaml`'s existing `real_rate` gold input through a noisier proxy — the exact
double-counting `gold-deep` B.4(2) already forecloses for gold specifically; this file forecloses it
for any equity or duration sleeve on the identical logic.

### B.3 The one legitimate watch

Given IR1–IR3 and the affirmative case above, exactly one condition deserves standing attention, and
it is not new — it is the escalation condition L15 already carries (§4.3): a **persistent breach of
India's own inflation band** (per A.5, the 2–6% corridor just re-confirmed through March 2031)
**occurring simultaneously with the fiscal-dominance state being ON** (`debt-deep`'s own definition:
high public-debt percentile AND sustained negative real rates — the same conjunction DS4's pooled
+4.5%-vs-+9.5% equity finding and L15's conditional gold-floor lift already key on). Either leg alone
is already priced somewhere in the existing machinery — a band breach alone is L6's business; fiscal
dominance alone is L15's business. The **conjunction** — a sovereign simultaneously failing its own
inflation mandate *and* showing the debt-and-real-rate signature of an incentive to inflate the debt
away — is the one pattern this literature (Sargent's own fiscal-monetary-regime framing, A.1) says is
qualitatively different from either leg alone: the closest India-specific analogue to an actual
regime-ending event, rather than a cyclical breach subsequently corrected (2020 and 2022 both were,
A.5). This carries no new numeric authority — it is a named condition for the annual review (G.4
below) to check for explicitly, the same register-level discipline `debt-deep`'s DS1–DS4 and
`reserve-deep`'s falsification/escalation conditions already apply to their adjacent inputs.

## PART G — Regime psychology: the generational forgetting cycle

### G.1 Stability breeds the doctrine drift that ends it

A.2's institutional-memory argument (Malmendier-Nagel) has a mirror-image failure mode for the
operators of *this* program, not just for households or central bankers. A desk built and staffed
during a low-inflation decade internalizes that world as the default backdrop, the same learning
mechanism running on an institution rather than an individual. Sustained regime stability is the
precondition for exactly the doctrine drift that eventually ends the regime: funding assumptions
calibrated on a "lower for longer" world (`realrates-deep` G.1 names this specific trap for the
rates literature), and — most relevant here — a design team that has never operationally lived
through a credibility test coming to treat the MPC's 4% ± 2% anchor as a law of nature rather than a
21st-year-old commitment that has already required one confidential failure report (A.5, 2022) to
defend. This is monetary doctrine's own instance of the failure-mode family Minsky named for
financial stability — a long stable run is not evidence the mechanism has stopped operating, it is
the precondition under which the next test arrives with the least institutional readiness to
recognize it.

### G.2 The 2021 "transitory" groupthink as the operator lesson

A.4's scorecard states the episode neutrally; the operator lesson is not. The Fed — the world's most
resourced, most academically supported monetary authority — held a forecasting error (inflation is
transitory) for the better part of a year while the evidence against it accumulated, not because the
data was ambiguous in real time but because the prior was institutionally load-bearing: reversing it
meant admitting the post-2008 low-inflation consensus might not generalize to a genuinely new shock.
The operational countermeasure this program already has in place for an analogous failure mode —
`gold-deep` G.1's reduce-only Tier-C ceiling, enforced in the registry validator rather than left to
discretion — generalizes here: no design component may treat "the regime has held for a decade" as
license to widen a persistence gauge's weight or shorten its lookback on the strength of recent,
comfortable prints. The gauge's parameters, once frozen (Contract §4), should require exactly the
friction to revisit that the Fed's own "transitory" call needed and initially lacked.

### G.3 India's food-price political reflexes

A.5 already establishes the mechanism: a food weight that was 45.86% of the pre-2026 CPI basket
(now 36.75%, still the largest single group by far) makes headline inflation intensely sensitive to
a handful of politically visible commodities — onions and tomatoes above all others in the modern
record. The **2023–24 onion episode** is a clean, recent instance: a 40% export duty (August 2023),
then a minimum export price of $800/tonne (Oct–Dec 2023), then a full export ban through **31 March
2024** as domestic output fell 15.6% on weak regional rainfall — buffer stock tripled to 3 lakh
tonnes and sold at a subsidized ₹25/kg — and the ban **lifted in May 2024, timed to the general
election**, specifically to keep prices politically quiet during the vote **[Verified: duty/MEP/ban
sequence, buffer-stock figures, output decline, and election-timed lifting via Business Standard,
National Herald and Deccan Herald's own contemporaneous coverage this session]**. This is the Indian
mirror of `gold-deep` G.3's "familiarity, not fear" hazard: an onion or tomato spike reads as an
urgent, politically unmissable event precisely because it is one, and the reflex is to treat it as
regime-relevant. IR2's own 81% stickiness finding, and H55's routing of food-CPI spikes away from
the regime channel entirely (A.5), is the explicit rebuttal: a monsoon-driven vegetable spike is the
clearest example of a print carrying near-zero regime information, however loud the domestic
political reaction to it becomes.

### G.4 Desk countermeasures

| Failure mode | Mechanism | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Doctrine drift during a stable run | Institutional learning over-weights the recent stable regime as permanent (Malmendier-Nagel's mechanism, applied to a desk rather than a household) | Persistence gauges (L15 Step 2, trailing 36m) with parameters frozen at inception per Contract §4; no re-weighting on the strength of recent comfortable prints |
| "Transitory"-style groupthink under an emerging shock | A load-bearing institutional prior resists revision even as evidence accumulates against it | Reduce-only Tier-C ceiling on any inflation-regime-derived allocation change, enforced in the registry validator (mirrors `gold-deep` G.1's anti-capitulation lock) — an *anti-capitulation lock*, not operator discretion |
| India's food-price political reflex | A visible, politically charged food-CPI spike reads as regime information because it is loud, not because IR2 says it is persistent | H55 routes food-CPI spikes to their own ENSO-conditioned channel, explicitly separated from the regime-arc read this file documents |
| Arc-timing temptation as IR1's numbers age | Two dated arc-length numbers (52y, 38y) invite a "the down-arc is now X years old, therefore..." inference the n=2 clock-test failure forecloses | **Annual re-dating of IR1**: the up-arc/down-arc/stickiness markers are re-run each design review on the latest JST vintage, dated exactly as computed, never extrapolated between reviews |

The pattern across every row: the operator hazard here is not that the desk will panic — it is that
a genuinely well-evidenced, slow-moving regime literature is exactly the kind of material that
invites quiet, well-reasoned overreach, one incremental step at a time, from people who have read
the same papers this file cites. The countermeasures are structural (frozen parameters, a registry-
enforced reduce-only ceiling, an explicit non-regime routing for the loudest domestic input) rather
than a reminder to "stay disciplined," for the same reason every other Part-G countermeasure in this
program's sibling monographs is structural: discipline that depends on remembering the argument in
the moment is exactly what a multi-decade regime is long enough to erode.
