## A. Political budget cycles, strongest form

### A.1 Nordhaus (1975): the opportunistic cycle, and why it needed correcting

**Nordhaus, William D. (1975), "The Political Business Cycle,"** *The Review of Economic Studies*
42(2): 169–190 [Verified via search]. Nordhaus's founding move was to treat the elected government
itself as a rational, plurality-maximizing actor operating a real macroeconomic policy lever — not
a passive victim of the business cycle but its author, on a timetable set by the election calendar
rather than by the economy's own dynamics. The mechanism as Nordhaus specified it: policymakers
exploit a short-run Phillips-curve trade-off, contracting demand early in an administration
(unemployment rising, inflation falling — the unpopular medicine, taken while the next election is
safely distant) and expanding it in the run-up to the vote (unemployment falling, GDP rising,
inflation only showing up after the ballots are counted) [Verified via search]. This is the
**opportunistic** model in its purest form: every incumbent, in every election, behaves the same
way, because voters are assumed to evaluate the government retrospectively and myopically on
recent economic performance rather than on its whole term or its policy platform.

The model's own weakness, identified within a decade of its publication and stated here because it
is the weakness the next generation of the literature exists to fix, is exactly the one this
desk's entire evidence-tier discipline (Contract §4) is built to catch: **a model that requires
every voter, in every election, to be fooled by the same trick in the same way is not a stable
equilibrium against learning.** If voters update even slowly on the pattern — inflation always
follows the election, every time — the pre-election boom stops working as a signal of anything,
and a rational incumbent should stop paying for it. Nordhaus's own opportunistic-cycle model has
no mechanism for that learning; it needed one, and the mechanism the field supplied is Part A.2's
subject. The steelman is stated first because Nordhaus's empirical instinct — elections are
followed by systematic macro patterns, not random noise — turned out to be broadly right even
where his specific myopic-voter mechanism needed replacing; the correction is about *why* the
pattern exists and *when* it survives, not about whether it exists at all.

### A.2 Rogoff–Sibert (1988) and Rogoff (1990): rational voters, asymmetric information, and
### composition over totals

**Rogoff, Kenneth & Sibert, Anne (1988), "Elections and Macroeconomic Policy Cycles,"**
*The Review of Economic Studies* 55(1): 1–16 [Verified via search]. This paper's contribution is
precisely the fix Nordhaus's model needed: it shows that an opportunistic-looking pre-election
cycle can survive **fully rational voters**, provided there is a genuine, temporary information
asymmetry between the incumbent and the electorate — specifically, that the incumbent has private
information about their own "competence" (their ability to deliver public services at low fiscal
cost) that voters cannot yet observe directly [Verified via search]. Incumbents signal competence
by running the economy hot before an election — not to fool voters about the economy's true state,
but because a genuinely competent incumbent *can* afford to do so at lower long-run cost than an
incompetent one, making the pre-election signal partially informative even though every type of
incumbent has an incentive to send it. The paper's sharpest empirical implication, easy to lose in
summary: **incumbents cheat least when their private information is either extremely favorable or
extremely unfavorable** — the signaling temptation is strongest in the middle of the competence
distribution, where a marginal boost to perceived competence changes the election outcome
[Verified via search]. This already reframes the cycle from "always happens" (Nordhaus) to
"happens conditionally, and its strength depends on the information environment" — the first move
toward the cross-country heterogeneity Part A.3 makes the center of the case.

**Rogoff, Kenneth (1990), "Equilibrium Political Budget Cycles,"** *American Economic Review*
80(1): 21–36 [Verified via search], sharpens the signaling logic specifically onto the *fiscal*
policy the government controls (taxes, spending, and — crucially — spending *composition*) rather
than onto the monetary/inflation levers Nordhaus's original model emphasized. Rogoff's central,
quotable result: prior to elections, **taxes tend to be suboptimally low and government
consumption spending suboptimally high, with the pre-election consumption binge financed at the
expense of government investment** [Verified via search]. This is the theoretical move that
matters most for this chapter, and it is worth stating why in the model's own terms rather than
asserting it: voters can observe **visible** current consumption spending (transfers, subsidies,
tax cuts, roads that get inaugurated) far more easily and far faster than they can observe
**investment** spending whose payoff arrives after the election, or whose quality is hard to judge
even after the fact. A competent-signaling incumbent, wanting maximum electoral credit per rupee
spent before voters go to the polls, rationally shifts the *composition* of a given fiscal envelope
toward the visible, near-term-payoff categories and away from investment — not necessarily toward
a larger deficit at all. This is the theoretical seed of the empirical finding (Khemani, Drazen–
Eslava, §A.4–A.5 below) that keeps recurring in the India-specific evidence: **the signature of a
political budget cycle in a data-rich, institutionally mature democracy is compositional, not a
blown-out deficit total** — a distinction this chapter's entire India record (Part B) is organized
around, because it is also the distinction that keeps a 2.7 seat from double-counting what L5 and
L6 already carry (Part C).

### A.3 Shi–Svensson (2006): the finding that matters most for India

**Shi, Min & Svensson, Jakob (2006), "Political budget cycles: Do they differ across countries and
why?"** *Journal of Public Economics* 90: 1367–1389 [Verified via search]. This is the paper this
chapter treats as load-bearing, because it is the one result in the whole literature that speaks
directly to which *kind* of democracy India is, evidentially, for this purpose. Working a large
cross-country panel, Shi–Svensson find that **government fiscal deficits rise by almost 1% of GDP
in election years on average** [Verified via search] — a real, non-trivial, average effect. But
the paper's headline is the heterogeneity, not the average: **political budget cycles are
significantly larger and statistically far more robust in developing democracies than in
industrialized ones**, where the effect is small or statistically indistinguishable from zero
[Verified via search]. Shi–Svensson's own explanation is a **moral-hazard model of electoral
competition**: in a young or weakly institutionalized democracy, voters have less information
about incumbent competence, weaker media and civil-society monitoring, and a shorter track record
of past governments to calibrate expectations against — precisely the conditions under which
Rogoff–Sibert's signaling mechanism has room to operate, because the information asymmetry the
model requires is *structurally larger and more persistent* in a young democracy than in an old
one. This is the single most consequential import for a book built on Indian markets: **the
theory's own strongest cross-country evidence says the mechanism should be more visible in India
than in the US/UK samples most of the founding papers were tested on** — which is exactly why this
chapter does not simply import a "PBCs are a US-tested small effect, ignore them" verdict from the
developed-market literature, and instead goes to India's own record in Part B before reaching a
verdict.

### A.4 Drazen–Eslava (2010): composition cycles, empirically, at the ballot box

**Drazen, Allan & Eslava, Marcela, "Electoral Manipulation via Voter-Friendly Spending: Theory and
Evidence,"** *Journal of Development Economics* 92(1): 39–52 (2010); circulated earlier as NBER
Working Paper 11085, "Electoral Manipulation via Expenditure Composition: Theory and Evidence"
(2005) [Verified via search]. This paper is Rogoff's (1990) composition-shift theory turned into a
direct, disaggregated empirical test — the closest thing in the literature to a controlled
demonstration of the mechanism, because it works at the *category* level of government spending
rather than at the deficit-total level almost all earlier PBC studies were confined to by data
availability. Testing Colombian municipal elections, Drazen–Eslava find incumbents **cutting**
spending on interest payments, transfers to retirees, and temporary-worker contracts, while
**significantly expanding** spending on highly visible categories — road construction and water
infrastructure — in the run-up to an election [Verified via search]. The paper's own framing is
precise and worth quoting in substance: voters and politicians are modeled as having genuine
preferences over *which kinds* of spending they value, and incumbents manipulate the electorate not
by inflating the total (which a fiscally literate electorate might punish) but by **reallocating
toward categories with high visibility** [Verified via search] — the identical logic Rogoff's
theory predicted two decades earlier, now demonstrated in disaggregated administrative data. The
methodological lesson this chapter carries forward into Part B: **a fiscal political-cycle study
that only looks at the deficit total is looking in the wrong place**; the place to look is the
expenditure *composition* line, exactly where India's own PM-KISAN and state farm-loan-waiver
record (Part B.2, episode 5) turns out to sit.

### A.5 Brender–Drazen (2005): new democracies, and the alpha-decay mechanism inside political
### economy itself

**Brender, Adi & Drazen, Allan (2005), "Political budget cycles in new versus established
democracies,"** *Journal of Monetary Economics* 52(7): 1271–1295 [Verified; note the journal —
several secondary sources misattribute this to the *American Economic Review*, a citation error
this chapter does not repeat]. Working a large cross-section of countries and election years,
Brender–Drazen find a political deficit cycle in the pooled sample overall — but the effect is
**driven entirely by "new democracies"**: restricting the sample to established democracies, the
political budget cycle **disappears** [Verified via search]. This is, on its own terms, the single
most important result in the whole literature for a desk operating under Contract §5's governing
principle ("assume your alpha decays"), because it supplies a *mechanism-internal* decay argument
rather than an arbitrage-capital one: **voters in new democracies have not yet had enough election
cycles to learn that the pre-election boom is a manipulation rather than a genuine improvement**,
and once they accumulate enough experience of the pattern (more elections observed, better
information institutions, a longer track record to calibrate against), they punish it rather than
reward it, and the cycle vanishes. This is McLean–Pontiff's "anomalies decay as they become known"
(Contract §5) running inside the electorate itself rather than inside an arbitrageur's capital
base — the same epistemic structure, a different population doing the learning. For this desk the
consequence is double-edged and must be stated as such: **India, on Brender–Drazen's own new-vs-
established axis, sits ambiguously** — a democracy continuously operating since 1947 (one of the
oldest outside the West), but one whose fiscal-transparency institutions (a Comptroller and
Auditor General flagging off-budget financing only from 2019 onward, Part B.1) and whose most
visible fiscal-signaling instruments (direct cash transfers via biometric-linked bank accounts)
are recent enough that the "have voters learned" question cannot be answered by citing Brender–
Drazen's own country classification off the shelf — it would need its own India-specific test,
which this chapter does not have the data to run and does not claim to have run (see Part B.3's
honest limits).

### A.6 The India-specific evidence, before the machinery chapter

**Khemani, Stuti (2004), "Political cycles in a developing economy: Effect of elections in the
Indian states,"** *Journal of Development Economics* 73(1): 125–154 [Verified via search]. Khemani
studied state-legislative-assembly elections across 14 major Indian states from 1960 to 1994,
using the distinction between constitutionally scheduled elections and midterm polls as an
identification strategy (midterm elections are plausibly less anticipated, giving a cleaner test
of policy manipulation around a *known* election date) [Verified via search]. The result set is
exactly the composition-not-totals pattern Rogoff (1990) and Drazen–Eslava (2010) predict, found
independently in Indian data a full six years before Drazen–Eslava's Colombian study and via a
different empirical route: **election years show a negative effect on some commodity taxes, a
positive effect on investment (development) spending, and — this is the finding this chapter's
title promises and delivers — no significant effect on the overall fiscal deficit**, because
Khemani finds the composition shift financed largely by a *reduction* in other consumption
spending rather than by more borrowing [Verified via search]. Separately, and this is the sharpest
single number in Khemani's paper for a book that will eventually need to read India's own
infrastructure-spending calendar (cross-ref L11, `docs/cycles/16-capex-cycle.md`): **elections have
a large, positive effect on road construction specifically**, with public-works spending far more
electorally responsive than the aggregate fiscal numbers would suggest on their own [Verified via
search]. Read against Part A.2–A.4, Khemani's India-specific result is the clean confirmation this
chapter needed before turning to the machinery chapter: **India's own state-election evidence says
the PBC signature here is compositional reallocation toward visible spending categories (roads,
targeted subsidies), not a deficit-total blowout** — precisely the framing that determines where
this entry can and cannot claim a budget line in Part C.

**Farm-loan-waiver literature and cash-transfer waves — the modern, national-election-adjacent
form of the same composition mechanism.** The Khemani-era evidence is confined to 1960–1994 state
data; India's most visible modern political-budget instrument — the farm loan waiver, and its
2016–2024 successor, the direct cash transfer — did not exist in that sample and is treated in
full, with verified figures, as its own episode in Part B.2 (episode 5) and Part B.2's closing
discussion of the 2023–24 women's cash-transfer wave, because both are best read against India's
actual fiscal calendar rather than abstracted into a stand-alone theory section. The short version,
stated here to close Part A's arc: the mechanism Rogoff (1990) modeled theoretically in 1990 and
Drazen–Eslava (2010) demonstrated in Colombian municipal data is visible, at national scale, with
verifiable rupee figures and election dates, in India's own 2017–2024 record — a stronger, more
direct confirmation than either the Khemani state panel or the cross-country Shi–Svensson result
could offer on their own, because it can be checked scheme-by-scheme against the actual electoral
calendar rather than inferred from aggregate panel coefficients.

## B. India's fiscal machinery and record

### B.1 The machinery

**The budget calendar and the Feb 1 move.** The Union Budget was presented on the last working day
of February for decades; starting with the **2017–18 Budget, presented on 1 February 2017** by
Finance Minister Arun Jaitley, the date moved to **1 February** and has stayed there since
[Verified via search]. Jaitley's own stated rationale was implementation lead time: a budget
presented in late February left barely a month before the new fiscal year began on 1 April,
whereas a 1 February budget gives roughly two months for parliamentary passage and departmental
rollout before the fiscal year starts [Verified via search]. The date move matters for this
entry's L5 seat directly: it *fixes*, by calendar law, the volatility window every year regardless
of which government is in power — exactly the "timing is law, direction is surprise" framing
`config/ladder.yaml`'s L5 entry and atlas row 4.1 already carry, and exactly why L5 schedules a
volatility window off `indiabudget.gov.in`'s own calendar rather than off any forecast of what the
budget will contain.

**Interim budgets and the vote-on-account convention.** In a general-election year, the outgoing
government does not present a full budget with new taxation and expenditure policy; it presents an
**interim budget (a vote-on-account)** covering only the expenditure needed to run the government
until a new administration can present its own full budget after the election. This pattern
recurred at each of the three most recent general elections: **February 2014 interim → July 2014
full budget** (post-election, first NDA-III budget); **1 February 2019 interim → July 2019 full
budget** (post-election, second Modi-government budget) — the interim budget that, per Part B.2
episode 5 below, is where PM-KISAN was actually announced; and **1 February 2024 interim → July
2024 full budget** (post-election, third Modi-government budget). This convention is the
institutional reason this entry's calendar seat (L5) treats the *result* month, not the *budget*
month, as the volatility event of consequence in an election year — an interim budget is
constitutionally constrained in exactly the way that limits how large an opportunistic fiscal
gesture it can legally contain, which is itself part of why the PBC literature's own compositional
framing (Part A.2, A.4) rather than a deficit-total framing fits India's institutional design.

**The FRBM Act (2003) and its escape clauses.** The **Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management
Act**, passed by Parliament in August 2003, set the original targets: fiscal deficit ≤3% of GDP and
the elimination of the revenue deficit, both by **2007–08** [Verified via search]; a July 2004
amendment shifted the terminal date to **2008–09**, with mandated annual reductions of at least 0.3
percentage points of GDP on the fiscal deficit and 0.5 points on the revenue deficit [Verified via
search]. The Act's original text contained **no formal escape clause at all**; one was introduced
only via the **Finance Act, 2018**, following the recommendations of the **N.K. Singh FRBM Review
Committee**, which fixed a revised fiscal-deficit target of 3% of GDP and a general-government debt
anchor of 60% of GDP (40% for the Centre alone), while permitting the government to deviate from
the annual fiscal-deficit target by **up to 0.5 percentage points of GDP** under defined trigger
conditions — national security or war, a calamity of national proportion, far-reaching structural
reforms with unanticipated fiscal implications, or a decline in real output growth of at least 3
percentage points below the average of the preceding four quarters [Verified via search]. The
escape clause has been invoked repeatedly since 2018 — most consequentially during the FY20–FY21
COVID episode (Part B.2, episode 6) — which is the honest characterization this subsection commits
to: **India's fiscal rule is a rule with a standing, legally codified exit, not an unconditional
constraint**, a fact any fiscal-cycle read has to carry rather than treat the 3%/60% numbers as
binding ceilings.

**CGA monthly accounts — the free high-frequency window, cross-referenced not duplicated.** The
**Controller General of Accounts (CGA)**, Department of Expenditure, publishes the Union
government's own monthly actuals at roughly **T+30–45 days** via the "Union Government Monthly
Accounts Dashboard" — genuinely the fastest free fiscal-flow series in this program's entire
catalog, materially faster than OBICUS, IIP capital goods, or GFCF, per `docs/cycles/
16-capex-cycle.md` §C.4 (full description, data-source URLs, and the state-level CAG monthly-
accounts caveat already built there, cross-referenced and not re-derived here to avoid the exact
double-pull this chapter's own §C rules against). For this entry's purposes, CGA's value is not the
capex line L11 already reads — it is that CGA's monthly revenue-and-expenditure actuals are the
*only* genuinely high-frequency, free window onto whether a scheme announced in a budget speech
(PM-KISAN, a transfer scheme, a subsidy expansion) is actually being disbursed at the pace and
scale the announcement implied, versus remaining an announcement — precisely the composition-watch
role Part C assigns this data source under Stage-2 briefing context rather than a Stage-1 seat.

**States' share: fiscal federalism as the harder half of the story.** India's general-government
fiscal position is a Centre-plus-states aggregate, and the states' own electoral calendar is
*continuous* rather than five-yearly — some state assembly election falls due almost every year
(atlas row 3.8, "State-election midterm pulses... too diffuse to price," rejected as a signal for
exactly this reason). States' consolidated fiscal deficit ran **2.7% of combined GSDP in 2022–23
(actual)**, widening to **3.41% in 2023–24 (revised estimate)** and budgeted at roughly **3.1–3.2%
for 2024–25** — above the Fifteenth Finance Commission's own 3%-of-GSDP recommendation
[Verified via search, PRS/NSE state-finance reporting; precise decimal composition varies slightly
across the RE/BE vintages cited — VERIFY exact figure at next refresh]. States' consolidated debt
stock declined to roughly **28.1% of GDP by end-March 2024**, down from a pandemic-era peak of
**31% in 2020–21** [Verified via search]. States fund this borrowing overwhelmingly through **State
Development Loans (SDLs)** — the state-government bond market RBI manages the auction calendar
for — which is itself the mechanical link between the electoral composition story (a state
announcing a farm-loan waiver or a cash-transfer scheme, Part B.2 episode 5 and the closing
discussion) and the debt market this desk's L10/L12 seats already watch through the credit and
real-estate blocks: a state fiscal slippage shows up first in its SDL spread over the Centre's own
G-secs, not in any Union-government number this chapter's Centre-focused episode table can capture
on its own.

**Off-budget borrowing eras: the measurement wedge, stated with real numbers.** The clearest,
best-documented instance of India's fiscal accounting understating the true deficit is the **Food
Corporation of India (FCI)** food-subsidy financing chain. Rather than fund the full food subsidy
through the Union Budget, successive budgets left FCI to borrow the shortfall — via the **National
Small Savings Fund (NSSF)**, bank cash-credit lines, and short-term bonds — with the Centre
recording only its *budgeted* subsidy transfer as expenditure, not FCI's actual, larger financing
need. By **2019–20, FCI's outstanding dues to the NSSF alone stood at roughly ₹2.54 lakh crore**,
with total off-budget borrowing (FCI plus other public-sector financing vehicles) projected to
exceed **₹2.35 lakh crore in 2020–21**, up from a revised **₹2.17 lakh crore in the 2019–20 Budget**
[Verified via search]. India's **Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG)** explicitly flagged this
practice as understating the fiscal deficit and preventing a transparent read of the government's
true fiscal indicators [Verified via search] — the institutional check that eventually forced the
correction. **Budget 2020** first brought partial transparency by showing the off-budget bonds
explicitly as a distinct line; **Budget 2021–22** went further, discontinuing the NSSF-to-FCI
lending practice outright and providing the full **₹2.54 lakh crore** to wipe out FCI's arrears
on-budget in one shot, with Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman explicitly framing the move as
transparency reform [Verified via search]. The honest lesson for a fiscal-cycle read: **for
roughly a decade, a meaningful slice of India's true fiscal impulse was invisible in the headline
deficit number**, which means any historical fiscal-deficit-based signal built naively on the
2010s headline series would have understated actual fiscal expansion in exactly the years (2014–
2020) this chapter's episode table (B.2) needs to read most carefully — a measurement-wedge
caveat this desk carries forward rather than papers over.

### B.2 The episode record

**(1) The 2003–08 consolidation: the FRBM era, deficit to 2.5% by FY08.** Union government gross
fiscal deficit fell from **4.34% of GDP in 2003–04** to **3.32% in 2006–07** and **2.54% in
2007–08** [Verified via search] — comfortably below the FRBM's own 3% target, reached *ahead* of
the amended 2008–09 deadline, and achieved during a period of strong nominal GDP growth that made
the arithmetic easier without requiring painful nominal expenditure cuts. This is the honest
counter-example to a pure-cynicism reading of Indian fiscal policy: a genuine, sustained, rules-
anchored consolidation that held through one full electoral cycle (the 2004 general election fell
in the middle of this run, and consolidation continued on the other side of it), disciplined by a
freshly enacted statute rather than by electoral opportunism.

**(2) The 2008–09 double stimulus: deficit 2.5%→~6.0–6.2% FY09, counter-cyclical, election-
adjacent, disentangled honestly.** The Global Financial Crisis prompted three fiscal stimulus
packages in quick succession — **7 December 2008, 2 January 2009, and 24 February 2009**
[Verified via search] — pushing the Centre's fiscal deficit from a **2.5% budget-estimate target
for 2008–09** to an actual outturn most commonly cited at **6.0% (revised estimate) to 6.2%
(provisional actual)** of GDP [Verified via search; the exact decimal varies slightly by vintage of
government document cited — VERIFY current authoritative figure]. The general election that
returned the UPA to power for a second term was held in **April–May 2009**, meaning the third
stimulus package (24 February 2009) and the accompanying **interim budget** were both delivered in
the immediate run-up to the vote, with the **full budget following in July 2009**, after the
result. This episode is the hardest one in the whole record to disentangle cleanly, and this
chapter states that honestly rather than forcing a verdict either way: the stimulus was a **real,
internationally coordinated, textbook counter-cyclical response** to the sharpest global demand
shock since the 1930s — every major economy ran a comparable deficit expansion in the same window
— and it is simultaneously true that the expansion's timing placed maximal pre-election stimulus
directly ahead of a national vote, exactly the Nordhaus/Rogoff-Sibert opportunistic-signaling
pattern in its textbook shape. Both readings are correct at once; the honest conclusion is that
this episode alone cannot identify which mechanism dominated, and this chapter does not claim it
can.

**(3) 2009–2012: the exit that wasn't.** Fiscal expansion continued into **2009–10, where the
deficit widened further to 6.8% of GDP** [Verified via search] — the second half of the "double
stimulus" in episode (2)'s framing. The government's own stated glide path called for **5.5% in
2010–11 and 4% in 2011–12** [Verified via search]. The 2010–11 number came in closer to target, but
the improvement was substantially a **one-off statistical artifact**: the **3G and Broadband
Wireless Access (BWA) spectrum auction** delivered roughly **₹1.06 lakh crore** against a budgeted
**₹35,000 crore** — a windfall equivalent to roughly **1.3% of GDP in the first quarter alone**
[Verified via search] — flattering the headline deficit print without representing any underlying
structural consolidation. The following year exposed exactly that: **2011–12's budget target of
4.6% of GDP was missed by a wide margin, with the actual outturn settling around 5.7–5.9%**
[Verified via search], driven by subsidy slippage (fuel, fertilizer, food) as global growth slowed
through the Euro-area crisis and domestic reform momentum stalled. Read together, episodes (2) and
(3) describe **three consecutive years (2008–09 through 2011–12) in which every attempt to exit
crisis-era stimulus either failed outright or succeeded only via a non-repeatable windfall** — the
"exit that wasn't" is not a single missed target but a multi-year pattern of a fiscal path that
kept promising consolidation and kept finding a reason (auction luck, subsidy overruns, a slowing
global economy) not to deliver it, right up to the ratings-threat turning point in episode (4).

**(4) 2013–14: the pre-election squeeze paradox — consolidation into an election, under a ratings
threat.** After the 2011–12 miss, **Fitch and Standard & Poor's both threatened to downgrade
India's sovereign credit rating** [Verified via search], and Finance Minister P. Chidambaram
responded by pursuing what contemporary coverage called India's **most austere budget in years**
for 2013–14 [Verified via search] — doing so *against* pressure from within his own Congress party
to loosen spending ahead of the general election due by May 2014 [Verified via search]. The
**2013–14 fiscal deficit target of 4.8% of GDP was actually contained at 4.6%**, an improvement
rather than a slippage, delivered in the fiscal year immediately preceding a general election
[Verified via search]. This episode is the cleanest available counter-example to a naive "every
pre-election year loosens" prior: when a credible external threat (a sovereign downgrade, which
would have raised borrowing costs economy-wide, not just for the government) outweighs the
electoral incentive to spend, **fiscal discipline can win even in a campaign year** — direct
confirmation that this entry's L5 seat is correct to schedule *volatility*, not a directional
fiscal-loosening bet, around election windows: the 2013–14 record shows the loosening does not
always come.

**(5) 2016–2019: the transfer-scheme era begins.** **PM-KISAN (Pradhan Mantri Kisan Samman
Nidhi)** was announced in the **interim budget of 1 February 2019** — presented roughly three
months before the April–May 2019 general election — providing **₹6,000 per year, in three
installments of ₹2,000**, via direct benefit transfer to bank accounts, to small and marginal
farmer families with combined landholding up to two hectares, with the scheme launched in person
by Prime Minister Modi at Gorakhpur, Uttar Pradesh, on **24 February 2019**, and disbursement
beginning that same month — inside the interim budget's own fiscal year, ahead of the vote
[Verified via search]. Estimated coverage at launch exceeded **12 crore farmer families**
[Verified via search]. This is, in this chapter's own judgment, **the cleanest opportunistic-
composition exhibit in India's entire modern fiscal record**: a targeted, highly visible, directly
disbursed transfer, announced in the one interim-budget slot the constitution permits before a
national election, timed and launched to complete its first disbursement cycle *before* voters went
to the polls — a textbook instance of Rogoff's (1990) visible-spending-over-investment signaling
mechanism, executed with a modern DBT rail that did not exist for any of episodes (1)–(4).

**Farm-loan waivers, the parallel state-level wave of 2017–18.** In the same window, several
states announced large farm-loan waivers around their own assembly elections: **Uttar Pradesh
(₹36,000 crore) and Maharashtra (₹34,020 crore)** in 2017, together reported at roughly **12–13% of
each state's GSDP that year**; **Karnataka (~₹8,100–8,165 crore)**, benefiting an estimated
**22.3 lakh farmers**, also in 2017; and **Punjab**, where sourcing diverges — contemporary
reporting cites both a **₹1,000 crore** figure and a separate **₹10,000 crore** figure (reported
elsewhere as roughly 9% of the state's GDP that year) for what may be different stages or
components of the same waiver [VERIFY: Punjab's exact waiver cost — sources conflict and this
chapter does not resolve the discrepancy]. Contemporary press coverage aggregated the national wave
into a claim that farm-loan waivers nationally would reach roughly **2% of GDP by the 2019 polls**
[VERIFY: headline claim, methodology not independently confirmed this pass]. Whatever the precise
aggregate, the direction and timing are unambiguous and independently verified state by state: a
cluster of large, visible, debt-relief transfers to a specific, electorally pivotal constituency
(farmers), concentrated in the twelve months before each state's own election — Drazen–Eslava's
(2010) voter-friendly-spending mechanism, replicated across five major Indian states within an
eighteen-month window, and the direct institutional predecessor to PM-KISAN's own national,
recurring version of the same instrument.

**(6) 2020–21 COVID: the honest counter-cyclical pulse, deficit 9.2% FY21.** The pandemic pushed
the Centre's fiscal deficit from an original **2020–21 budget estimate of 3.5%** to a **revised
estimate of 9.5%**, with the final actual outturn settling at **9.2% of GDP** (₹18.21 lakh crore,
roughly ₹27,194 crore below the revised estimate) [Verified via search] — the largest single-year
fiscal expansion in this chapter's entire episode record, invoking the FRBM escape clause described
in B.1. Unlike episode (2), this expansion has essentially no live election-timing ambiguity to
disentangle: no general election fell anywhere near this window, and the deficit spike tracks a
verifiable, exogenous, once-in-a-century public-health shock and its associated lockdown-driven
revenue collapse rather than any plausible electoral-signaling motive. This is the episode this
chapter treats as the cleanest test of the "counter-cyclical, not opportunistic" alternative
explanation this literature always has to rule out before crediting an electoral mechanism — and
here the counter-cyclical read wins outright, with no serious competing story.

**(7) 2021–26: the capex-composition era.** **Budget 2021–22** announced the fiscal-consolidation
**glide path** still in force: a 2021–22 deficit target of 6.8% of GDP, declining to **below 4.5%
of GDP by 2025–26** [Verified via search]. The path has run largely on schedule and, per the
capex monograph's own arc (`docs/cycles/16-capex-cycle.md`, cross-referenced and not re-derived
here), the consolidation has been achieved substantially by holding down the *revenue* side of
spending while protecting — and in nominal terms growing — the *capital-expenditure* line, the
opposite compositional tilt from Rogoff's (1990) prediction that pre-election budgets favor
consumption over investment. The Centre's deficit narrowed to **5.6% of GDP in 2023–24 (actual)**,
against a Revised Estimate of 5.8% and an original Budget Estimate of 5.9% [Verified via search].
The **2024–25 election-year sequence is the chapter's cleanest recent test of composition
discipline under an electoral deadline**: the **interim budget of 1 February 2024** — presented
roughly three months before the April–May 2024 general election — set the deficit target at
**5.1% of GDP**, *tighter*, not looser, than the prior year's actual; the **full budget of July
2024**, presented after the election by the returning government, revised the target down further
to **4.9%**; the February 2025 Revised Estimate brought it to **4.8%**; and the final actual
settled at **4.77%** [Verified via search, all figures]. **This sequence is the single strongest
piece of evidence in this chapter's own record against a naive Nordhaus-style "every election year
loosens fiscal policy" prior**: the one interim budget most exposed to opportunistic-cycle pressure
in the entire 2021–26 glide path *tightened* the target rather than loosening it, extending exactly
the same discipline the 2013–14 episode (4) demonstrated a decade earlier. Reported FY26 guidance
clusters around **4.4% of GDP** [Verified via search; precise final figure pending the FY26 actual
— VERIFY at next refresh], consistent with the government's own restated commitment to the 2021
glide path.

**The 2023–24 women's cash-transfer wave — the modern state-level composition instrument, dated
and verified.** Ahead of the November 2023 Madhya Pradesh assembly election, the state government
launched the **Ladli Behna Yojana** on 5 March 2023, a direct monthly cash transfer to eligible
women, beginning at **₹1,000/month** with the first installment disbursed in June 2023 and raised
to **₹1,250/month from October 2023** — inside the same calendar year as the election — with
further increases to ₹1,500/month following the BJP's subsequent victory [Verified via search].
Ahead of the November 2024 Maharashtra assembly election, the state launched the **Ladki Bahin
Yojana** in **August 2024** — three months after the April–May 2024 general election and three
months before the state election — paying **₹1,500/month** to eligible women aged 21–65 from
lower-income households, enrolling more than **2.6 crore (26 million) women**, with an announced
**₹35,000 crore budgetary allocation for 2024–25** [Verified via search]; the ruling Mahayuti
alliance went on to win the subsequent state election decisively, and post-election coverage widely
credited the scheme as a defining factor. Both schemes are, in this chapter's own reading, a modern,
gender-targeted, direct-transfer descendant of exactly the mechanism episode (5)'s farm-loan
waivers and PM-KISAN embody — a large, highly visible, bank-account-disbursed transfer to a
specific, electorally salient constituency, launched inside the twelve months preceding the
relevant election, with the funding drawn overwhelmingly from the *state* fiscal envelope (and
therefore from the SDL market B.1 already flags) rather than from the Union budget this episode
table otherwise tracks — a further, verified confirmation that India's PBC signature, from Khemani's
1960–1994 state panel through 2024, remains the same one: **composition, targeted and visible, not
a Union deficit-total blowout.**

### B.3 The pre-election record vs the market

**The desk's own pre-registered test.** `research/register/trial-ledger.md`'s FP1a/FP1b entries
(2026-09-02) tested India's own market-factor data (`iima_monthly_factors.csv`, monthly 1993-10 to
2025-12) against the eight general-election result months usable within that window (1996-05,
1998-03, 1999-10, 2004-05, 2009-05, 2014-05, 2019-05, 2024-06 — **n=8**, tiny, stated up front, out
of the **n=9** general elections since 1991 the atlas's own row 3.7 counts; the 1991 election
predates the factor series). Results, now available at `research/cycles/fiscal-deep/
fiscal-RESULTS.md`: **FP1a** — the folk "pre-election rally" claim — **passed as registered**: the
mean monthly market-factor return in the three-month campaign window (the window ending in the
result month) was **+3.99%/month**, against an all-months mean of **+0.72%/month**, with **7 of 8**
elections showing a positive window mean (the pre-registered bar was mean-above-base AND ≥6/8
positive). **FP1b** — the measurement leg supporting L5's own scheduling rationale, with no pass/
fail bar — found the median absolute monthly move **inside** election windows at **5.4%**, against
**4.2%** for all months, and result-month absolute moves ranging from **1.0% to 33.6%** (median
6.5%) [all figures per `fiscal-RESULTS.md`, verified against the committed vaulted dataset].

**Why a registered pass is not, by itself, a license to trade direction.** This chapter does not
re-run or re-interpret the dissection the results file itself already carries out (that
deflation, and its routing consequence, is Parts D–H's own document, `partDH-verdict-routing.md`,
cross-referenced and not re-derived here) — but the headline number cannot be read honestly without
naming the three reasons, already on the record, that keep FP1a's pass from being a directional
signal: the registered window **ends in** the result month itself, conflating ordinary pre-election
drift with the result-day surprise it is meant to be measured separately from; a single election
(May 2009, +18.8%/month — the global post-GFC rebound coinciding with the vote) carries the pooled
mean, with the other seven averaging a far more ordinary ≈+1.9%/month; and **n=8 is an association
sample with no counterfactual control**, exactly the kind of trial count this desk's deflated-
Sharpe discipline (Contract §9) exists to keep honest.

**The result-month column re-proves the design's own founding sentence.** The same result-month
data that produced FP1a's pooled pass shows result-month returns ranging from **−17.7% (2004) to
+33.6% (2009)** — the identical range, to the decimal, that `config/ladder.yaml`'s L5 entry and
atlas row 3.7 already carry as provenance ("2004 −15.5%, 2009 +10.7% halt, 2024 −5.9%" in the
atlas's own index-level framing; the factor-level figures cited here are the trial's own measured
numbers on a different series, consistent in direction and order of magnitude). **Three elections
— 2004, 2009, 2024 — moved markets violently in a direction nobody had scheduled**: 2004's Congress-
led UPA victory over a widely-expected NDA "India Shining" re-election produced the sharpest single-
day crash in the sample; 2009's larger-than-expected UPA mandate (removing Left-party veto leverage
over reform) produced the sharpest rally; 2024's weaker-than-expected BJP mandate (a coalition
government rather than the anticipated outright majority) produced a sharp initial selloff. **No
version of the fiscal-policy-composition theory argued in Part A predicts the sign of any of these
three moves** — Rogoff's signaling model, Shi-Svensson's developing-democracy magnitude finding,
and Drazen-Eslava's composition-shift mechanism are all theories about *what governments do before*
an election, not about *what markets learn on results day* about which government will be doing
it. This is precisely why L5's design, unchanged by this entry, treats election and budget dates as
a **scheduling-only, reduce-only** input (`ladder.yaml`: `reduce_only: true`, role "leverage/vol
scheduling into election/budget windows... never a directional bet") — the fiscal-cycle theory
argued in Part A explains the *composition* of what gets spent, which this chapter's Part B.2
record confirms in rich, verified, scheme-level detail; it has never claimed, and this desk does
not treat it as claiming, to predict the *sign* of the market's reaction to who wins.

## C. The verdict

**CONTEXT folded into L5/L6, exactly as the atlas states — and here is precisely why, claim by
claim.** Three distinct pieces of content live inside "the fiscal/political spending cycle," and
each already has a home in this program's registry before this chapter was ever commissioned:

1. **Election and budget-date volatility scheduling is L5's job, already seated.** The budget
   calendar is fixed by law (Feb 1 since 2017); the election calendar is fixed by the Election
   Commission; both are public, non-forecastable-in-advance-of-announcement dates around which
   volatility reliably rises (FP1b: window absolute moves 5.4% vs 4.2% all-months) while direction
   stays a coin flip determined only on results day (FP1a's dissected pass; the −17.7%..+33.6%
   result-month range; the atlas's own 2004/2009/2024 provenance). `config/ladder.yaml`'s
   `L5_calendar_windows` (tier B, `reduce_only: true`, tau_half 1–2 months) already does exactly
   this job and nothing more. Nothing in Part A or Part B's episode record argues for widening L5's
   mandate to a directional bet; if anything, episode (4)'s 2013–14 consolidation-into-an-election
   and the 2024 interim budget's own tightening (episode 7) are two independent, verified
   confirmations that "election year = fiscal loosening" is not even reliably true of the *policy*,
   let alone of the market's reaction to it.

2. **Fiscal-dominance regimes — a debt trajectory sustained enough to structurally constrain
   monetary policy — are L15's domain, not this entry's.** `config/ladder.yaml`'s
   `L15_long_wave_fiscal` entry (tier C, tau_half 120–999 months, `reduce_only: true`, expressed
   only through the structural gold floor and slow-debasement tail budget, per `docs/DESIGN.md`
   §4.3) already carries the *structural*, decades-scale version of "does the state's own debt
   position force the central bank's hand" — the debt monograph's own subject
   (`docs/cycles/05-debt-supercycle.md`), cross-referenced and not re-derived here. This entry's
   subject is the *5-yearly, electorally timed* fiscal pulse, an entirely different frequency band
   from L15's multi-decade fiscal-dominance question; conflating the two would be exactly the
   label-shopping error this program's own atlas §14 de-duplication principle and the Kitchin/
   Juglar monograph's closing argument (`research/cycles/kitchin-juglar/partAB-theory-cases.md`
   §B.3) both warn against.

3. **The fiscal impulse's macro transmission is already a downstream input to L6 and the shared
   macro-credit block — a separate seat here would double-count it.** A pre-election spending pulse
   (episode 5's transfer waves, episode 2's stimulus) works on growth, inflation, and credit
   conditions through the same channels every other fiscal impulse works through — and RBI's own
   monetary stance (`L6_monetary_stance`, tau_half 12–24 months) already reads and reacts to those
   downstream growth/inflation prints with its own ~1-year lag, per the calibration in
   `config/ladder.yaml`'s L6 entry and the MP1–MP3 trials in `research/register/trial-ledger.md`.
   The 2008–09 stimulus (episode 2) and the 2020–21 COVID pulse (episode 6) are each, in this
   sense, **stance events that L6 already sees arriving through growth and inflation data** —
   `docs/DESIGN.md` §4.2's de-duplication rule (the shared macro-credit-block budget, cited at
   ≤20% of regime score in this program's own prior monograph and at ≤25% in `docs/DESIGN.md` §5.1
   — the registry's enforced number, not re-litigated here) exists precisely to stop the same
   underlying macro impulse from buying two budget lines under two different names, the identical
   discipline this desk already applied to retire the "Juglar" label while keeping its object fully
   funded inside L10/L11.

**What remains, after removing the timing claim (owned by L5) and the macro-transmission claim
(owned by L6/the shared block), is the atlas's own sizing verdict, restated with this chapter's own
evidence behind it: the fiscal impulse as a macro conditioner is real (Part A's literature, Part
B.2's verified episode record) but small relative to the credit, global-cycle, and monetary blocks
this program's center of gravity already occupies** — exactly the "small vs the macro block"
one-line verdict atlas row 2.7 states, now earned rather than merely asserted. What does carry
forward, at zero allocation authority, is a **composition-watch**: which visible spending category
(a farm-loan waiver, a PM-KISAN-style national transfer, a Ladli-Behna-style state transfer, a
capex push) a government is emphasizing ahead of a known election date, read as Stage-2 briefing
context for the desk's forward-looking, human-vetoed judgment layer (Contract §2's Stage 2) — never
as a Stage-1 signal, and never with any allocation authority of its own — plus the **CGA monthly
accounts table**, already seated in the capex runsheet (`docs/cycles/16-capex-cycle.md` §C.4) as
the free, high-frequency instrument for checking whether an announced scheme is actually being
disbursed, with no separate pull proposed here. No seat, no budget — precisely what the atlas
already wrote, now with the full argument, and India's own verified record, standing behind it.

The pattern across this chapter is the one this program's sibling monographs keep re-finding from
their own angles: a real, carefully argued literature — Nordhaus's founding instinct, Rogoff's
signaling refinement, Shi-Svensson's developing-democracy magnitude, Drazen-Eslava's composition
mechanism, Brender-Drazen's own decay finding — survives fully at the resolution its own evidence
supports (a real compositional tilt in visible pre-election spending, verified in India scheme by
scheme from Khemani's 1960s state data through the 2024 cash-transfer wave) while adding **no**
allocation authority beyond what this program's registry already prices through L5's scheduling
seat, L6's macro-transmission seat, and L15's structural fiscal-dominance seat. Retiring the idea
of a standalone "political cycle" seat costs this program nothing: the object — a real, dated,
rupee-denominated compositional shift in government spending ahead of known elections — stays
exactly where the evidence and the existing ladder already put it, read as briefing context for
Stage 2's human judgment, never as a Stage-1 signal with a budget of its own.
