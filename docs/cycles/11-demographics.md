## PART A — Theory and evidence

### A.1 The demographic transition as the one truly forecastable macro process

Every other object in this atlas is forecast from a model of behavior — credit booms, inflation
regimes, monetary-policy transmission — and every such model can be wrong because the future can
behave differently than the past. Age structure differs in kind, not degree: a 20-year-ahead
projection of who will be 15–64 is, for most of that population, arithmetic on people already alive,
not a forecast of behavior at all. Everyone who will be 15–64 at T+20 was born between T−49
and T+5. Roughly the first 45 of those 50 birth-years — on the order of nine-tenths of the T+20
working-age population — is alive and countable *today*, subject only to mortality (the single most
predictable demographic rate, rarely surprising by more than a point or two even across pandemics)
and net migration. Only the youngest sliver, those turning 15 in the window's final five years,
depends on a fertility rate not yet realized. This "demographic momentum" is why age-structure
projections are the nearest thing to a free 20-year lookahead available under Contract §9's
out-of-sample discipline: the input data for most of the window has already happened.

**The UN World Population Prospects (WPP), and what "near-deterministic" does not mean.** The UN
Population Division's WPP (`population.un.org/wpp`; free; biennial, 2024 the current 28th edition —
`docs/masterplan/A-data-catalog.md` M1) applies this cohort-component logic at global scale: current
population by age and sex, projected forward under assumed fertility, mortality and migration paths.
Since the 2010s the fertility and mortality legs have run probabilistically — Bayesian hierarchical
models fit via Markov-chain Monte Carlo, yielding a distribution of trajectories, not one point path
— and the **2024 revision extended this to international migration for the first time**, the one leg
previously deterministic **[Verified via UN Population Division documentation and multiple independent summaries this
session]**. This
is Tier-A methodology by this program's own ladder (Contract §4: ≥30 independent observations,
purged-CV-eligible) — but Tier-A on the *demographic* object, not its market translation, and that
distinction is this file's entire argument.

**The revision record — a systematic, one-directional miss.** WPP's successive editions are the
cleanest evidence of where the model has been wrong, and the direction is consistent, not noisy.
Global population-in-2100 point forecasts fell across three consecutive editions — **11.2 billion
(2015) → 10.9 billion (2019) → a peak of roughly 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s (2024)** **[Verified via UN DESA's 2024 Summary of Results and multiple secondary summaries this
session]** — each edition marking down future population because realized fertility kept coming in
below the *previous* edition's assumption. Our World in Data's own retrospective on 50+ years of UN
projections finds the same regional pattern: in Europe, "UN projections would often overestimate the
number of future children ... but underestimate the number of elderly people, because the UN expected
birth rates to be higher than they turned out" **[Verified via Our World in Data's own hosted
retrospective this session]**. The bias has not visibly resolved even in the latest vintage: a
2025–26 comparison of WPP2024's near-term projected births against subsequently *registered* births in
37 countries with ≥90%-complete registration found actual births running **roughly 9.3% below the
WPP2024 projection** in aggregate, with gaps as wide as Colombia (−36.5%) and South Africa (−33.4%)
**[VERIFY: a journalistic report (zmescience.com, citing unnamed "economists"), not a located primary
paper — sourced only secondarily this session]**. The honest characterization: **WPP's fertility error
has run one direction across every edition with a clean before/after comparison — toward assuming
fertility higher, and its decline slower, than what subsequently happens** — for design purposes, an
India-relevant working-age-share *peak* should be read as more likely *earlier* than the current
edition's central case states, and any single-edition number re-pulled at every biennial vintage
rather than frozen (the vintage-sensitivity discipline `A-data-catalog.md` M1 already flags).

**The honest limits — migration and participation, not fertility.** Over a 15–20-year India-relevant
window the fertility leg barely matters (A.1's own arithmetic); what moves the usable forecast is the
two legs demographic momentum does *not* neutralize. **Migration** is near-irrelevant nationally
(net international migration is a small single-digit-percent-of-cohort factor) but is the dominant
unresolved variable at the *state* level, where internal migration reallocates an already-fixed
national labor supply across wildly different regional age structures — A.4 is the load-bearing case.
**Participation** is the larger gap: age structure counts bodies, not workers, and the gap between
"working-age" and "working" is Bloom-Canning-Sevilla's conditionality (A.2(a)) — measurable only with
a lag (PLFS) and not forecastable at all. Age structure is Tier-A data on a near-deterministic input;
how many of those bodies work, at what productivity, is a genuinely open question this file never
claims to have closed.

### A.2 The channels — five claimed transmission mechanisms, graded independently

The literature claims age structure moves markets through at least five channels, of varying and
non-overlapping evidentiary quality. Grading them separately, rather than averaging into one
"demographics matters" verdict, is the discipline this section enforces — the same discipline
`gold-deep` and `realrates-deep` apply to their own adjacent literatures rather than accept a single
omnibus narrative grade.

**(a) Labor-supply / growth accounting — the demographic-dividend arithmetic.** David Bloom and
Jeffrey Williamson's "Demographic Transitions and Economic Miracles in Emerging Asia" (*World Bank
Economic Review* 12(3), 1998, pp. 419–455) **[Verified via NBER's page (NBER WP 6268) and the World Bank Economic Review's listing this
session]** is the founding decomposition: demographic variables in a cross-country growth
regression attribute **1.4–1.9 percentage points of East Asia's 6.11%/yr per-capita GDP growth,
1965–1990 — the largest regional demographic effect in their panel** — to the working-age share
rising faster than the dependent share as fertility fell ahead of mortality. Popular summaries
round this to "**a third of the East Asian miracle**"; a companion literature puts the fuller
contribution, including secondary savings/investment effects, as high as **one-third to one-half**
**[Verified: the 1.4–1.9pp/6.11% figure and "roughly one-third" framing via multiple summaries this
session, incl. the paper's World Bank-hosted PDF; the wider "one-third to one-half" figure — VERIFY]**. This is Tier-B evidence by this
program's ladder — a single, highly-cited panel, not ≥30 observations purged of the miracle's other
drivers (export orientation, education, the Bretton Woods-era peg) — and the load-bearing qualifier
arrives one paper later. Bloom, Canning & Sevilla's *The Demographic Dividend* (RAND MR-1274, 2003)
**[Verified via dossier 07's own recall-verified citation, cross-checked this session]** states the
dividend is **conditional, not automatic**: a rising working-age share is dependency-ratio-shaped
*potential*, cashed only via productive employment — absent that, a youth bulge is a liability, not a
growth event. **Grade: Tier B mechanism**, with the conditionality treated as load-bearing, not a
footnote — India's own translation of this channel is A.4's central question.

**(b) Savings / rates channel — life-cycle saving and the demographics-and-r-star literature.** The
mechanical version is Modigliani's life-cycle hypothesis: individuals dis-save young, save through
middle age, dis-save again in retirement, so a population bulge moving through its saving years should
push the aggregate saving rate — and, via loanable funds, the equilibrium real rate — down while it
saves and up as it retires. Two modern papers formalize this cross-country. Carlos Carvalho, Andrea
Ferrero & Fernanda Nechio, "Demographics and Real Interest Rates: Inspecting the Mechanism" (FRBSF WP
2016-05; *European Economic Review* 2016) and its 2023–25 extension with Felipe Mazin (CEPR DP18616;
*Journal of International Economics* 157, 2025) **[Verified via SF Fed, CEPR and JIE listings this session]** build a multi-country OLG model where both domestic and *foreign* demographics
move a country's real rate through capital mobility — a rising global old-age dependency ratio is, in
their calibration, a first-order contributor to the secular decline in real rates since the 1980s.
Etienne Gagnon, Benjamin Johannsen & David Lopez-Salido, "Understanding the New Normal: The Role of
Demographics" (Fed FEDS WP 2016-080; *IMF Economic Review* 69(2), 2021) **[Verified via the Fed's hosted working paper and the IMF Economic Review's listing this
session]** calibrate a richer OLG model to US demographic change and find it accounts for **a little
more than a 1-percentage-point decline in both trend real GDP growth and the real rate since the
1980s** — a large share of the total secular decline on some estimates. Both papers are careful,
model-based, and mutually corroborating on direction; neither escapes the identification problem
`realrates-deep` A.3 already documents in detail for the *identical* mechanism under Goodhart-Pradhan's
name — the labor-supply-shock story runs both directions depending on demographic phase, and
`realrates-deep`'s own verdict — "the one genuinely live case" in the real-rate literature, unresolved
by 2022–24 either way — is inherited here rather than re-argued. **Grade: Tier B mechanism
(multiple independent estimates, directionally consistent), Tier C on any point-in-time claim about
where a country sits on the curve** — atlas 0.3's own caution, imported unchanged.

**(c) Asset-demand channels — the favored-cohort model, the MY-versus-M/O confusion, and the
meltdown debate's clean empirical failure.** The most literature-crowded and most publicly overclaimed
of the five channels, and it rewards separating two distinct models the popular press routinely
conflates. John Geanakoplos, Michael Magill & Martine Quinzii, "Demography and the Long-Run
Predictability of the Stock Market" (*Brookings Papers on Economic Activity*, 2004, pp. 241–326)
**[Verified via the Brookings-hosted PDF and IDEAS/RePEc this session]** build
a general-equilibrium "favored-cohort" model around the **MY ratio — middle-aged adults relative to
young adults (roughly age 20–29)** — a large middle-aged cohort, accumulating retirement savings,
bids up securities relative to a small young cohort borrowing against future income; critically, the
effect is **not weakened by rational anticipation** — agents who fully forecast the demographic cycle
bid prices up *more*, not less, because anticipated future scarcity of buyers is itself capitalized
into today's price. Zheng Liu & Mark Spiegel's "Boomer Retirement: Headwinds for U.S. Equity
Markets?" (FRBSF *Economic Letter* 2011-26) **[Verified: authors, venue, year via the SF Fed's own
economic-letter series and multiple independent summaries this session]** applies a *different*
ratio to the same intuition — the **M/O ratio: middle-aged (40–49) relative to old-age (60–69)**,
chosen to capture the buy-versus-sell-to-retirees dynamic rather than GMQ's borrow-versus-save one.
**The task brief's "MY-ratio work" spanning both papers conflates two distinct ratios from two
distinct models; this file does not carry that conflation forward.** Liu-Spiegel's claim was precise
and falsifiable: the M/O ratio tracked the S&P 500 trailing P/E closely through 2010, and their model
projected P/E falling from **roughly 15 in 2010 to about 8.3 by 2025** (a 2013-vintage re-estimate:
**17 to 8.23 by 2025**), before a partial recovery toward 9 by 2030 **[Verified: figures via multiple independent secondary summaries and the Society of Actuaries' 2015
review this session; primary FRBSF page blocked by egress]**. **This
is now a resolved, failed forecast.** Writing in September 2026, past the 2025 target, the S&P 500
trailing P/E has traded far above 8.3 throughout, and the SF Fed's own later commentary confirms the
break happened in real time: "since 2011 ... the close correlation ... has disappeared, with the M/O
ratio continuing to fall but the P/E ratio steadily climbing" **[Verified via SF Fed's 2014-and-later
follow-ups this session; exact current P/E level — VERIFY, no live pull this session]**. This dated failure is the modern instance of
an older one: N. Gregory Mankiw & David Weil, "The Baby Boom, the Baby Bust, and the Housing Market"
(*Regional Science and Urban Economics* 19(2), 1989) **[Verified via ScienceDirect this session]** forecast US real housing prices would **fall 47% between 1987
and 2007**; real prices instead *rose* substantially, through the largest housing boom in the modern
US record. James Poterba's "Demographic Structure and Asset Returns" (*Review of Economics and
Statistics* 83(4), 2001) and 2004 Jackson Hole follow-up **[Verified via MIT Economics and the Kansas City Fed's hosted PDFs this session]** is the standing rebuttal both episodes illustrate:
**no evidence US real stock returns over 75 years correlate with age structure**, with only weak,
inconsistent evidence even on T-bill returns; the "meltdown" intuition (retirees sell everything at
once) ignores lifetime-uncertainty and bequest motives keeping retiree asset demand elevated longer
than the mechanical story assumes — a point Andrew Abel's 2001 NBER rebuttal (*Will Bequests
Attenuate the Predicted Meltdown...*, NBER WP 8131) **[Verified via NBER's own hosted page this
session]** partially answers (a bequest-motive model still predicts *some* decline, because Poterba
treats asset *supply* as fixed) without rescuing either paper's dated, now-failed forecast. **Grade:
Tier C on any asset-demand valuation-timing claim** — mechanistically plausible, twice independently
modeled, with a clean falsified test on the record and Poterba's 75-year null as the harder evidence.

**(d) Inflation channel — Goodhart-Pradhan, cross-referenced not re-derived.** Goodhart & Pradhan's
*The Great Demographic Reversal* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2020) argues three-plus decades of low global
inflation and real rates were substantially a one-off effect of a huge, cheap, underemployed global
labor supply — China's internal migration, post-1989 Eastern Europe, rising Western female
participation — entering the world workforce, a shock now reversing as those cohorts age out and
China's own working-age population shrinks (A.3). `inflation-deep` A.2 and `realrates-deep` A.3
already carry this thesis at full length on both sides of the 2021–23 test episode; this file does not
re-litigate it, only notes the mechanism is identical to (b)'s savings-and-rates channel run through
labor-bargaining-power rather than loanable funds — one demographic story, two transmission legs,
already scored **Tier C, live and unresolved** in both sibling files.

**(e) Equity-return translation — the honest read of DG1 plus the published cross-country
evidence.** This is where the other four channels are supposed to cash out into a tradable signal, and
the channel this program has actually tested on its own data. DG1's result — pooled correlation
between trailing 10-year population growth and forward 10-year real equity returns across 18 JST
countries, 1880–2010, is **−0.03** (n=1,977), flips sign by era (**+0.22** pre-1945, **−0.14**
1945–79, **−0.08** 1980–2010), sign-inconsistent across countries (4/16 positive, median −0.26) — is
deliberately the *crudest* version of this channel (population growth, not age structure), and DG2
pre-registers UN WPP age-structure work as the follow-up "with the SAME bar: sign consistency across
countries before any India use." The published cross-country literature on the *richer* age-structure
object is, at best, mixed: GMQ (c) find MY-ratio correlation with P/E; Poterba (c) finds none on 75
years of US stock returns; Liu-Spiegel's M/O relationship (c) failed live, in real time, post-
publication. No paper reviewed here reports a cross-country age-structure-to-equity panel meeting
this program's Tier-A bar (≥30 observations, purged CV) — every serious test is either single-country,
model-calibrated rather than estimated, or (Poterba) a clean null. **Grade: Tier C on the equity-return
translation, converging with DG1's own result rather than contradicting it** — two independent lines
of evidence, this program's own JST test and the published literature, point one direction: the *data*
is excellent, the *equity translation* is not close to tradable.

### A.3 Japan as the master aging story — and China's entry into the same corridor

Japan is the case every demographic-dividend or demographic-doom argument cites, and this program's
clearest instance of the attribution trap A.1 warns against. **What the numbers did.** Japan's working-age (15–64) share peaked at **just under 70% in 1991–93** and has
since fallen to roughly **61%** by the early 2020s — one of the largest, fastest working-age-share
reversals in the developed-world record **[Verified via multiple independent demographic summaries this session]**. **What else happened at the identical moment.**
Japan's equity and real-estate bubble peaked in **December 1989**, collapsing through the early-to-mid
1990s — `debt-deep/partB-cases.md` §6b documents the aftermath: fiat-era public debt/GDP running to
**254% (2020, JST panel)** with **zero points of sustained deleveraging**, the BOJ pinning yields near
zero for three decades, and the Nikkei **price index not reclaiming its 1989 level until February
2021 — 32 years later** (total return recovered faster, still decades: roughly **+1.3%/yr real (yen)**
across 1989–2024 against a **−1.1%/yr real** price return). **The attribution
problem, stated plainly.** Japan's working-age peak (1991–93) and its bubble peak (December 1989) sit
within two years of each other — close enough that no clean test run only on Japan can separate "the
lost decades were a demographic story" from "the lost decades were a balance-sheet-recession story
that happened to coincide with the demographic turn." Both mechanisms plausibly ran the same direction,
which is exactly why n=1 cannot discriminate between them, and why this program declines to. `debt-deep`
§6b's own lesson for L15 is that Japan proves a fiat-era debt ratio can sit above 250% for three
decades with no crisis "provided the debt is overwhelmingly domestically held" — a debt-and-currency
explanation requiring no demographic content at all, which is the honest-attribution posture this file
adopts: Japan is consistent with the demographic-doom story, and *equally* consistent with a pure
balance-sheet-recession story, and this program does not manufacture false resolution between them
(the same Contract §4 discipline `inflation-deep` A.4 applies to its own contested episode).

**China's entry.** China's working-age (15–64) population **peaked around 2013–2015** and has been
declining since **[Verified via multiple IMF/AMRO and demographic summaries this session]**; UN-projection-based estimates put the further decline at roughly **22% between 2022 and
2050** (≈0.9%/yr), a drag some estimates put at **up to half a percentage point off annual GDP growth**
through mid-century **[Verified via AMRO's analytical note and independent summaries this session]**. China entered its aging corridor without Japan's coincidence timing — its
property-sector stress arrived roughly a decade *after* its working-age peak, not two years before —
which if anything makes it a *cleaner* natural experiment for the demographic channel alone, one this
program flags for a dedicated future dossier rather than treats fully here (out of scope for an
India-double-weighted chapter, and Chinese official-statistics access is a separate open question).

### A.4 India — the actual numbers (double weight)

**The working-age share and its peak window.** India's working-age share rises into a peak window
commonly dated to the **mid-2030s to early 2040s**, though the exact year is sensitive to age band
and WPP vintage — a real measurement subtlety, not a rounding triviality. On a 15–59
definition, the share is projected to reach roughly **65–69% around 2036** (World Bank and multiple
WPP-2024-derived estimates); on the Economic Survey 2018–19's own 20–59 definition, the dividend peaks
around **2041, at roughly 59%** **[Verified: both figures via the World Bank's India office, Statista's WPP-2024 series, and the
Economic Survey 2018–19's demographics chapter this session — the two conventions do not reconcile
into one number without restating one series on the other's age band, not attempted here]**.
India's TFR fell **below replacement (2.0 against ~2.1) per NFHS-5 (2019–21)**, down from 2.2 in
NFHS-4 (2015–16) **[Verified via NFHS-5's topline and multiple summaries this session]** — compressing the dividend window relative to older "to 2045–2055" framings, exactly
A.1's point about WPP's systematic direction of miss. India **overtook China as the world's most
populous country around April 2023** per UN estimate **[Verified via UN DESA's policy brief this
session]**.

**State-level heterogeneity — the internal-migration hinge.** National aggregates conceal a two-speed
country. Kerala, Tamil Nadu and Andhra Pradesh are already past their own working-age-share peak,
aging on a trajectory closer to middle-income East Asia than the national figure; Uttar Pradesh, Bihar
and Madhya Pradesh remain young and still rising **[Verified via dossier 07's finding #17,
flagged there for state-level date verification, carried forward unresolved]**. This
is the mechanism by which any national dividend actually flows: Economic Survey 2016–17 used railway
unreserved-passenger-traffic data — a genuine, if indirect, migration proxy — to produce the first
large-scale inter-state work-migration estimate: an **annual average flow of roughly 9 million people
between states, 2011–2016**, well above the ~4 million/year the Census series implied, with a
separate cohort-based method putting 2001–2011 mobility at **5–6.5 million/year**, an inter-state
migrant stock of roughly **60 million**, and inter-district migration as high as **80 million**
**[Verified via the Economic Survey 2016–17's own chapter (indiabudget.gov.in) and PIB's summary]**. The direction matches the aging/young split: labor moves from
UP, Bihar, Rajasthan, MP, Odisha, West Bengal and Assam toward Maharashtra, Delhi-NCR, Gujarat, Tamil
Nadu, Kerala and Karnataka — the young states are the aging ones' effective labor supply, which makes
"India's demographic dividend" an internal-reallocation problem with its own frictions (language,
housing, welfare portability, politics), not a single national clock ticking toward one date.

**PLFS and the jobs-absorption condition.** Bloom-Canning-Sevilla's conditionality (A.2(a)) is not
abstract for India — it is the entire question, and the free data (PLFS, `A-data-catalog.md` H5) now
shows real, if qualified, movement. Female labour-force participation rose from **23.3% (2017–18) to
41.7% (2023–24)** per the PLFS Annual Report [July 2023–June 2024], concentrated in rural areas
(**24.6% → 47.6%**, nearly doubling, versus urban's **47.6% → 52.0%**) **[Verified via
MOSPI's PLFS 2023-24 Annual Report press note and PIB's release this session]**. The honest
qualifier: independent analysis (CEDA, and separate coverage of the underlying microdata) attributes
most of the rural gain to a rise in **self-employment**, not wage or salaried work — precisely the
distinction between counted participation and productive, income-secure employment Bloom-Canning-
Sevilla's conditionality warns against collapsing **[Verified via
CEDA's own published analysis and independent secondary coverage this session]**. Not a reason to
dismiss the PLFS improvement — it is real, large, and directionally the single most important input
the dividend-cashes-or-doesn't question has — but not yet evidence the conditional is *satisfied* at
the quality Bloom-Canning-Sevilla's framework requires.

**What "demographic dividend priced in?" would even mean, testably — and why the design cannot build
that test today.** A testable version of the claim is not "India's working-age share is rising" (known
to every market participant the moment WPP publishes it) but a lead-lag relationship between *realized
jobs-absorption data* — EPFO net payroll additions, PLFS year-on-year formal/wage-employment growth —
and *subsequent* equity returns, with DG1's own null as the pre-registered skeptical prior. Running
that test requires a jobs-absorption series with a short enough lag and long enough history to be a
usable leading input, and the free data does not currently supply one: PLFS is annual with a
multi-quarter lag, EPFO payroll data is higher-frequency but narrow (formal-sector, contribution-
linked coverage only — itself a small, non-representative slice given the composition point below),
and neither series has the ~30-year run this program's Tier-A bar requires. The honest position is
not "the dividend isn't priced in" or "it is" — it is that **the free-data instrument needed to test
the claim does not yet exist at usable frequency and length, a data-availability fact, not a
market-efficiency verdict** — exactly why L16 carries a **2030 design review** rather than a standing
signal or a permanent dismissal: by 2030 PLFS and EPFO will have several more years of history, and the
question can be re-asked on better data rather than resolved by narrative today.

## PART B — What Tier-A data and Tier-C translation permit: the affirmative case for zero allocation authority

### B.1 DG1's own sign-inconsistency is the argument, not an obstacle to one

This program does not need an elaborate theory for why L16 carries zero allocation authority — DG1
supplies it directly, on this program's own data, at this program's own bar. A signal sign-inconsistent
across 4-of-16 countries, median −0.26, flipping sign by era in a pooled 150-year panel is not a weak
version of a real signal; by Contract §4's clock-test logic it has not cleared the bar for *any*
allocation weight, let alone one calibrated to India's specific dividend-window dating. The richer
age-structure literature in A.2 does not rescue the translation — it *replicates* DG1's pattern at
every turn: GMQ's model-implied correlation, uncontaminated by a live falsification test; Liu-Spiegel's
dated forecast, now publicly failed; Poterba's 75-year null standing unrebutted on the demand side.
Three independent lines of evidence — this program's own JST-panel test, the field's most-cited
asset-demand forecast failing on schedule, and the most careful null-result paper in the same
literature — converge on one verdict, which is why DG1 is treated as the empirical spine, not a
caveat to argue around.

### B.2 Three named rejected uses

**(1) Demographic "destiny" allocation tilts.** No rule may set or scale an equity, sector, or
country-mix weight from a working-age-share trajectory, India's own or any comparator's. A.2(e)'s
convergent evidence — DG1's sign-inconsistent pooled result plus the absence of any Tier-A-qualifying
cross-country age-structure-to-equity panel in the reviewed literature — forecloses this outright, the
same way `gold-deep` B.4's arc-timing prohibition and `realrates-deep` B.4's r-star-as-position-input
prohibition foreclose their own adjacent temptations. This also forecloses the mirror-image move for
China and Japan exposure: no rule may underweight either market on an aging-doom read of a channel
that fails to support an overweight read for India (G.2 names the psychology this asymmetry invites).

**(2) MY-ratio (or M/O-ratio) valuation timing.** No signal may condition entries or exits on a
middle-aged-to-young or middle-aged-to-old population ratio, India's or any market's. Liu-Spiegel's
forecast — a specific, dated, model-derived P/E path — is this file's clean, falsified worked example
of exactly this move, on the market (US) with the best available data; India's own age-structure and
market-microstructure data are both thinner, giving no reason to expect a better result from a
strictly harder version of an already-failed test.

**(3) Buying the dividend narrative at index level — the listed-economy-versus-street-economy gap.**
"India's demographic dividend" is a claim about roughly **1.4 billion people and their labor-market
outcomes**; the NIFTY 750 is a claim about ~750 listed companies' earnings. The two are not the same
object, and the gap is measurable, if only roughly, with free data: **only a little more than 10% of
India's workforce is in the formal sector**, informal and agricultural employment together close to
90%, and — on a worker-protections-and-benefits rather than enterprise-type basis — the ILO's more
recent estimate puts India's informal-employment rate at roughly **87% as of 2025** **[Verified: the
~10% formal-sector figure via Data For India's own analysis and multiple summaries this session; the
87% ILO figure via secondary coverage — VERIFY against ILO's primary release]**. A rising working-age share that cashes out
disproportionately into informal, unlisted, or subsistence-agricultural employment — which PLFS's own
self-employment-driven participation gain (A.4) is at minimum consistent with — does not mechanically
translate into listed-sector earnings growth, let alone at a rate an index-level "buy India on
demographics" thesis requires. This is the same composition-gap discipline the credit-cycle work
applies via bank-plus-NBFC aggregation (a partial-coverage proxy misreads the whole); here it
forecloses treating any national demographic statistic as an index-level buy signal without first
establishing — which no reviewed paper does, for India or anywhere else — that the listed universe's
composition tracks the national demographic trend rather than diverging from it.

## PART G — Narrative hazards

### G.1 The dividend story as India's perma-bull crutch

No India-market narrative has been invoked at more valuation peaks, across more cycles, with less
varying content, than "India's demographic dividend." It requires no new information to deploy — the
working-age-share chart looks the same in a cheap market and an expensive one — which is exactly what
makes it a dangerous justification rather than a useful one: a story that costs nothing to tell and
never needs updating is doing narrative work, not analytical work, supplying comfort for a position
taken on other (often momentum or flow) grounds. DG1's sign-inconsistency is the structural reason
this story keeps recurring safely: because no clean, falsifiable, dated test of "the dividend is priced
in" has ever been run at index level (A.4's own admission — the data does not yet exist at usable
frequency), the narrative has never had the chance to be *falsified* the way Liu-Spiegel's or
Mankiw-Weil's forecasts were. An unfalsified narrative is not evidence for the narrative; it is
evidence the test has not been run — cited as context when true, never as a reason inside a sizing
memo.

### G.2 The aging-doom mirror image for China and Japan exposure decisions

The identical hazard runs in reverse for underweighting China or Japan. "China is aging, therefore
avoid it" carries the same DG1-shaped hole as "India is young, therefore buy it" — both treat a
Tier-A demographic fact as though it already implies a Tier-A market conclusion, and A.3's honest read
of Japan (the demographic and bubble peaks sit two years apart, n=1 cannot separate their
contributions) is the standing rebuttal to any confident aging-doom read of *any* single country,
China included. G.4's table applies identically to both directions of this narrative, by design — the
desk should notice if it reaches for a demographic justification only when it happens to support a
position momentum, valuation, or flow signals already favor.

### G.3 The 25-year horizon mismatch with every incentive a desk runs on

India's working-age-share peak window (mid-2030s to early-2040s, A.4) sits 8–19 years from this
monograph's writing date. No one currently on this desk, under this compensation structure, will be
held accountable in 2040 for a demographic call made in 2026 — the cleanest instance in this program
of the horizon mismatch that makes long-wave narratives comfortable to hold and risky to act on: the
payoff, if it arrives, arrives on a clock longer than any plausible tenure or fund-structure horizon,
while the cost of *believing* the story today — a mistimed tilt, a sizing decision leaned on narrative
rather than a tested signal — is paid now, inside the window anyone currently accountable is actually
measured against (Contract §3's drawdown ceiling, §9's out-of-sample discipline). Structurally
identical to `inflation-deep` G.1's doctrine-drift mechanism and `realrates-deep` G.3's countermeasure,
applied to a channel with an even longer clock than either.

### G.4 Desk countermeasures

| Failure mode | Mechanism | Countermeasure |
|---|---|---|
| Dividend-as-crutch at valuation peaks | An unfalsified, cost-free narrative supplies comfort for positions taken on other grounds | Demography never appears in a sizing memo (Contract §6's no-magic-numbers discipline extended to narrative); L16 stays Context-only, registry-enforced, not memory-enforced |

The pattern matches every Part-G section in this program's sibling monographs: the hazard is not
panic, it is patient, well-read overreach — a story with real, Tier-A-grade data underneath it is
exactly what a careful desk talks itself into trading, one reasonable-sounding step at a time. The
countermeasures are structural (a registry-enforced zero-authority seat, a calendared review with a
named test, a symmetry check on the mirror-image error), not a reminder to "stay disciplined": the
discipline this literature requires is exactly the kind that erodes fastest under data this good and a
horizon this long.
