## PART A — Theory: the intellectual history of the debt arc

### A.1 Before the arithmetic — Hume, Smith, Ricardo, and the equivalence that fails

**(i) Mechanism.** Three pre-formal observations set the terms every later model re-derives.
**Hume (1752)**, "Of Public Credit," is the lineage's opening polemic, landing on a stark
either/or — "either the nation must destroy public credit, or public credit will destroy the
nation" — grounded not in arithmetic but in a behavioral claim: public credit is a sound
institution only if the men running it are perfectly rational and predictable, and since they
are neither, the institution's own success (cheap, abundant borrowing) removes the natural
check that would otherwise limit it. **Smith (1776)**, *Wealth of Nations*, Book V, Ch. 3,
supplies the first empirical illustration: Britain's sinking funds — reserves earmarked to
retire wartime debt during peacetime — were "almost always" diverted to other spending once
war ended, so that "the reduction of the public debt in time of peace has never borne any
proportion to its accumulation in time of war" — a two-century-old documentation of exactly
the asymmetric fiscal behavior Reinhart-Rogoff would later call "this-time-is-different"
(A.5). **Ricardo (1821)**, *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*, Ch. 17, is the
equivalence argument's true origin: a government that borrows £1 today to be serviced by
taxes forever after imposes, in present-value terms, an identical burden to taxing £1 today
outright — rational households should be indifferent between the two. Ricardo's own coda is
the detail most retellings drop: having derived the equivalence, he warns against treating it
as license — "it must not be inferred that I consider the system of borrowing as the best
calculated to defray the extraordinary expenses of the state." Ricardo discovered his own
theorem and argued against relying on it, 150 years before Barro gave it a name.

**(ii) Formal structure.** **Barro (1974)**, "Are Government Bonds Net Wealth?," formalizes
Ricardo's argument in an overlapping-generations model linked by operative bequests: a tax
cut of ΔT financed by new debt ΔB=ΔT, to be repaid via future taxes with present value ΔB,
leaves consumption unchanged if households are forward-looking with effectively infinite
planning horizons (via intergenerational altruism), face no borrowing constraints, and pay
lump-sum (non-distortionary) taxes with certainty about who bears them: private saving rises
dollar-for-dollar with the deficit, `ΔS = ΔB`, exactly offsetting the fiscal stimulus. **Why
it fails in practice** — the standard list: liquidity-constrained households cannot borrow
against a future tax liability they will never personally pay, so they consume the tax cut;
most households plan over finite horizons with no operative bequest motive; real-world taxes
are distortionary, not lump-sum, so financing choice changes incentives, not just wealth; and
absent a bequest motive, an OLG economy can shift the burden onto generations not yet born —
the counter-case Diamond (1965) formalized against Barro. **Domar (1944)**, "The Burden of the
Debt and the National Income," *American Economic Review* 34(4) [VERIFY: exact page range],
supplies the arithmetic that survives once Ricardian neutrality is abandoned entirely:
treating debt/GDP purely as a ratio, its evolution depends only on growth relative to the
effective interest rate on the debt, not the deficit's level. In continuous time with no
primary balance, `db/dt = (r−g)·b`: if `r<g`, a constant deficit converges the ratio to a
stable level; if `r>g`, the same deficit diverges it without bound. Domar wrote this about
wartime US debt in 1944, arguing the postwar question was "achieving a growing national
income," not the deficit's size — the direct, 45-years-earlier ancestor of both Blanchard's
2019 argument (A.6) and partDEFH's own D1 identity (`Δb_t=(r_t−g_t)·b_{t−1}−pb_t+sfa_t`).

**(iii) For our seats.** This subsection sets the null hypothesis every later mechanism must
overturn: under exact Ricardian neutrality, a rising debt/GDP trajectory would carry *no*
risk-pricing implication at all, fully absorbed by offsetting private saving — L15 would have
nothing to measure. The entire debt-supercycle literature from here forward is, in effect, a
catalogue of *how far* real economies depart from that neutrality (liquidity constraints,
finite horizons, distortionary taxes, generational cost-shifting) — far enough that fiscal
dominance (A.2) and repression (A.4) become live, measurable phenomena rather than a
theoretical curiosity. Domar's identity is the arithmetic skeleton partDEFH's D1 already
states as fact; this subsection supplies its 75-year prior history and the reason it needed
Ricardian neutrality to fail first.

### A.2 Sargent-Wallace (1981) — "Some Unpleasant Monetarist Arithmetic," in full

**(i) Mechanism.** The paper's own framing: even in a strict "monetarist" world of flexible
prices, clearing markets, and constant velocity — where inflation should be a purely monetary
phenomenon — inflation is, in the long run, a **fiscal** phenomenon. This follows from the
unified government budget constraint plus an **upper bound on the real per-capita stock of
interest-bearing public debt** the private sector is willing to hold. Sargent-Wallace model
fiscal and monetary authorities as a **two-move Stackelberg game**: the **fiscal authority
moves first, as leader** — its deficit path is independent of, and unresponsive to, monetary
policy; the **monetary authority moves second, as follower**, constrained to satisfy the
budget constraint given whatever deficit path it inherits. This one assumption ("fiscal
dominance") drives the whole result.

**(ii) Formal structure — symbols defined.** Budget constraint each period:
`D_t + r·B_{t−1} = ΔB_t + ΔM_t/P_t`, where `D_t` = real primary deficit (fiscally set,
exogenous), `B_t` = real interest-bearing debt stock, `M_t` = base money, `P_t` = price level,
`r` = real bond yield, `g` = real growth. Suppose the monetary authority holds `ΔM_t/P_t`
(seigniorage) low for `t=1…T` — "tight money now." At an unchanged deficit path, the shortfall
must be bond-financed: `ΔB_t` rises, so `B_t` grows faster than under loose money. **The
critical condition is `r>g`**: the real per-capita bond stock `b_t=B_t/N_t` then grows
explosively relative to income, since interest compounds on a principal itself financed by
more borrowing — and private bond demand has a plausible upper bound `b*`. **Once `b_t`
reaches `b*`, bond financing is exhausted**: from `T` onward the *entire* remaining deficit
plus accumulated interest on the now-larger stock must be seigniorage-financed, so `ΔM_t/P_t`
jumps to a **permanently higher** level than under loose money from the start — and because
that stock compounded at `r>g` in the interim, the eventual required money growth, and
inflation, can be **higher**, not lower. The "unpleasant" conclusion: under fiscal dominance
and `r>g`, tighter money now causes more inflation later.

**(iii) For our seats.** The result is **explicitly conditional on `r>g`** — this is the load-
bearing link to partDEFH's own D1/D3 regime classification. Sargent-Wallace's forcing
mechanism operates precisely in the **liberalization-era arithmetic** (DS3: `r−g=+1.1pp`,
`r<g` only 37% of years, 1981–2007) — the regime where a constant deficit path genuinely
diverges the debt ratio and eventually forces monetization under fiscal dominance. In the
**repression-era or post-GFC arithmetic** (DS3: `−4.3pp` and `−1.0pp` respectively, `r<g`
73–83% of years), the identical Stackelberg logic does **not** force monetization — a debt
ratio can stabilize or fall *without* the eventual-seigniorage mechanism ever triggering,
because `b_t` need not grow explosively relative to income even under a persistent primary
deficit. This is the precise theoretical reason L15's real-rate-persistence gauge (partDEFH
Step 2) and debt-trajectory percentile (Step 1) must be read **jointly**, never separately:
Sargent-Wallace names the exact condition — a return to `r>g` under an unreformed, fiscally
dominant deficit path — under which the mechanism reactivates, and the 2022–23 global rate
spike (A.6) is the live test of whether that condition is returning.

**(iv) Citations.** Sargent, Thomas J. & Wallace, Neil (1981), "Some Unpleasant Monetarist
Arithmetic," *Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis Quarterly Review* 5(3):1–17 **[Verified]**.

### A.3 The Fiscal Theory of the Price Level — Leeper, Sims, Cochrane

**(i) Mechanism.** The FTPL takes the same government budget constraint A.2 used as a
*constraint* and, iterated forward under a transversality condition, reinterprets it as a
**valuation equation for money and debt**: the real value of outstanding nominal government
liabilities must equal the present discounted value of all future real primary surpluses —
formally identical to a stock's price equaling the present value of its expected dividends.
**Leeper (1991)** supplies the taxonomy that makes this rigorous: monetary policy is
**"active"** if the policy rate responds aggressively to inflation (feedback coefficient >1,
ignoring debt sustainability) and **"passive"** otherwise; fiscal policy is symmetrically
**"active"** if the surplus path is set independent of debt or prices, **"passive"** if
surpluses adjust to stabilize debt/GDP for *any* price level. Using the Blanchard-Kahn (1980)
determinacy condition, Leeper shows determinacy requires **exactly one** authority active:
**active monetary/passive fiscal** ("regime M") is the conventional monetarist world; **active
fiscal/passive monetary** ("regime F") is the FTPL regime, where the price level itself becomes
the variable that adjusts to make the valuation equation hold.

**(ii) Formal structure — symbols defined.** Real government budget constraint, iterated
forward: `B_{t−1}/P_t = Σ_{j=0}^∞ E_t[s_{t+j}/R_{t,t+j}]`, where `B_{t−1}` = nominal debt
inherited from last period (predetermined), `P_t` = today's price level, `s` = real primary
surplus, `R` = cumulative real discount factor. Under regime F, the right-hand side is fixed
by fiscal policy independent of `P_t` or the debt stock; the equation must still hold in
equilibrium, so `P_t` becomes the unique variable that adjusts to make the identity true.
**Sims (1994)** builds the minimal representative-agent, transactions-cost-money version of
exactly this determinacy result. **Cochrane's (2023)** book generalizes the construction into a
full New-Keynesian-compatible framework and derives predictions conventional theory does not:
e.g., inflation can *fall* during a recession despite rising deficits, because a recession
lowers discount rates, which — for a given nominal debt stock — raises the present value of
expected future surpluses, satisfiable only by a **lower** price level, the opposite of the
naive "deficits cause inflation" intuition.

**(iii) The honest state of the empirical debate.** FTPL is genuinely contested, not settled.
Identification is hard because testing which regime (active-fiscal or active-monetary)
prevailed historically requires assumptions about the very policy rules and regime in question
— a textbook joint-hypothesis problem. Proponents point to episodes (e.g., attributing part
of 2021–2023 US inflation to pandemic-era deficit-driven fiscal expansion interacting with a
surplus path markets read as not fully credible) and to Cochrane's recession-disinflation
prediction as evidence; skeptics — a substantial share of mainstream monetary economists —
argue the fiscal channel, while theoretically elegant, has weak independent empirical
identification against conventional demand-channel monetary transmission, and that most
advanced-economy fiscal authorities have in fact behaved "passively" (adjusting surpluses to
stabilize debt) for most of the postwar sample, consistent with ordinary monetary dominance
rather than FTPL. This monograph takes no side; both readings remain live.

**(iv) For our seats.** FTPL is the cleanest modern formal expression of what Sargent-Wallace
argued informally: when debt is large and fiscal policy is "active" (will not move to
stabilize it), the price level — inflation — becomes the slack variable that revalues debt
down in real terms. L15 is built to detect the **preconditions** consistent with markets
already pricing an active-fiscal, FTPL-adjacent path — rising debt trajectory jointly with
persistent negative real rates is precisely that observable signature — never to identify
which regime (F or M) is actually in force, or to predict when one gives way to the other.
This is exactly why the seat is Tier C, reduce-only, state-classification-only (partDEFH D3):
regime identification is not something a real-time indicator can cleanly resolve even in
principle, per the empirical debate above, so the design does not pretend otherwise.

**(v) Citations.** Leeper, Eric M. (1991), "Equilibria under 'Active' and 'Passive' Monetary
and Fiscal Policies," *Journal of Monetary Economics* 27(1):129–147 **[Verified]**. Sims,
Christopher A. (1994), "A Simple Model for Study of the Determination of the Price Level and

### A.4 Financial repression mechanics — McKinnon-Shaw and the Reinhart-Sbrancia liquidation math

**(i) Mechanism.** **McKinnon (1973)**, *Money and Capital in Economic Development*
(Brookings), and **Shaw (1973)**, *Financial Deepening in Economic Development* (Oxford),
independently coined **"financial repression"** — deliberately pejorative, echoing "political
repression" — for a policy package observed across developing economies in the 1960s–70s:
interest-rate ceilings on deposits/lending (often below inflation), high statutory reserve/
liquidity requirements forcing banks into low-yielding government paper, capital controls
against exit to foreign assets, and directed-credit rules. Together these manufacture a
**captive domestic buyer base** the sovereign can borrow from at below-market, often negative
real, rates — a hidden, unlegislated tax on savers, collected through rate suppression and
inflation rather than the tax code.

**(ii) Formal structure — the liquidation rate.** **Reinhart & Sbrancia (2011/2015)**, "The
Liquidation of Government Debt," formalize the tax: the ex-post real return on government
debt each period is `r_real,t = i_t − π_t` (nominal rate minus realized inflation). When
`r_real,t<0`, the real value of the debt shrinks automatically; the annual **liquidation flow**
≈ `−r_real,t · (B_t/Y_t)`, a direct share of GDP. Advanced-economy real short rates were
**negative in roughly half of 1945–1980 country-years** — matching this program's own DS2
replication almost exactly (44%, same window) — and the **US and UK liquidated roughly 3–4% of
GDP per year** across 1945–1980 via this channel; **Australia and Italy, with higher
inflation, closer to 5%/year**. Uncompounded, a 3–4%/year flow accumulates to **30–40% of GDP
over a decade** — the order of magnitude behind several of DS1's completed post-1971
deleveragings. Their sample also covers a 13-country emerging-market group including
**India**: India's own 1949–1980 share of negative-real-rate years was **53%**, slightly
*above* the advanced-economy average — direct evidence India ran its own captive-market
repression episode across exactly the period its SLR apparatus reached its historical maximum
(roughly 38.5% by the early 1990s [VERIFY: precise peak year/figure], before the Narasimham
Committee (1991) recommended a glide to 25%; SLR stands at 18% today, per an April-2020 cut
from 19% [VERIFY: primary-source confirmation]).

**(iii) For our seats.** DS2's headline figures (44% share 1945–80, 76% post-GFC) *are* the
Reinhart-Sbrancia replication already computed in-house (`jst-debt-RESULTS.md`); this
subsection supplies the mechanism, the historical liquidation-tax magnitude (3–5%/yr for
advanced economies), and — critically — the **captive-audience apparatus checklist** (rate
ceilings, reserve/liquidity mandates, capital controls) that L15's own captivity/composition
input (partDEFH Step 3: SLR trajectory, CB gold buying, COFER USD drift) is explicitly
modeling. India's SLR is not an *analogue* of the 1945–80 developed-world apparatus; it is
**the same mechanism, still standing**, only shrunk from 38.5% to 18% — precisely the reason
(per partDEFH D2) this seat exists for an Indian book at all, and precisely the fact G.4 below
returns to as an institutional-psychology risk in its own right.

### A.5 Reinhart-Rogoff — the eight-century chronology, the 90% threshold, and Herndon-Ash-Pollin as both substance and method

**(i) Mechanism.** **Reinhart & Rogoff (2009)**, *This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of
Financial Folly*, assembles a crisis chronology across 66 countries and roughly eight
centuries covering sovereign default, banking crises, currency crashes, and inflation crises.
Two large-sample, well-replicated findings: **serial default** ("debt intolerance") — most
sovereigns that default once do so again across generations — and the **"this-time-is-
different" syndrome** — the recurring belief, immediately before each crisis, that improved
institutions or policy make historical debt limits inapplicable (Smith's own sinking-fund
observation, A.1, is a two-century-earlier instance of the identical pattern). This chronology
is genuinely Tier A/B on its own observation count — dozens of countries, hundreds of
crisis-years.

**(ii) The narrower claim and its correction — substance and a methods parable.** **Reinhart &
Rogoff (2010)**, "Growth in a Time of Debt," is the much narrower, far more famous companion:
public debt/GDP above roughly 90% associated with a sharp drop in median GDP growth.
**Herndon, Ash & Pollin (2013)** found three compounding problems in the 2010 paper's
Excel-based calculation: **(a)** a spreadsheet coding error accidentally excluding five
countries (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark) from the >90%-debt bucket average;
**(b)** selective exclusion of high-debt/high-growth country-years (notably early postwar
Australia/New Zealand/Canada, high wartime debt with strong growth); **(c)** an unconventional
equal-country weighting scheme letting one anomalous country-year (New Zealand, one sharply-
negative-growth year) carry the same weight as another country's twelve-plus years of ~2%
growth. Correcting all three, average growth in the >90% bucket flips from the reported
"cliff" to **positive, roughly +2.2%** — already this program's own canon (`docs/
CYCLE_ATLAS.md`'s exclusion list).

**(iii) For our seats — why this design uses percentile states, never threshold cliffs.**
HAP is *both* substance and method. As **substance**: the 90% threshold is discredited and
must never re-enter the design as a parameter (CONTRACT §6's "no magic numbers" ban, already
enforced by the atlas's exclusion). As **method**: HAP is the clearest documented case here of
how a handful of country-years' weighting and inclusion can flip a long-run panel's headline
sign — and a fitted *threshold* (a round, discontinuous cutoff like "90%") is exactly the
construct most exposed to that failure, since its output changes discontinuously with which
observations sit near the cutoff, while a **percentile-rank state** — L15's actual debt-
trajectory input (partDEFH Step 1: "debt/GDP expanding percentile + 5y slope sign") — degrades
gracefully as any single country-year is added or revised. That input's percentile-not-
threshold construction is therefore not merely compliant with CONTRACT §6; it is
*specifically* the design HAP's correction argues for, recorded here explicitly for the first
time.

### A.6 r<g as the historical norm, its limits, and the honest counterpoint

**(i) Mechanism.** **Blanchard's (2019)** AEA presidential address, "Public Debt and Low
Interest Rates," argues that a safe real rate below the growth rate (`r<g`) is the
**historical norm** for the US, not a post-GFC aberration — and that under `r<g`, debt
rollover (issuing new debt to service old, with no future tax increase ever strictly required)
may be **fiscally costless**. Even so, Blanchard shows public debt still crowds out capital
accumulation and can carry a welfare cost — but a smaller one than usually assumed, because
the safe rate is the *risk-adjusted* return to capital, and if it sits below the growth rate,
that itself signals capital's risk-adjusted return is low. This is Domar's (A.1) 1944
arithmetic given a full modern welfare treatment 75 years later.

**(ii) Formal structure.** The same `db/dt=(r−g)·b` identity: Blanchard's contribution is
demonstrating that the US `r<g` condition has held for *most* of the historical sample, not
merely the 2010s — while stating plainly, as a condition of his own argument, that everything
reverses if `r>g` resumes.

**(iii) The immediate counterpoint.** The same global tightening cycle that resolved
2022–2023 inflation pushed borrowing costs above nominal growth in several advanced economies
within roughly three years of Blanchard's address — DS3's own post-GFC mean (`r−g=−1.0pp`,
`r<g` in 73% of years) is precisely the regime Blanchard describes, but the atlas's own JST
data (partDEFH D1/D3) treats this as **era-conditional, not permanent**: 2022–23 is a live
demonstration that `r−g` can flip sign within a couple of years even after decades of
one-directional evidence — the same interruption Schmelzing's own 700-year "suprasecular
decline" thesis suffered within two years of its 2020 publication (dossier 07's own worked
illustration of long-wave timing uncertainty).

**(iv) Mauro-Zhou — "not really."** **Mauro & Zhou (2021)**, "r−g<0: Can We Sleep More
Soundly?," use average effective sovereign borrowing costs across 55 countries and up to 200
years of data. Negative `r−g` differentials prevail for both advanced and emerging economies —
but several sovereign **default** episodes have followed periods of negative `r−g`, and
`r−g` differentials are **no higher** before a default than in normal times; instead,
**marginal** government borrowing costs (the rate on *new* issuance, distinct from the
*average* effective rate on the outstanding stock) often spike sharply just before default.
Their own answer to their title's question: **"not really."**

**(v) For our seats.** This is the single most important honesty-check for the seat's premise.
Blanchard's "may have no fiscal cost" argument is the closest thing in this literature to a
case for complacency about debt; Mauro-Zhou is the direct empirical rebuttal — a comfortable
*average* `r−g` reading gives no forward guarantee against a sudden *marginal*-rate shock
precisely when debt is highest, since the two coexist right up to a default. L15 already
reflects this: the debt-trajectory input stays a level+slope *percentile* (never an "`r<g`, so
we're fine" binary), and the seat's output is reduce-only insurance, never a green light to add
leverage when `r<g` looks comfortable.

### A.7 Dalio's big-debt-cycle framework — a practitioner synthesis, epistemics stated

**(i) Mechanism.** **Dalio (2018)**, *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises*, is an
explicitly **practitioner** synthesis pattern-matched across roughly four dozen historical
debt crises spanning gold-standard defaults, fiat-era inflations, and wartime repressions —
heterogeneous episodes pooled under one taxonomy, not a formal statistical panel. Dalio names
**four levers** policymakers pull to bring debt and debt-service down relative to income:
**(1) austerity** (spending cuts/tax rises — deflationary, rarely sufficient alone);
**(2) default/restructuring** (a discontinuous write-down — deflationary, redistributes losses
onto creditors); **(3) money-printing/asset purchases** by the central bank (inflationary,
eases the real burden — the modern QE-era lever, and financial repression's own channel, A.4);
**(4) redistribution/transfers** (wealth taxes, or repression's captive-lending mechanism
viewed as an implicit transfer). A **"beautiful deleveraging"** balances the four so that
debt/GDP falls with positive growth and acceptable inflation; an **"ugly" deleveraging** is
unbalanced — either deflationary (excess austerity/default, historically a 50–90% stock-market
decline range in Dalio's own accounting) or inflationary (excess printing, the Cagan boundary,
A.8).

**(ii) Formal structure.** There is no formal model beyond the four-lever accounting
identity itself — essentially a decomposition of partDEFH's own `Δb=(r−g)b−pb+sfa` by *who
bears each term*: austerity raises `pb`; default cuts `b` discontinuously (a haircut, or
`sfa`); printing lowers `r` (often deeply negative, A.4's own mechanism) and/or lets the price
level revalue debt (the FTPL-consistent channel, A.3); redistribution operates through
transfer-like `sfa` terms or an off-balance-sheet default on one specific group (savers, via
A.4).

**(iii) For our seats — epistemics stated plainly.** Dalio's framework is used here **purely
as an organizing taxonomy** for classifying which lever(s) a fiscal-dominance episode is
actually using once one is underway — never as a forecasting or timing tool. Its status is
identical to the atlas's own treatment of Dalio's separate reserve-currency "Big Cycle"
framework in dossier 07 (n≈3 completed cycles, Tier C, pattern-matched narrative, no
independent return-signal authority): here the four-lever taxonomy covers a larger but still
heterogeneous set of episodes, same caution applies. Its use inside this seat is entirely
descriptive — the quarterly policy-review memo (partDEFH Part F: "the r−g regime
classification, quarterly, to the humans") may report *which lever(s) appear in use*, but this
carries **zero allocation authority** of its own; DS1–DS4's pooled JST statistics, not Dalio's
taxonomy, do all of the seat's actual sizing.

**(iv) Citation.** Dalio, Ray (2018), *Principles for Navigating Big Debt Crises*, Bridgewater
Associates (self-published; also excerpted free at principles.com) **[Verified]**.

### A.8 The boundary case — Cagan (1956) and why repression and hyperinflation are different attractors

**(i) Mechanism.** **Cagan (1956)**, "The Monetary Dynamics of Hyperinflation," defines
hyperinflation operationally as beginning the month inflation first exceeds 50%/month (ending
when it falls below that rate and stays there for at least a year) and builds the seminal
adaptive-expectations money-demand model explaining why, once inflation crosses a threshold
region, the dynamics can turn genuinely **non-linear** rather than a smooth continuation of
moderate inflation.

**(ii) Formal structure — symbols defined.** Demand for real balances: `log(M_t/P_t) =
−α·π_t^e` (expected inflation `π_t^e` depresses real balances as agents economize on cash
whose value it erodes), with adaptive expectations `π_t^e = π_{t−1}^e + β(π_t − π_{t−1}^e)`.
The government targets a real seigniorage flow `S=ΔM/P` to finance its deficit. Because higher
expected inflation *itself* shrinks the real base being taxed (`M/P` falls as `π^e` rises),
there is a Laffer-curve-like **maximum sustainable seigniorage rate**: past some inflation
rate, further money-supply growth yields *less*, not more, real revenue, forcing ever-faster
nominal money growth just to hold real revenue constant — a self-reinforcing spiral, not the
smooth, linear liquidation-rate arithmetic of A.4.

**(iii) For our seats — the two attractors.** Advanced-economy financial repression (A.4,
DS2) is a **linear, capped** process: a few percent of GDP liquidated per year, sustained for
decades, precisely because the captive-audience apparatus (rate ceilings, reserve mandates,
capital controls) keeps velocity from spiking — the debt melts **slowly**. EM/Cagan-style
hyperinflation is the **other attractor**: the captive-audience apparatus fails or was never
built, velocity escapes control, and the *same* underlying arithmetic pressure — a government
financing a gap ordinary taxation and borrowing cannot cover — resolves in months, via a
non-linear inflation spiral, not a decades-long tax. This is precisely why L15's expression is
the *slow*-debasement tail budget (0.3–0.6%/yr NAV, sized to be carried indefinitely, per
partDEFH D4/Step 5) and never an EM-hyperinflation-style fast-crash hedge: India's own
post-1991 apparatus (SLR, capital controls, inflation-targeting since 2016) sits, and has
always sat, in the *first* attractor, not the second. Stating the Cagan boundary explicitly is
what lets this monograph say plainly that the seat currently prices **zero** probability of
the second attractor into its budget, as a stated, revisitable design choice — not an
oversight buried in the arithmetic.

**(iv) Citation.** Cagan, Phillip (1956), "The Monetary Dynamics of Hyperinflation," in Milton
Friedman (ed.), *Studies in the Quantity Theory of Money*, University of Chicago Press
**[Verified]**.

### A.9 Modern instruments — QE, yield-curve control, and CBDC captivity

**(i) QE as a duration swap.** On a **consolidated** government balance sheet (Treasury plus
central bank as one entity), large-scale asset purchases are, in substance, a **duration
swap**: long-dated, fixed-rate bonds held by the private sector are retired and replaced with
central-bank reserves. **Greenwood, Hanson & Stein (2016)**, "The Federal Reserve's Balance
Sheet as a Financial-Stability Tool" (Kansas City Fed, Jackson Hole), describe reserves as
functionally closer to **"infinite-maturity floating-rate notes"** than to fixed-maturity
bills — QE shortens the consolidated government's effective liability duration and floats its
funding cost to the policy rate, identical in substance to a debt-management office issuing
bills instead of bonds, just executed through the central bank's balance sheet. This
suppresses the term premium (lowers `r` along the curve) while making the interest bill more
policy-rate-sensitive — continuous with A.4's captive-audience apparatus, not a
consequence-free choice.

**(ii) Yield-curve control — Japan's endurance, Australia's failure.** YCC makes the swap
explicit and pre-committed: the central bank targets a **yield**, not a quantity, and defends
it without limit. The **Bank of Japan** ran YCC from **September 2016** (10-year JGB near
zero, −0.1% policy rate) until formally ending it on **19 March 2024** — the longest sustained
developed-market YCC episode on record. The **Reserve Bank of Australia** is the counter-
instance: a three-year bond-yield target from **March 2020** (~0.25%, lowered to ~0.10% in
November 2020), held over a year, then **abandoned without prior announcement in late
October/November 2021** as inflation expectations shifted and the market tested the peg — the
RBA's own review conceded "reputational damage." The lesson: YCC works while the inflation
regime cooperates and fails **abruptly, not gradually**, the moment expectations turn against
it — a credibility-contingent device, not a free lunch, the same regime-dependence A.6 flagged
for `r<g` itself.

**(iii) CBDC — captivity's prospective frontier.** A retail or wholesale CBDC would give a
central bank direct, **programmable** control over the unit of account — holding limits,
expiry, routing conditions embedded in the money itself, rather than A.4's *indirect*
apparatus (rate ceilings, reserve mandates, capital controls on intermediaries). This is
**evidence-thin and forward-looking**: no advanced economy has deployed a repression-
configured CBDC at scale; live CBDCs (Bahamas, Jamaica, Nigeria) are small-scale, financial-
inclusion instruments. The mechanism is nonetheless a strict superset of A.4's apparatus —
what McKinnon-Shaw's package achieves through banking regulation, a programmable CBDC could
achieve directly (e.g., a negative rate applied only above a balance threshold, without an
intermediary to arbitrage it). India's own Digital Rupee (e₹) pilots (launched 2022) are the
domestic instance to monitor — not yet evidence of anything beyond a payments experiment.

**(iv) For our seats.** None of the three changes the arithmetic of `Δb=(r−g)b−pb+sfa`; each
is a **delivery mechanism** for the same rate-suppression or debt-revaluation channels A.2–A.4
already established, observable through the identical free proxies L15 already tracks. This
subsection adds no new numeric input to L15 — what it adds is the honest record, carried in
the qualitative policy-review memo (partDEFH Part F), that the delivery mechanism has
broadened from banking-regulation-based repression toward balance-sheet and, prospectively,
code-based repression.

**(v) Citations.** Greenwood, Robin; Hanson, Samuel G. & Stein, Jeremy C. (2016), "The Federal
Reserve's Balance Sheet as a Financial-Stability Tool," Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City

September 2016 – 19 March 2024 **[Verified]**. Reserve Bank of Australia three-year yield

## PART G — Operator psychology

Part A documents a genuinely two-sided theoretical inheritance: real mechanisms (Sargent-
Wallace, FTPL, McKinnon-Shaw/Reinhart-Sbrancia) that argue debt matters enormously, sitting
beside equally real honesty-checks (Ricardian critiques, Mauro-Zhou, HAP) that argue against
treating any of it as license for a large, confident bet in either direction. This Part maps
the operator failure modes that inheritance invites — normalizing an ongoing wealth transfer
until it stops registering at all, or converting a well-evidenced insurance floor into a
conviction trade — to the countermeasures this program's governance layer already carries.

### G.1 The frog-boil — money illusion and bondholder complacency

**Mechanism.** **Shafir, Diamond & Tversky (1997)**, "Money Illusion," document a durable,
replicated bias: people systematically evaluate financial outcomes in **nominal** terms even
when the real, inflation-adjusted terms are what matters — their survey evidence shows
subjects judging a nominal raise during high inflation as a "gain" even when it is a real pay
*cut*, and a nominal freeze during deflation as a "loss" even when it is a real *gain*. Applied
to this seat: a holder of government debt earning a **negative real return** during a
repression episode does not necessarily *experience* it as a loss if the nominal coupon is
comfortably positive — Indian banks holding SLR-mandated G-Secs through the 2000s–early-2010s
earned positive nominal yields even in years the CPI-adjusted real return was negative (A.4),
and money illusion is exactly the mechanism by which this never registers internally as the
wealth transfer it mechanically is. Because repression is *by design* gradual — a few percent
of GDP per year (A.4's own liquidation-rate magnitude), never a single shock — and because
money illusion masks the erosion in nominal terms, an operator can spend years treating "the
real rate is negative" as background noise rather than as the state-defining fact it is:
normalizing a slow rise the way an organism that would visibly react to a sudden shock of the
same eventual magnitude fails to react to it delivered slowly.

**Countermeasure.** L15's real-rate-persistence gauge (partDEFH Step 2: rolling 36-month
share of trailing months with negative real rates) is explicitly a **mechanical, computed**
series — never a judgment call — precisely so the frog-boil bias has nothing to attach to: it
does not ask the operator "does this feel concerning yet," it computes the share and feeds the
phase object (0.6U/0.6D notation, per the principal's 2026-09-01 phase-object decision)
regardless of how comfortable the nominal numbers look. The fiscal-dominance dummy (Step 4)
fires on the same computed state, not on a narrative "things still feel fine" read — the
structural answer to a bias whose defining feature is that the biased party cannot detect it
through introspection.

### G.2 The doom cult — perma-bear capitulation into all-gold, and why DS4 forecloses it

**Mechanism.** The mirror-image failure is, for a Tier-C, narrative-adjacent seat like this
one, arguably the more dangerous of the two — precisely *because* the underlying story (debt
supercycle, fiscal dominance, currency debasement) is a genuinely compelling narrative with
real mechanism behind it (A.1–A.8 are not fabricated), it seduces an operator into treating
the *seat's existence* as license for a portfolio-level conviction call: rotate hard into gold
and hard assets, treat equities as a currency-denominated "melting ice cube," and size the
"insurance" so large it becomes the dominant bet rather than a floor. **The seat must never
become a gold-bug thesis** — and the evidence against it sits inside this monograph's own
already-established data. **DS4** (`jst-debt-RESULTS.md`) shows real equity returns in the
fiscal-dominance state (high debt AND negative real rates) average **+4.5%/yr** (median
+3.4%, n=196) — **halved** relative to the "normal" high-debt/positive-real-rate state
(+10.0%), but firmly **positive**. A perma-bear exiting equities entirely into gold on the
*strength* of this narrative trades away a well-evidenced positive expected real return for an
asset whose own very-long-run real return is close to flat: **Erb & Harvey (2013)**, "The
Golden Dilemma," find gold's long-run real return approximately zero absent century-scale
horizons, with its real price itself mean-reverting — richly priced gold has historically
predicted *below-average* subsequent real returns. DS4's table is arguably the single most
load-bearing number in this seat: repression halves equity returns; it does not reverse their
sign.

**Countermeasure.** The reduce-only clamp (CONTRACT §4's Tier-C rule; partDEFH Step 5: "ALL
reduce-only vs the frozen gold cap (≤50%), never a timing trade, never an equity exit") is the
**structural** answer — L15 cannot exceed its pre-registered floor-plus-tail-budget band
(gold-floor attribution 0.40–0.50 of the existing floor, tail budget 0.3–0.6%/yr NAV,
conditional lift capped at +1–2pp, per `docs/DESIGN.md` §4.3) no matter how compelling the
narrative feels. No path exists by which conviction in the debt-supercycle thesis can push
gold toward its 50% mandate ceiling or trigger an equity exit, because the registry's own
validator (CONTRACT §10: "Tier-C signals may only REDUCE risk... a registry violating its own
budget must fail to load") enforces the cap in code, not discretion — the structural mirror of
value-deep's own anti-capitulation lock, running in the opposite direction: that lock stops a
sleeve from being *abandoned* at its cheapest; this stops one from being *expanded* at its most
compelling.

### G.3 Narrative capture by fiscal doom media cycles

**Mechanism.** Fiscal-doom narratives — "the debt crisis is imminent," "the rupee/dollar is
about to collapse," "hyperinflation is coming" — recur in financial media on a cadence that
has essentially nothing to do with the r−g arithmetic or the repression gauge; they correlate
with salience (a weak bond auction, a rating-agency headline, a viral thread) far more than
with the computed state. Structurally this is a **perpetual** narrative: since the debt
supercycle genuinely has not resolved for 14 of 18 JST countries (DS1: only 4 of 18 completed
a lasting ≥30pp deleveraging), "the crisis is imminent" can be told, plausibly, in nearly every
year of the sample without ever being falsified inside an operator's career — a stopped clock
right once or twice a lifetime, while media incentives reward *telling* the story far more
often than it resolves. The failure mode: attention to the fiscal-dominance state driven by
media salience rather than the computed phase object reacts to noise at news-cycle frequency,
not the multi-quarter cadence this seat runs at.

**Countermeasure.** The quarterly refresh cadence (partDEFH Step 5/MONITOR) is itself the
disciplining device — L15 is deliberately **not** a fast-layer input (contrast L2's vol/
funding triggers, which genuinely do react that fast); its τ½ (120m+, `docs/DESIGN.md`'s L15
row) means the *state* should move meaningfully only a few times per decade, so any narrative
urgency arriving faster is, by construction, arriving from the media cycle, not the mechanism
this monograph documents. The Stage-2 red-team channel (CONTRACT §10: "tactical thesis/buy-call
generator... human-vetoed, logged, Brier-scored") is the explicit quarantine for this: partDEFH
Part F already assigns "Perez/fiscal narratives quarantined here — Stage-1 sees only the
observables." This dossier documents *why* that line exists: the fiscal-doom narrative is real
and evidence-adjacent, and almost perfectly uncorrelated in timing with the state this seat
tracks.

### G.4 Institutional captivity acceptance — the Indian SLR framing

**Mechanism.** The reverse of G.2/G.3 in a different sense — not a trading bias but an
**institutional** one, specific to operating this seat in India. Because SLR-driven captive
G-Sec demand is a decades-old, legally mandated feature of every Indian bank's balance sheet —
not a crisis-era emergency measure the way US QE/YCC were publicly framed — the desk risks
treating India's own repression apparatus as simply "how the bond market works here":
background furniture, not a live wealth transfer with a computable magnitude (A.4/DS2). The
failure mode is **acceptance-as-normal**: because the apparatus is old, legal, and universal —
every bank holds SLR-mandated G-Secs, none treats it as remarkable — the operator can lose the
ability to see it *as* repression at all, precisely McKinnon-Shaw's own naming problem: such
policies present as prudential regulation, never as repression.

**Countermeasure.** L15's captivity/composition input (partDEFH Step 3: SLR trajectory as a
first-class, time-varying series, not a fixed constant) is the structural answer — SLR is
modeled across its full 38.5%-peak-to-18%-today history so that a *change* in captivity
registers as a movement in the seat's state, never an invisible constant assumed away. This
mirrors dossier 07's treatment of the analogous WGC/COFER inputs: India's SLR trajectory is
logged on the same footing as the more visible real-rate and debt-trajectory gauges,
specifically so institutional familiarity cannot let one input quietly go unwatched.

## B1. The resolution menu, with the panel numbers

The debt identity from Part D (Δb = (r−g)·b − pb + sfa) has exactly five channels through which a
sovereign has ever brought b down for good: **growth**, **primary surpluses**, **repression/inflation**,
**default/restructuring**, and **wealth levies**. History's verdict on how often each channel is
actually used, and how large its effect is when it works, is now reasonably well quantified.

### 1. Growth

Growth reduces b for free whenever r<g — DS3 already establishes that this was the operative
arithmetic for **83% of country-years in the 1945–80 repression era** and **73% of country-years
post-GFC (2008–2020)**, versus only **37–41%** of years in the two liberalization-era regimes
(1870–1944 gold/interwar; 1981–2007). Growth alone, however, is the weakest evidenced channel in
isolation: every episode below in which growth gets credit for delevering (UK 1815–1914, the "growth"
half of the US and UK WWII stories) turns out on closer inspection to have been growth **combined**
with negative real rates, primary surpluses, or both — a pattern **Acalin & Ball (2024, NBER WP 31577
/ IMF WP 2024/005, "Did the U.S. Really Grow Out of Its World War II Debt?")** make explicit for the
canonical growth case: their counterfactual removing surprise inflation and the 1942–51 rate peg shows
US debt/GDP would have fallen only to **74% by 1974**, not the actual **23%** — i.e. growth and primary
surpluses on their own explain roughly two-fifths of the historical decline, and the rest is
repression. "Growing out of it" alone is close to a myth even in its best-documented instance.

### 2. Primary surpluses

**Eichengreen & Panizza (2014), "A Surplus of Ambition: Can Europe Rely on Large Primary Surpluses to
Solve its Debt Problem?"** (*Economic Policy* 31(85), NBER WP 20316) is the definitive rarity study.
Across **54 advanced and emerging economies, 1974–2013** (235 five-year country-periods), they find:
- primary surpluses of **≥3% of GDP sustained for ≥5 years**: **36 episodes**, or **15%** of all
  five-year periods;
- the same **≥3% of GDP threshold sustained for ≥10 years**: only **12 instances** in the entire
  sample;
- surpluses of **≥4% of GDP sustained for ≥10 years**: "extremely rare" — effectively a handful of
  cases, always under exceptional circumstances (oil windfalls, post-crisis IMF programs, currency-
  union entry requirements);
- their own verdict on the general policy prescription: **no historical episode** shows a
  debt-stabilizing surplus **above roughly 3–3.5% of GDP for a prolonged period** without
  extraordinary, non-repeatable conditions attached.
The primary-surplus channel is real (it is exactly what delevers Belgium, Italy's 1990s, and Ireland
below) but it is the rarest of the five channels to sustain, and never sustainable at the magnitude
(3–5% of GDP for a decade) that would resolve a >100%-of-GDP debt stock on its own.

### 3. Financial repression and inflation

**Reinhart & Sbrancia (2011), "The Liquidation of Government Debt"** (NBER WP 16893; also IMF WP
2015/07) is the direct ancestor of DS2. Their 12-advanced-economy sample finds average annual
"liquidation" — the fiscal saving from negative real interest rates on the domestic-currency debt
stock — of **1–5% of GDP per year over 1945–80**, with the **US and UK specifically averaging 3–4% of
GDP per year**, and **Australia and Italy** (which ran hotter inflation) as high as **~5% per year**.
DS2's own replication confirms the mechanism at the country-year level: **44% of 1945–80 country-years**
had negative real short rates (mean real short rate **−0.8%**), against only **10%** in the
liberalization era (1981–2007, mean **+3.2%**) — and the **76%** post-GFC share (mean **−0.7%**) is,
as Part D already notes, the modern echo of exactly this mechanism. Repression is the *largest*
single-channel magnitude of the five (3–5% of GDP/year, sustained for **35 years** in the canonical
episode) precisely because it is the hardest for the person paying it to see or resist in real time —
an "unlegislated tax," in Reinhart-Sbrancia's own phrase, rather than a vote.

### 4. Default and restructuring

**Reinhart & Rogoff's "This Time Is Different" chronology** (2009 book; NBER WP 13882) counts
**250 sovereign external-debt default episodes since 1800** — roughly **12 defaults per decade** on
average across their up-to-66-country sample — plus a further **~70 episodes of domestic public debt
default or restructuring** identified separately in their companion "Forgotten History of Domestic
Debt" work, a category most of the earlier sovereign-debt literature had ignored entirely. External
defaults cluster: regionally (Latin America and southern Europe after the Napoleonic Wars), and
internationally (the 1930s Depression wave, the 1980s Latin American wave). The chronology's other
headline finding, directly relevant to the inflation channel above: **inflation in the three years
following a domestic default episode averages nearly 120%** across the sample — domestic default and
inflationary liquidation are not separate channels in practice so much as two points on one
continuum. Default is thus the *second most frequent* channel by episode count (250+ in two centuries)
but the *most concentrated in time* — it resolves the debt stock in one discrete event (PSI 2012 below
is the modern template) rather than the multi-decade grind of repression.

### 5. Wealth levies

The rarest, most discrete channel, and the one furthest from anything an Indian desk needs to model
as a recurring input — but it belongs on the menu because two of this Part's cases used it explicitly.
Post-WWII capital levies were enacted in **Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Japan,
Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Norway**. Japan's 1946–47 progressive property levy (*zaisan zei*)
fell on the richest 2–3 centiles of Japanese society and **raised over 10% of national income in the
single year it was collected**. Germany's 1952 *Lastenausgleich* ("Burden Equalization") required
undamaged property-holders to pay **one-half of their assessed 1948-reform property value**, in
installments over 30 years, and is credited with cutting Germany's top-1% wealth share by **~5.5
percentage points** — more than the physical destruction of the war itself. Both episodes required a
society-wide sense of shared postwar sacrifice that has no obvious peacetime analogue; the channel
appears in the historical record almost exclusively in the direct aftermath of total war.

**Which channels are even available depends on the r−g regime a country is in.** DS3 supplies the
governing fact: in the **1945–80 repression era**, mean (r−g) was **−4.3pp** and r<g held in **83% of
years** — debt could melt via growth+repression with primary balance doing almost no work. In the
**1981–2007 liberalization era**, mean (r−g) flipped to **+1.1pp**, r<g held in only **37% of years**
— and in that regime, per Eichengreen-Panizza, only sustained surpluses or outright default can bring
b down; growth and quiet repression stop being available for free. Case 8 below (Canada, Sweden,
Ireland) is precisely the set of countries that ran successful, non-repression consolidations *inside*
the liberalization regime — the hard version of the problem, solved through the primary-surplus and
expenditure channels rather than the repression channel that dominates every pre-1980 case.

## B2. Ten case studies

### 1. United Kingdom, 1815–1914 — the gold-standard-era, surplus-and-growth century

**Setup.** Twenty-two years of war against Revolutionary and Napoleonic France left Britain with the
highest debt/GDP of any country that has ever fully repaid its debt: outstanding debt of roughly
**£1 billion**, representing **~213% of GDP** at the 1815 peace and continuing to climb to **roughly
260% of GDP around 1819** as wartime obligations were finally settled. **The descent.** Over the
following century, without a single default and with only very brief episodes of deflation rather
than inflation, debt fell to **30% of GDP by 1914** — a **~230-percentage-point** decline, the largest
sustained deleveraging in the modern historical record, reaching a **200-year low of 29% of GDP** on
the eve of WWI in 1913–14. **Which terms did the work.** This was overwhelmingly a **growth-and-modest-
surplus** descent, not a repression or inflation descent: British consols paid a **3% coupon** against
**near-zero average inflation (~0.28%/year, 1692–1914)**, so gilt holders earned a real return close to
the nominal coupon throughout — the opposite of every 20th-century repression case below. Population
growth alone — Britain's population grew **2.4×** between 1815 and 1913 — mechanically reduced the
debt/GDP ratio by an estimated **~125 percentage points** of the total decline, with real per-capita
growth and modest primary surpluses (the gold standard's implicit commitment to eventual budget
balance) doing the rest. Consol price volatility itself **fell by more than half** after 1815, and
Dimson-Marsh-Staunton's 20th-century UK equity series shows **5.8%/year real** returns over
1900–2000 — this was, in every dimension, the friendliest possible environment for both bondholders
and equity holders to sit through a sovereign deleveraging. **Lesson for L15.** This is the proof that
a "painless" descent is possible without repression — but it required a full century, a monetary
regime (gold, with essentially zero structural inflation bias) that no longer exists anywhere, and a
demographic tailwind India cannot count on repeating on the same terms. It is the historical floor
case for how good the growth-and-surplus channel can be; it is not a template that transfers.

### 2. United States, 1946–1974 — the canonical repression case, decomposed

**Setup.** US federal debt held by the public reached **119% of GDP in FY1946** (some series report a
close but not identical **106%** figure depending on the exact debt concept and GNP-vs-GDP base
`[VERIFY: reconcile the 119% vs 106% series definitions]`), up from **45–50% of GDP in FY1941** — a
**~70-percentage-point** wartime run-up in five years. **The descent.** By **1974** the ratio had fallen
to roughly **23–32%** depending on series (Hall & Sargent's own 2011 AEJ:Macro paper puts the 1946→1974
move as an **~83-point** fall; the Fed's debt-held-by-public series shows **80.1%→25.3%** across the
slightly wider 1950–1975 window). **Which terms did the work — the decomposition.** Hall & Sargent
(2011, *AEJ: Macroeconomics*, "Interest Rate Risk and Other Determinants of Post-WWII US Government
Debt/GDP Dynamics") attribute a substantial share of the fall to **negative real returns via surprise
inflation** operating on the government's fixed-rate debt stock `[VERIFY: primary paper's exact
point-estimate split was not independently retrieved this pass]`. The more recent and more precisely
sourced reassessment, **Acalin & Ball (2024, NBER WP 31577 / IMF WP 2024/005)**, runs the sharpest test
available: a counterfactual removing (i) the **surprise inflation** of 1946–48 (CPI inflation reached
**~17.6%** in 1946–47) and (ii) the **1942–51 Fed-Treasury interest-rate peg** (short bill rates held
at **0.375%** throughout the war and Korea) shows debt/GDP would have fallen only to **74% by 1974**
— not the actual 23%. In other words: of the roughly 83-point actual fall, **growth and primary
surpluses alone explain only the ~32–45 points that would have happened anyway; the remaining
~40–51 points are repression** (surprise inflation plus the administered-rate peg). Acalin-Ball's own
words: "only a small amount of debt reduction has been achieved through growth rates that exceed
undistorted interest rates" — the US did **not**, on their reading, simply grow out of its WWII debt.
**Investor outcomes, in real terms.** US Treasury bonds recorded **four consecutive negative-real-return
decades starting with the 1940s**: **−2.63%/year real in the 1940s**, **−1.80%/year real in the 1950s**
— the worst and second-worst Treasury decades of the entire 1900–2020 record, coinciding exactly with
the peg years and its immediate aftermath. **Lesson for L15.** This is the highest-resolution evidence
in the entire dossier that the repression channel's magnitude is large (tens of percentage points of
debt/GDP over ~15 years) and falls specifically and disproportionately on **long-duration nominal
bondholders** — precisely the DS2/DS4 mechanism the gold floor is built to hedge, now decomposed down
to the instrument level.

### 3. United Kingdom, 1946–1990s — repression plus two devaluations

**Setup.** UK net public debt peaked at **~252% of GDP in FY1946/47** (some series cite a slightly
higher **~270%** shortly after). **The descent.** By **1971/72** debt/GDP had already fallen to
**62.1%**, and it continued down to **~50% of GDP** by the early 1990s. **Which terms did the work.**
Nominal GDP grew **8.8%/year on average** across the descent — **2.3%/year real growth plus 6.5%/year
average inflation** — while the interest rate on government debt sat **below** the inflation rate in
**24 of the 30 years** from 1950, the direct UK analogue of the US peg-and-surprise-inflation
mechanism, run for longer and at higher average inflation. Two step-devaluations punctuate the period:
**1949** (sterling cut ~30.5% against the dollar) and **1967** (a further ~14.3% cut, "another
devaluation and economic humiliation" in the contemporary phrase) — each one a discrete real write-down
of sterling-denominated external claims, layered on top of the continuous domestic repression. Budget
deficits **averaged only 1.2% of GDP** through the 1950s–60s: this was **not** a primary-surplus-led
descent at all — it is repression and growth doing nearly all of the work, with fiscal policy close to
balance rather than actively retiring debt. **Lesson for L15.** The UK case is the clean "double dose"
version of the US story: the same repression mechanism, run over a longer window (45 years vs the
US's ~28), reinforced by two devaluations that a country on a fixed peg (the US, effectively, in this
period) did not have available — a reminder that currency flexibility widens, not narrows, a sovereign's
menu for debasement.

### 4. Germany, 1919–1923 hyperinflation and 1948 currency reform — the hard-reset path, twice

**Setup.** Weimar Germany entered peace owing **98 billion marks in war bonds** to its own citizens on
top of Allied reparations obligations, with government revenue covering **never more than 35% of
outlays, 1919–23**. **The descent — hyperinflation.** The Reichsbank's financing of the deficit by
printing drove the mark from parity to **4.2 trillion marks per US dollar by November 1923** — a
default on the entire domestic debt stock via currency destruction rather than a legislated
repudiation. **Who paid.** War-bond holders and cash savers were **wiped out**; some revalued debts
(mortgages) allowed lenders partial recoupment, but the overwhelming transfer was from creditor/saver
households to debtor households and the state itself. **The second reset — 1948.** Postwar West
Germany's currency reform converted Reichsmark cash and bank deposits into new Deutsche Marks at
**100:1 for cash/deposits down to effectively ~6.5:1 for practical purposes, and 10:1 for debts and
most wages/prices** — a second, administratively engineered wipeout of nominal claims, again borne
overwhelmingly by savers rather than holders of physical assets (goods, companies, real estate), who
gained relative to cash-holders. A **1952 Lastenausgleich** capital levy (one-half of assessed 1948
property value, payable over 30 years) then redistributed a further slice from the undamaged to the
damaged, cutting the top-1% wealth share by an estimated **~5.5 percentage points** — larger than the
effect of wartime physical destruction itself. **Lesson for L15.** Germany is the dossier's clearest
demonstration that the "hard reset" path is not one channel but typically **two or three stacked
together** (hyperinflation, then a currency-reform devaluation of nominal claims, then in some cases a
capital levy) — and that in every layer, the group that pays is the same: holders of nominal claims
(bonds, cash, bank deposits), never holders of real/physical assets. This is the strongest single
historical argument, outside DS4's own pooled equity result, for why a portfolio's debasement hedge
must be a **real** asset (gold, per L15's design) rather than an inflation-linked nominal claim, which
in a genuine hard-reset scenario is exactly the instrument most likely to be redefined out from under
the holder.

### 5. France, post-WWII — inflation as the quiet default

**Setup and mechanism.** France emerged from occupation with a heavy domestic debt stock and, per the
Banque de France's own historical review, its rapid postwar debt reduction "is regularly analysed as
being the result of inflationary policies initiated by governments," with a further slice of debt
"transferred to commercial banks after 1945 through financial repression." **The specific instruments.**
In **February 1945** the state administratively converted outstanding **4% (1917/1918 vintage) and
4.5% (1932 vintage)** annuities down to **3%** — a direct, legislated coupon cut on existing
bondholders, executed by decree rather than negotiated restructuring. Tight exchange controls, with
the external value of the franc "fixed in an authoritarian way according to bilateral agreements,"
persisted until **1947**, insulating the repression from an immediate capital-flight response.
**Growth cover.** The subsequent *Trente Glorieuses* (1947–1973) delivered **~5%/year average
growth**, providing the denominator-side cover that made the inflation-driven numerator erosion look,
in retrospect, like a growth story rather than a default. **Lesson for L15.** France is this Part's
cleanest illustration of Reinhart-Rogoff's "inflation is default without saying so" framing applied
outside the Anglophone cases: an explicit coupon cut (not merely negative real rates) combined with
capital controls and then thirty years of strong growth as cover — the same recipe as the US and UK
cases, but with the additional, more visible step of a legislated interest-rate haircut that a purely
inflation-only mechanism does not require.

### 6a. Japan, 1944–1950 — hyperinflation, the 1946 capital levy, and the Dodge Line

**Setup.** Wartime and immediate-postwar Japan financed itself through what the literature calls an
"internal financing mechanism" that produced outright hyperinflation once occupation-era expansionary
policy met an overheating economy: wholesale prices rose **more than 700% from September 1945 to
August 1948**, and **13-fold from April 1946 to March 1949**. **Who paid — the 1946–47 capital levy.**
Rather than relying purely on inflation, occupation-era Japan layered an explicit, steeply progressive
property tax (*zaisan zei*) on top of it, falling on the richest 2–3 centiles of society and **raising
over 10% of national income in the single year it was collected** — the levy channel (B1 §5) used at
genuine scale, not as a footnote. **The stabilization — the Dodge Line.** American banker Joseph
Dodge's March 1949 program forced a **balanced budget**, a fixed exchange rate, and an abrupt end to
inflationary financing — at the cost of a sharp "stabilization crisis" recession, with US aid running
at **~25% of GDP** through the transition and a balanced-budget norm that held **until the mid-1960s**.
**Lesson for L15.** Japan 1944–50 combines all three of the harshest channels — hyperinflationary
default, an explicit wealth levy, and then externally-imposed fiscal austerity — in under six years,
the fastest full-menu resolution in this dossier. It is the direct ancestor, forty years removed, of
the second Japan case below, and the contrast between the two is itself the lesson: postwar Japan
resolved debt in five brutal years; today's Japan has spent thirty-five years explicitly avoiding
that path.

### 6b. Japan, 1990–present — the 250%+ ongoing experiment

**Setup and current state.** Japan's post-1971 (fiat-era) debt/GDP peak, per our own DS1 computation,
is **254% in 2020** with **0pp of sustained decline** — the JST panel's most extreme "still ascending"
case. Independent 2024–26 data confirm the order of magnitude: general **gross** government debt was
**234.9% of GDP at end-March 2025** (¥1,324 trillion); a narrower Cabinet Office measure shows the
ratio **peaking at 202.8% in FY2022** and easing only slowly toward **~187.6% by FY2026** on a
different accounting basis — the exact level is measure-dependent, but every series agrees Japan runs
the highest sovereign debt ratio of any major economy, with **no** JST-verified completed deleveraging
episode. **How it has been carried.** Roughly **95% of JGBs are held by domestic residents**
`[VERIFY: exact current share; widely cited but not independently re-confirmed against a primary BOJ
table this pass]`, and JGB yields sat **near zero for decades** even as the debt ratio climbed —
"an unusual pattern," as the literature notes, in which elevated debt did *not* pressure yields upward,
attributed to durable home-country bias among domestic holders rather than any external discipline.
**The 2022–24 stress test of the arrangement.** The Bank of Japan's Yield Curve Control regime
(targeting the 10-year JGB near 0%, with a widening reference band through 2022–23) was formally
**ended on 19 March 2024**, alongside the exit from **negative policy rates** — the first BOJ rate
hike since 2007 and the end of the world's only negative-rate regime. This was the live test of
whether three-plus decades of repression-adjacent policy could be unwound without a disorderly JGB
sell-off; the exit proceeded in an orderly fashion, but yen weakness and a subsequent foreign bid for
JGBs became live watch-items in its aftermath. **Investor outcomes, in real terms.** The Nikkei 225's
**price** index only regained its December 1989 nominal peak (38,915.87) on **22 February 2024** —
**34 years** later, and on a pure price basis the **1989–2024 real return was negative** (**−1.1%/year
in yen, −8.6%/year in USD**, price-only). Including reinvested dividends, the picture flips: **total**
returns since end-1989 reached **+54% cumulative in yen / +42% in USD** (versus **+3,331%** for the
S&P 500 total return over the same window) — the dividend-inclusive index first exceeded its 1989
level in **February 2021**, 32 years in. **Lesson for L15.** Japan is the live proof that a fiat-era
debt ratio can sit above 250% of GDP for over three decades with **no** completed deleveraging and no
crisis, provided the debt is overwhelmingly domestically held, the currency floats, and the central
bank is willing to suppress the entire yield curve — but the price for bond and equity holders alike
is a multi-decade real-return drought (near-zero JGB real yields; a negative-to-flat real equity
index for a generation) that DS4's pooled "fiscal-dominance-state" mean real equity return of
**+4.5%/year** does not fully capture at this extreme end of duration. It is the strongest evidence in
this Part that L15's design choice — sizing gold as insurance against the *state*, not against any
specific *resolution date* — is correct: nobody who modeled a 5–10 year Japan reversion in 1995 would
have been right yet in 2025.

### 7. Italy and Belgium, 1990s — EMU-convergence consolidations, one that held and one that didn't

**Setup.** At the 1992 Maastricht Treaty, both countries sat far above the 60%-of-GDP reference value:
**Belgium at close to 140% of GDP**, **Italy above 100% and rising to 117% in 1994** (our own JST panel
records Belgium's post-1971 peak at **135% in 1993**). Both signed up to a treaty text that, because a
hard 60% ceiling was unreachable "anytime soon" for either, substituted a softer standard: debt must be
"sufficiently diminishing" toward the reference value "at a satisfactory pace." **Belgium — the channel
that held.** A sustained, multi-year primary-surplus effort — exactly the Eichengreen-Panizza rare
category from B1 §2 — brought Belgian debt down; DS1 records a **48-percentage-point decline from the
135% (1993) peak, with no subsequent re-ascent above it through 2020** — one of only **four** JST-panel
countries whose post-1971 peak arc is classified **YES, completed**. By euro launch in 1999, both
countries' debt had fallen to **roughly 110% of GDP**. **Italy — the channel that didn't hold.** Italy's
own consolidation continued after 1999, reaching **102% by 2002** and **below 100% by 2007** — a real,
documented ~17–21-point decline from the 1994 peak — but our own JST panel shows Italy's **post-1971
peak is actually 2020, at 156% of GDP, with 0pp of net decline**: the GFC and then COVID pushed debt
back up past the 1994 level and kept climbing, erasing the entire 1990s consolidation and then some.
**Which terms did the work, and why the difference.** Both consolidations ran on the same primary-
surplus-plus-EMU-discipline mechanism; the difference is durability, not mechanism — Belgium's
political system sustained the surplus effort through multiple subsequent governments and never let
debt reascend past its old peak, while Italy's did not survive two subsequent shocks. **Lesson for L15.**
This is the single cleanest matched pair in the entire case record for the point B1 already makes in
the abstract: a genuine Eichengreen-Panizza-style large sustained primary surplus **can** delever a
100%+-of-GDP economy — but "sustained" turns out to mean sustained across multiple electoral cycles and
multiple external shocks, not merely sustained long enough to hit a treaty benchmark. The consolidation
that looks identical in 2002 can look completely different by 2020.

### 8. The r>g-era successes — Canada 1995–2008, Sweden post-1993, Ireland post-2013

These three are the case record's answer to the hardest version of the question: can a country
delever **without** the repression tailwind, under the liberalized-rate, r>g regime DS3 shows governed
1981–2007 (mean r−g **+1.1pp**, r<g in only **37%** of years)? **Canada.** Federal net debt fell from
**74.4% of GDP in 1995 to 28.9% in 2008** — a **~46-point** decline — via Finance Minister Paul Martin's
1995–96 "Program Review": departmental budget cuts ranging **5–65%**, a **~20% cut to the core federal
public service**, and federal transfer reforms, turning a **~3%-of-GDP deficit (1995)** into **annual
surpluses from 1997 through the 2007–08 crisis**. This is expenditure-led consolidation with essentially
no repression assist — real rates were positive through most of the period. `[Note: this specific
"federal net debt" series differs from the general-government gross-debt concept our own JST panel
uses; DS1 records Canada's post-1971 peak on that broader basis as 117% in 2020 with 0pp decline —
i.e., a real and substantial 1990s federal consolidation did not, on the broader all-government gross
measure, prevent a new post-1971 high two decades later. Both facts are true simultaneously; they are
different debt concepts, not a contradiction.]` **Sweden.** Our own JST panel is the cleanest record
here: post-1971 peak **73% of GDP in 1996**, declining **38 points with no re-ascent through 2020** —
one of DS1's four "YES, completed" cases. The mechanism: a 1990–93 banking crisis (GDP fell three
straight years, unemployment rose from **2% to 11%**, the budget deficit peaked at **13% of GDP in
1993**) forced both bank recapitalization (at ultimately near-zero net fiscal cost — Sweden ranks
favorably among 39 systemic banking crises 1970–2007 on this metric) and a hard expenditure
consolidation that turned the deficit into **surplus by 1998**. **Ireland.** Post-bailout debt peaked
near **120% of GDP around 2012–13** (DS1: **120% in 2013**) and fell **63 points with no re-ascent
through 2020** — another of the four completed arcs. The consolidation combined VAT and carbon/motor-
vehicle tax increases, a supplementary personal income surtax, civil-service cuts, and capital-spending
savings, against a strong post-bailout growth recovery: one detailed accounting shows debt falling
from **118.9% (2012) to 65.3% (2017)** — a **53.6-point** move in five years, among the fastest
completed descents in the entire panel. **The shared political precondition.** All three required a
visible, acute crisis (a currency-and-banking crisis for Sweden, a sovereign bailout for Ireland, a
~30-year deficit habit reaching a breaking point for Canada) to generate the political mandate for
multi-year expenditure cuts; none of the three ran their consolidation as a discretionary choice absent
that precondition. **Lesson for L15.** The non-repression path exists and is well-documented — but in
every instance it required either a genuinely small, open economy with unusual political cohesion
(Sweden, Ireland) or a crisis-driven mandate (all three) that a larger, less externally-constrained
economy has historically found much harder to generate absent an acute shock.

### 9. Greece, 2010–2018 — default inside a monetary union

**Setup.** Greek debt reached **~186% of GDP by 2018** (some series: 181%) at the formal end of its
adjustment programs, after **six consecutive years of GDP contraction (2009–2015) totaling a
cumulative ~25% loss** — over a quarter of the economy gone by constant prices between 2008 and 2016.
Unemployment rose from **9.6% (2009)** to a peak of **27.5% (September 2013)**, with youth unemployment
above **60%**. **The PSI — default, precisely quantified.** The March 2012 Private Sector Involvement
restructuring is this Part's cleanest, most exactly documented default event: **~97% of privately held
Greek bonds** took a **53.5% nominal haircut** on face value, with the reduced coupons on the new bonds
pushing the **net-present-value loss to an estimated 75–80%** for private creditors — the largest
sovereign restructuring loss of the post-WWII era, executed entirely without a currency devaluation
(Greece had no exchange-rate channel available inside the euro, which is precisely why the fiscal and
internal-devaluation adjustment had to be so much larger than in any of the currency-flexible cases
above). **Investor outcomes, in real terms.** Beyond the PSI haircut itself: the Athens Stock Exchange
lost **more than 85% of its collective value from 2007 to August 2015**; after adjusting for inflation,
Greece's stock-return index was reported in 2025 as **lower than its 1999 level, and lower even than
its 1961 level** — a genuine, multi-generational real equity loss with no parallel elsewhere in this
dossier. The **4.4%-of-GDP primary surplus posted in 2018** — itself brushing the very upper edge of
the Eichengreen-Panizza rarity band from B1 §2 — illustrates how much fiscal effort a monetary-union
member without a repression or currency channel had to extract to even begin stabilizing the ratio.
**Lesson for L15.** Greece is the modern proof-case for what happens when a sovereign loses the
inflation/repression/devaluation channels simultaneously (currency union) and cannot generate a
sufficient primary surplus fast enough (political limits, per B1 §2): the residual adjustment lands on
bondholders (default, precisely as PSI shows) and on real output and equity values (a quarter-century,
not merely a cyclical, loss) rather than on savers-via-inflation. For an Indian book that is never
going to be inside a currency union, the direct transferability is limited — but Greece is the sharpest
available evidence for how much *worse* the outcome gets when the repression/currency channels are
foreclosed and only default and internal devaluation remain.

### 10. India — the repression apparatus, dormant but standing

**Pre-1991: financial repression in full.** On the eve of the 1991 crisis, India's Statutory Liquidity
Ratio stood at **38.5%** and the Cash Reserve Ratio at **15%** — together forcing banks to hold roughly
**half their deposit base** in government paper or with the RBI, at administered, below-market rates.
This was the repression apparatus at genuine, textbook scale: a captive domestic banking system
functioning as the fiscal system's primary financing arm, the exact mechanism Reinhart-Sbrancia
document for the 1945–80 advanced-economy era, running in India in essentially its purest form as late
as 1990–91. **The 1991 BoP crisis and the gold pledge.** Foreign reserves fell from **$1.2 billion in
January 1991 to about half that by June**, financing barely **three weeks of imports**; against a
Gulf-War oil shock and a collapse in remittances, the RBI **airlifted 47 tonnes of gold to the Bank of
England and 20 tonnes to the Union Bank of Switzerland** (67 tonnes total) as collateral for a
**$600 million** bridge loan, alongside a separate **$2.2 billion emergency IMF facility** — the
collateralized-pledge, not sale, structure meant the gold was recoverable, and the episode became the
proximate trigger for the 1991 liberalization program. **Post-2003: the growth-led decline.** The 2003
FRBM Act's deficit targets coincided with India's strongest sustained growth run: general-government
debt/GDP, at roughly **84–86% in 2003**, fell to approximately **75% by 2007** and toward a **~66–67%
trough around 2010–11** `[VERIFY: exact trough year and precise level — sources converge on
"mid-60s%, ~2010–12" but do not agree to the single point]` — a genuine growth-led descent of
15–20 points achieved with no repression assist and with India's own r−g arithmetic favorable through
most of the run. **The FRBM arc and its suspensions.** The Act was formally **suspended from FY2008–09
through FY2012–13** to fund the GFC-era stimulus (fiscal deficit widened to **6.2% of GDP** against a

(N.K. Singh Committee recommendation), permitting up to a **0.5pp-of-GDP** deviation for national

**The COVID spike and today's level.** Combined general-government debt/GDP **peaked at
approximately 89% in FY2020–21** (IMF: "from 74% to almost 90%" across the pandemic year) before
easing with nominal-GDP recovery to roughly **82% in 2024** and a projected **~80% for 2025**.
**Today's ownership structure.** As of December 2025, commercial banks hold **34.31%** of outstanding
central-government securities, insurance companies **25.89%**, the RBI **14.52%**, and FPIs only
**2.96%** — domestic institutions (banks, insurers, RBI) together own **nearly three-quarters** of the
market, a captive-adjacent structure that, while today's SLR sits at only **18%** (unchanged through
the RBI's June 2025 policy review) versus the pre-1991 38.5%, still concentrates the debt stock inside
regulated, domestically-anchored balance sheets rather than distributing it to price-sensitive foreign
holders — the repression apparatus dormant, in the Contract's own phrase, but standing. **What the

**Lesson for L15.** India is the only case in this Part where the desk can watch the entire menu —

managing India's own debt stock is *itself*, right now, accumulating the asset the seat treats as the

### Six pooled conclusions, ranked by evidence strength, mapped to L15

1. **(Strongest — DS1's own 18-country, 150-year panel, corroborated in every pre-1971 case above.)**
   Successful, *lasting* fiat-era (post-1971) deleveraging is genuinely rare — 4 of 18 JST-panel
   countries — while the pre-1971 record (UK, US, France, Germany, Japan) is full of dramatic, largely
   repression-driven descents. The two facts are not in tension: they describe *different monetary
   regimes*. → **Design implication:** L15's Tier-C, reduce-only, state-classification-only mandate is
   the correct response to this exact asymmetry — the historical base rate for "this resolves the way
   the textbook 1940s–70s cases resolved" is lower under the current (post-1971, open-capital-account)
   order than the popular narrative assumes, which is precisely why the seat sizes insurance rather
   than timing a resolution.
2. **(Strong — Reinhart-Sbrancia's 12-country magnitude study, DS2's independent country-year
   replication, and the US/UK/Japan case decompositions above all agree on order of magnitude.)**
   Where a lasting descent *did* happen without default, repression (negative real rates operating on a
   captive or currency-flexible domestic debt stock) did more of the work than growth alone in every
   well-decomposed case (US, UK, France) — Acalin-Ball's reassessment is the sharpest single data point:
   only ~40–45% of the US's 1946–74 fall survives a counterfactual with undistorted rates. →
   **Design implication:** the gold-floor attribution band (0.40–0.50 of the gold sleeve's rationale)
   and the conditional +1–2pp lift when the fiscal-dominance state is ON are sized against a channel
   (repression) that is not a tail scenario in the historical record — it is the modal resolution
   mechanism whenever a resolution has actually occurred.
3. **(Strong — Eichengreen-Panizza's 54-country, 235-period study, directly corroborated by the
   Italy/Belgium matched pair and the Canada/Sweden/Ireland trio.)** Large, sustained primary surpluses
   (≥3% of GDP for ≥10 years) are achievable but require either an external convergence anchor (EMU,
   in Italy/Belgium's case) or an acute, mandate-generating crisis (Sweden, Ireland, and Canada's own
   near-crisis fiscal trajectory) — and even then durability is not guaranteed, as Italy's reversal
   shows. → **Design implication:** none of the ten cases supports treating "India will run a large
   sustained primary surplus" as a base-case input to any horizon of this design; the seat should never
   assume the surplus channel activates absent an equivalent acute domestic trigger, which is itself
   unforecastable and therefore correctly excluded from Stage 1.
4. **(Strong on direction, Tier C on India-specific magnitude — DS4's pooled cross-country result is
   Tier A/B; the India-specific instantiation is asserted by design, not yet measured.)** DS4 shows
   equities earn positive but roughly halved real returns in the fiscal-dominance state (+4.5%/yr vs
   +9.5%/yr normal) — this Part adds that the *tail* of that distribution (Japan's 35-year experiment,
   Greece's PSI-plus-internal-devaluation) can be far worse than the pooled mean, particularly when
   bond and property claims (not just equities) are counted, or when the repression/currency channels
   are foreclosed entirely (Greece). → **Design implication:** this is the direct justification for
   never treating DS4's pooled mean as a floor — the seat's tail budget (0.3–0.6%/yr of NAV) exists
   precisely because the mean understates the worst realized outcomes this Part documents (Japan,
   Greece), and the design must not fatigue even if the state persists for a decade, exactly as Part H
   already specifies.
5. **(Moderate — a single, well-documented but narrow domestic case: India's own 1991–2025 arc.)**
   India has run the entire five-channel menu (repression, BoP/gold-collateral crisis, growth-led
   decline, fiscal-rule suspension under stress) within living memory, on its own currency, and is
   *currently* running a live instance of the reserve-diversification/gold-accumulation leg (RBI
   holdings 557.8t→~880t since 2015, accelerating since 2017, including a symbolically pointed 2024
   repatriation from the same London vault used in the 1991 pledge). → **Design implication:** this is
   the strongest domestically-sourced evidence available for the L15 seat's gold-floor rationale — not
   a cross-country prior imported from the JST panel, but the balance sheet of the institution that
   actually manages India's own debt, observed making the same allocation choice the seat encodes.
   **What would falsify the seat:** a multi-year, sustained *reduction* in RBI gold holdings
   accompanied by a stable or improving domestic r−g regime (i.e., the repression gauge from Part E
   turning and staying OFF, with no CB gold-accumulation leg) — that combination, sustained, is the
   observable pattern this design would need to see before the gold-floor attribution band should be
   revisited downward.
6. **(Moderate — a definitional finding, not a market finding, but one this Part surfaces cleanly via
   the Canada/Italy contrasts.)** The choice of debt series (federal vs general government; net vs
   gross; held-by-public vs total) materially changes which decade reads as "the peak" and whether a
   consolidation reads as "completed" — Canada's celebrated 1995–2008 federal turnaround coexists,
   without contradiction, with a JST-panel finding that Canada's broader general-government gross-debt
   post-1971 peak is actually 2020. → **Design implication:** any India debt/GDP input the ladder
   consumes (per Part E's STEP 1) must be pinned to one explicit, stated series definition and
   vintage-tracked exactly as Part D's D07/DB3 failure-mode already specifies — a "peak" or "descent"
   claim is meaningless without naming which debt concept it refers to, and this Part shows how easily
   two contradictory-sounding claims about the *same country* can both be true under different
   definitions.

## C.1 The core problem — centre debt is not general government debt, and neither is "the" debt ratio

Every headline "India's debt/GDP" number conceals a choice among at least four different
aggregates, and the choice moves the number by 20–25 points of GDP:

| Aggregate | ≈FY26 level | What's in it | What's left out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Centre government debt (Budget/Status Paper definition) | **~58%** of GDP (FY26 RE, missing the government's own 55.6–56.1% target by ~200bp per press reporting **[VERIFY exact bp figure, single-source]**) | Dated G-secs, T-bills, external loans at book value, small savings/provident-fund liabilities booked to the Centre | State debt, PSU debt, RBI's own liabilities, contingent guarantees |
| State + UT debt (RBI State Finances aggregate) | **~28.5%** of GDP (end-March 2024) | Market borrowings (SDLs), loans from Centre, NSSF-funded securities, provident funds, reserve funds | Centre debt; municipal/local-body debt; off-budget SPV debt |
| **General government debt** (Status Paper's own consolidated figure) | **~80–81%** of GDP (end-March 2024) | Centre + states, **netting out** inter-government transactions (Centre's loans to states, states' NSSF-funded securities, states' T-bill holdings with the Centre) | Still excludes PSUs, quasi-sovereign SPVs, and contingent liabilities (§C.13) |
| IMF/BIS cross-country "general government gross debt" | Close to, but not identical to, the Status Paper's own general-government figure — different consolidation conventions (GFSM 2014 vs. India's own budget accounting) can diverge by a few points of GDP | IMF/BIS apply a standardized cross-country methodology, not India's own consolidation rule | Same as above, plus whatever GFSM-vs-India definitional gaps exist — **[VERIFY]** the exact reconciliation, not found this pass |

**The rule this forces on every downstream construction**: never plot "India debt/GDP" from a
single series without stating which of these four it is. L15's debt-level-and-slope input (Part E
STEP 1) must be built on the **general government** aggregate — the fiscal-dominance question is
about the sovereign's aggregate claim on real resources, and a centre-only series would understate
the true state by ~20+ points of GDP precisely because state borrowing has been the faster-growing
leg (states' debt/GSDP rose from 16.7% in 2013-14 to 23.0% in 2022-23 per CAG's own decadal
report, more than doubling in rupee terms). A centre-only series is acceptable only as a
**faster-updating proxy** between general-government publication dates (§C.12 pipeline step 3).

## C.5 The general-government consolidation trap — the honest measurement problem

This is the section the task brief calls out by name, and it deserves to be stated as a list of
concrete failure modes, not an abstract caveat:

1. **Centre's loans to states** are an asset on the Centre's balance sheet and a liability on the
   state's — summing "Centre debt + state debt" naively double-counts this leg. The Status Paper's
   own General Government figure nets it out; a naive WEO-vs-RBI cross-check that doesn't will
   overstate the true stock.
2. **NSSF (National Small Savings Fund)** securities: states borrow from the NSSF (a Public Account
   instrument, not a Budget/fiscal-deficit line for the Centre) and the resulting state liability
   is a Centre *asset* — the same netting requirement as #1, with an added wrinkle: NSSF's own
   on-lending to entities like the **Food Corporation of India** (to fund the food-subsidy bill)
   is a genuinely **off-budget** liability that never appears in the Centre's own fiscal-deficit
   number, because Public Account transactions sit outside the Consolidated Fund of India by
   construction — not a loophole exploited once, a standing structural feature of how the FRBM
   Act's own fiscal-deficit definition is drawn.
3. **Extra-budgetary resources (EBRs) / off-budget borrowing**, more broadly: government-guaranteed
   or PSU-issued bonds whose debt service is effectively borne by the Budget (FCI bonds for food
   subsidy, oil-marketing-company bonds historically, power-sector recapitalization bonds) sit on
   the issuing entity's balance sheet, not the sovereign's — CAG's July 2019 assessment pegged
   FY2017-18's *true* fiscal deficit at **5.85% of GDP** against the reported **3.46%**, almost
   entirely on this basis; off-budget borrowing exceeded **₹2.35 lakh crore in FY2020-21** alone.
   The **2021 Budget's reform** (bringing several EBRs onto explicit accounting from FY2020-21)
   narrowed the gap going forward but does not restate the pre-2021 series — flag the boundary as
   a **level break**, identical discipline to a GDP/CPI/WPI base-year change.
4. **PSU and quasi-sovereign SPV debt** (bank recapitalization bonds, DFI-style vehicles, state
   power-distribution-company debt under UDAY-style restructurings): not on the Status Paper's
   General Government balance sheet at all — the largest genuinely un-consolidated piece, treated
   as unmeasurable-free rather than approximated (§C.13).

**The construction rule**: treat the Status Paper's "General Government Debt" line as the primary,
already-netted figure for #1–#2 (never re-derive the netting from raw Centre+state series — that
risks a second, uncross-checked convention); treat #3 as a dated, flagged level-break; treat #4 as
outside the free-data perimeter entirely (§C.13), never silently absorbed as if included.

## C.7 Maturity and ownership profile — the captivity metrics L15 needs

**Source**: the **Quarterly Report on Public Debt Management**, published by the **Public Debt
Management Cell (PDMC)** — formerly the "Middle Office," housed physically at RBI but organizationally
part of the Budget Division, **Department of Economic Affairs, Ministry of Finance** — not an RBI
publication in its own right, despite common attribution. Access path: `dea.gov.in/public-debt-
management-documents` (filename pattern `Quarterly Report on Public Debt Management for the
quarter {Month-Month yyyy}.pdf`); also press-released via PIB. **History**: running **since Q1
(April-June) FY2010-11** — 60+ editions by 2026. **Cadence/lag**: quarterly, released with an
approximate 8–10 week lag after quarter-end (exact figure **[VERIFY]**).

**What it carries, and why it is exactly the L15 Tier-C captivity input**: each edition tabulates
the **ownership pattern of outstanding dated government securities** by holder category — banks,
insurers, provident/pension funds, mutual funds, RBI, FPIs. This is the direct, quarterly, free
measurement of Reinhart-Sbrancia "liquidation rate" captivity (Part D §D2): how much of the
government's own debt is held by entities captive — bound by regulation, not free choice — to it.

| Holder | Approx. share of outstanding dated G-secs | Captivity mechanism | Vintage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commercial banks | **~38–39%** | SLR (§C.8) — statutory minimum holding requirement, the explicit repression-apparatus rule this seat's whole India-specific rationale rests on | end-June 2020: 39.0%; end-Sep 2022: 38.3% — broadly stable in this band across recent editions |
| Insurance companies | **~26%** | IRDAI Investment Regulations — life insurers must hold **≥25%** of controlled fund assets in government/approved securities (with a further ≥25% tier in gov't-or-approved securities); general insurers ≥20% (+10% further tier); reinsurers ≥20% (+10%) — a second, independent, explicit captive-holding rule | end-June 2020: 26.2% |
| RBI (own holdings, via OMO/secondary-market operations) | **~14%**, declining | Not a captivity rule in the SLR/IRDAI sense — RBI's own balance-sheet choice, but relevant to the "who absorbs supply" story and to COFER/gold-leg balance-sheet context (§C.9) | end-March 2023: 14.3%; end-June 2023: 13.8% |
| FPIs | **~3.3%** | RBI investment-limit caps (General Route + Fully Accessible Route) — **not** a captivity floor but a captivity *ceiling*, capping foreign ownership rather than mandating domestic ownership | May 2026: 3.34% of stock (₹3.75 lakh cr of ₹112.42 lakh cr); FY2025-26 limit ~6%, utilization ~22% as of April 2025 — **[VERIFY]**, moves fast on flow data |
| Provident/pension funds, mutual funds, others | Residual | EPFO/pension mandates carry their own minimum-G-sec rules (a further captivity leg, not separately quantified — **[VERIFY]** exact EPFO mandate %) | — |

**The composite captivity score for L15 Tier C** (Part E STEP 3): construct as banks' SLR-bound
share + insurers' IRDAI-bound share, tracked as a **trajectory**, not a level — the design already
frames this as "SLR trajectory" because *direction* matters more than level: a rising captive share
under negative real rates is the Reinhart-Sbrancia liquidation mechanism re-arming; a falling
captive share (SLR itself has fallen, §C.8) with FPI-driven yields is a less-repressed regime. This
is why the input is Tier C, clamped, reduce-only (Contract §4): the level is observable with
confidence every quarter, but fewer than four full SLR tightening/loosening cycles exist post-1991
to fit any stronger claim to.

**Maturity profile**: the same report also carries new-issuance weighted-average maturity and the
outstanding stock's maturity-bucket distribution — a free, complementary pull from the same document.

## C.8 The repression apparatus — SLR and CRR history

| Instrument | Statutory basis | 1990-91 peak | Deregulation path | 2026 level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **SLR** (Statutory Liquidity Ratio) | Banking Regulation Act 1949, §24 | **38.5%** | Reduced to the then-statutory floor of 25% by **1997** (post-Narasimham Committee I, 1991); the **Banking Regulation (Amendment) Act, 2007** removed the 25% statutory floor entirely, giving RBI full discretion to set SLR anywhere up to a 40% ceiling; subsequently reduced well below the old floor over the 2010s–2020s | **18.00%** [VERIFY current figure against RBI's live circular — search-corroborated for 2026 but not independently fetched] |
| **CRR** (Cash Reserve Ratio) | RBI Act 1934, §42(1) | **15%** | The **RBI (Amendment) Act, 2006** (effective 2006-06-22) removed both the 3% floor and the 20% ceiling on CRR, giving RBI unrestricted discretion; automatic ad-hoc Treasury Bill monetization of the fiscal deficit — the practice CRR had originally been calibrated to offset — was itself abandoned in **1994** | **3.00%** [VERIFY current figure, same caveat] |

**Why both instruments moved together, historically**: pre-1991, RBI automatically monetized the
Centre's fiscal deficit via ad-hoc Treasury Bills, and the high CRR+SLR combination existed
specifically to **offset** that monetary expansion — "only 35% of the increment in bank deposits
was actually available for commercial [private-sector] advances" under the pre-reform regime, per
the standard account. This is the cleanest illustration in the whole India dataset of Part D's
repression mechanism as **standing, legislated infrastructure** rather than an emergency measure —
SLR is not a metaphor for repression here, it is repression's statutory instrument, with a fully
dated three-decade unwind trajectory (38.5% → 25% → today's much lower figure) that is itself the
India-specific input DB1 (Part F) calls for.

**Interest-rate deregulation milestones** (the companion series to SLR/CRR — when the "captive
buyer, administered price" system gave way to a market-determined one):

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| 1989 | Ceiling on (non-SLR) interest rates withdrawn; Certificates of Deposit introduced |
| 1990 | Commercial Paper introduced — money-market instruments begin pricing off market rates |
| 1991 (Nov) | Narasimham Committee I report — the founding document of the deregulation program |
| 1994 (Oct) | RBI abolishes the minimum lending rate; banks set their own Prime Lending Rate |
| 1991–2002 | Lending rate declines from ~19.0% (1991-92) to ~10.5–11.0% (2001-02) as the system re-prices |
| 2000 | Liquidity Adjustment Facility (LAF) framework established (interim LAF from April 1999), repo/reverse-repo become the operative signaling rates |
| 2016 | Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) era begins — repo formally the sole policy rate, price-stability-mandated |

**The pre-1991 honesty note — real "market" rates are structurally unobservable, and the gauge
must say so, not paper over it.** Before the 1989–94 deregulation sequence, deposit and lending
rates were RBI-administered, not market-clearing — a computed "real rate" for this period (nominal
administered rate minus inflation) measures the *gap the administered price left unarbitraged*,
not a market-clearing signal the way the post-2000 repo-CPI construction is. The design's own
repression gauge (Part E STEP 2: repo − CPI, rolling share of trailing 36m negative) is **only
meaningfully comparable to itself post-2000** — a pre-1991 reading is a different, cruder object
(closer to JST's own repression-era readings, DS2's 1945-80 44% negative-real-rate share, than to
a genuine market signal), and DB1 (Part F) must carry this caveat inline every time it is shown.

**Capital-control indicator — the Chinn-Ito (KAOPEN) index.**

| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Source | Chinn & Ito (2006), maintained at `web.pdx.edu/~ito/Chinn-Ito_website.htm` |
| Construction | Principal-components index built from the IMF's own **Annual Report on Exchange Arrangements and Exchange Restrictions (AREAER)** binary capital-control indicator tabulation |
| Coverage | 1970–2022 (latest confirmed update, per the "2022 Update," described in Ito & Chinn 2025), ~181–182 countries, India included |
| Cadence | **Annual, but released with a ~2–3 year lag** — the "2022 Update" itself was published May 2025; treat any KAOPEN value for the most recent 2–3 years as not-yet-available, not zero/missing-by-error |
| Format | Free Excel/Stata download, no login |
| India relevance | A single free, standardized, cross-country-comparable annual score for how open India's capital account is at any point since 1970 — the natural companion to the SLR/CRR domestic-repression series, since capital controls and domestic captive-holding rules are the two legs of the same repression apparatus (a captive domestic buyer is a much weaker tool if capital is free to flee to a better-yielding foreign asset instead) |

## C.9 The real-rate gauge — building one continuous series from three different rate regimes

**Policy-rate splice rule** (India leg):

| Regime | Rate | Window | Free source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Longest series | **Call money rate** (overnight uncollateralized interbank rate) | Available at least from the early 1970s (CRR was as low as 3% and SLR 25% in the early 1970s per historical accounts, implying an active call market already) | RBI Handbook of Statistics on the Indian Economy (annual, stable, small file — A-catalog G3); DBIE for the queryable modern tail |
| Pre-LAF signaling rate | **Bank Rate** | From **1935-07-04** (RBI's first-ever announced Bank Rate, 3.5%) through the 1990s | Same Handbook — the single cleanest long, clean, rate-change-dated table in the whole catalog (A-catalog G3) |
| Modern operative rate | **Repo rate** (LAF framework) | From **2000** (interim LAF from April 1999, full LAF 2000); MSF added 2011; MPC-era repo-centric framework from **2016** | DBIE current-rate tables; Handbook for the historical rate-change-date ledger |

**The splice rule, stated precisely**: use Bank Rate pre-2000 (or call money where a purer
market-clearing series is wanted, accepting its own pre-1991-94 administered-era caveat), repo
2000-onward, with the **regime label itself carried as a column** (`rate_regime ∈
{bank_rate_administered, call_money, repo_laf, repo_mpc}`) rather than silently concatenating
levels — the identical discipline A-catalog G3 already flags for this exact series.

**CPI splice — the input side of the real-rate calculation:**

| Series | Base | Window | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| CPI-IW (Industrial Workers) | 1949→1960→1982→2001→**2016** (current, effective Sept 2020) | Longest continuously-revised retail-price series, Labour Bureau | `labourbureau.gov.in/CPI` |
| CPI-Combined (Rural+Urban) | **2011=100** | The MOSPI headline "CPI" since 2011, the standard real-rate deflator input (A-catalog H1) | `esankhyiki.mospi.gov.in`, `cpi.mospi.gov.in` |
| CPI-Combined, new series | **2024=100**, COICOP 2018 classification | Released starting **2026-02-12**, replacing the 2011 base, driven by the 2023-24 Household Consumption Expenditure Survey's updated weights | Same portals, new series tagged distinctly |

**The construction rule (already the catalog's own rule, restated for this seat specifically)**:
any real-rate series spanning **2026-02** must decide, per historical date, which CPI base is in
force, and splice across the break rather than concatenate — precisely Part E's own named
"FAILURE MODE: CPI regime break (2026 rebase splice)". Pre-2011, CPI-IW is the only continuously-
available retail index; a genuinely long real-rate series for India therefore itself needs an
**internal CPI-IW→CPI-Combined splice** around 2011-12, a second, nested splice problem beneath
the 2026 one.

**US leg**: **DFII10** (10-year TIPS real yield, FRED, `fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DFII10`) from
**2003-01-02** — already on the principal-machine runsheet as J7 (A-catalog). **Pre-TIPS proxy**:
TIPS only began issuance in 1997, and FRED's constant-maturity series starts 2003; for the
1970s-1990s window, Part D's DS1-DS4 panel already covers the need at JST-panel level (nominal
short rate minus realized CPI, the same "ex-post real rate" JST itself uses) rather than requiring
a separate US proxy — **no clean pre-2003 US market-priced real rate exists free**; FRED's own
`REAINTRATREARAT10Y` is *model-implied* (nominal 10Y minus a survey/model inflation estimate, not
a traded instrument) and must be labeled as such, never given DFII10's market-price confidence.

# Part D — The mathematics of the debt arc (atlas 0.1)

## D1. The one equation that runs the whole cycle

The debt-dynamics identity (an accounting fact, not a model):

    Δb_t = (r_t − g_t)·b_{t−1} − pb_t + sfa_t

b = debt/GDP, r = effective nominal rate on the stock ÷ deflator (real effective rate),
g = real GDP growth, pb = primary balance/GDP (surplus reduces debt), sfa = stock-flow
adjustments (bailouts, valuation effects, skeletons). Everything in this monograph is about who
controls each term and what they will do when b gets large. Three regimes fall out:
- **r > g** (liberalization era, DS3: +1.1pp mean): debt falls only via surpluses or default.
- **r < g** (repression era −4.3pp; post-GFC −1.0pp): debt can melt without surpluses — the
  "painless" arc — IF the state can keep r low, which is precisely financial repression.
- **Fiscal dominance**: when b is high enough that raising r to fight inflation blows up the
  budget, monetary policy loses independence (Sargent-Wallace "unpleasant monetarist arithmetic":
  tight money today ⇒ more debt ⇒ more inflation later). The fiscal theory of the price level is
  the limiting case: the price level becomes the variable that revalues nominal debt to satisfy
  the government budget constraint.

## D2. Repression, formalized as a tax

Reinhart-Sbrancia's "liquidation rate": the real return on government debt when real rates are
negative, times the domestic debt stock held captively (banks via SLR-type rules, pension
mandates, capital controls) = an unlegislated tax on savers. Measurement in our data: share of
country-years with negative real short rates (DS2: 44% in 1945-80, 76% post-GFC) and the
liquidation flow ≈ (−real rate)·(debt/GDP) when negative. India-specific: SLR is an EXPLICIT
captive-holding rule — the repression apparatus is standing infrastructure here, which is why
this seat exists for an Indian book at all.

## D3. Why n<2 and what that permits statistically

DS1: the fiat era contains 4 completed deleveraging arcs in 18 countries; 11 are right-censored
at their 2020 peaks. A process observed to completion ~4 times, under two different monetary
orders, admits NO fitted cycle model (clock test fails catastrophically). What the data DOES
support: (i) era-conditional distributions of investor outcomes (DS4 — computable because
country-years, not arcs, are the unit); (ii) regime CLASSIFICATION (which r−g / repression state
are we in NOW — an observable, not a forecast); (iii) cross-country sign consistency of the
repression→real-asset channel. Hence the seat's Tier C, reduce-only, state-classification-only
design — the statistics permit nothing stronger, and we take nothing stronger.

## D4. The gold-floor arithmetic (L15's budget, derived)

The seat's expression is a floor on the gold sleeve's attribution (0.40–0.50 of the gold
allocation's rationale) plus a slow-debasement tail budget (0.3–0.6%/yr of NAV) with a
conditional lift (+1–2pp) when the fiscal-dominance state is ON (high debt percentile AND
persistent negative real rates AND CB gold-accumulation leg). DS4 is the justification: the
state's real equity mean halves (+4.5% vs +9.5%) but stays positive — so the response is
insurance-sizing arithmetic, not allocation flight: budget ≈ P(state)·E[equity shortfall]·
hedge-effectiveness, evaluated on the pooled priors, floored and capped in the registry. No
optimizer touches it.
