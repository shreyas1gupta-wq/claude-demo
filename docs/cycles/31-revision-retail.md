## A. The earnings-revision cycle, and the register's first DATA-based rejection (entry 3.5)

### A.1 What the cycle claims, and why the claim is not exotic

The earnings-revision cycle is not a hypothesis this register discovers or steelmans against
skepticism — it is one of the oldest, most replicated findings in empirical asset pricing, sitting
adjacent to (and, on some readings, a co-parent of) the price-momentum finding this ladder runs as
its central EDGE (`L3_momentum_composite`, `config/ladder.yaml`). The claim, stated plainly: sell-side
analysts forecasting a company's forward EPS do not revise all at once when new information arrives —
they revise **partially and then keep revising in the same direction** over subsequent weeks and
months, so a stock whose consensus estimate is being raised tends to keep rising in price alongside
it, not because the market is slow to price the first revision, but because the *next* revision has
not happened yet and is itself forecastable from the pattern already observed. This is the
**anchoring mechanism** the Atlas names directly (`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` line 110: "analyst revisions
trend (anchoring)"): analysts, like the investors who read them, anchor on their own prior estimate
and adjust insufficiently, so the fully-updated forecast a rational analyst would publish immediately
instead arrives in a trickle of serially-correlated revisions — a **behavioral underreaction claim
about forecasters**, structurally identical to the underreaction claim the momentum composite already
carries a decay-survival argument for (`docs/cycles/01-momentum-reversal.md`, cross-referenced),
applied one step upstream, to the professional intermediaries whose job is to process that news.

### A.2 The evidence, at strength

#### A.2.1 Givoly & Lakonishok (1979, 1980) — the founding result

**Givoly, Dan & Lakonishok, Josef (1979), "The Information Content of Financial Analysts' Forecasts
of Earnings: Some Evidence on Semi-Strong Inefficiency,"** *Journal of Accounting and Economics*
1(3): 165–185 **[Verified]**, is the founding empirical result: computing abnormal stock returns in
the months surrounding revisions in analysts' consensus earnings forecasts, the paper finds the
direction of a forecast revision itself carries genuine information content — months in which the
consensus is revised upward are, on average, followed by positive abnormal returns, and downward
revisions by negative ones, a pattern the paper reads as evidence against semi-strong market
efficiency with respect to this specific, professionally-intermediated information channel. The
companion paper, **Givoly, Dan & Lakonishok, Josef (1980), "Financial Analysts' Forecasts of
Earnings: Their Value to Investors,"** *Journal of Banking and Finance* 4(3): 221–233 **[Verified]**,
extends the same evidence base toward a direct practitioner question — do these forecasts, and their
revisions, carry enough value to justify an investor paying attention to them — and answers yes, on
the 1970s US data available. Together the two papers establish, a full generation before this
register's own decay-survival vocabulary existed, exactly the object atlas row 3.5 names: a forecast-
revision *signal*, not merely a forecast-*level* one, with a direction that predicts subsequent
returns.

#### A.2.2 Stickel (1991) — the drift, isolated

**Stickel, Scott E. (1991), "Common Stock Returns Surrounding Earnings Forecast Revisions: More
Puzzling Evidence,"** *The Accounting Review* 66(2): 402–416 **[Verified]**, sharpens Givoly-
Lakonishok's aggregate finding into this cycle's own name: stock prices continue drifting in the
direction of a forecast revision for an **extended period after** it, rather than jumping instantly to
a new equilibrium — the "drift," not "jump," signature Chan-Jegadeesh-Lakonishok's later work (§A.2.3)
formalizes jointly with price momentum, and this program's own house style (`docs/cycles/
01-momentum-reversal.md`) already treats as the generic tell of underreaction. Stickel's reading — the
market under-reacts to the revision itself, not merely the news it is based on — is the direct
empirical anchor for the Atlas's "anchoring" gloss: the market, like the analyst, adjusts to new
information in increments rather than all at once.

#### A.2.3 Chan, Jegadeesh & Lakonishok (1996) — the earnings-momentum leg, formalized alongside price momentum

**Chan, Louis K.C.; Jegadeesh, Narasimhan & Lakonishok, Josef (1996), "Momentum Strategies,"**
*Journal of Finance* 51(5): 1681–1713 **[Verified]**, is the paper this seat's decay-survival argument
must reckon with most directly, since it tests the revision claim against the same sample as the
price-momentum claim this ladder runs as its central EDGE. Its central result: **both past price
return and past earnings surprise independently predict large subsequent drifts, each surviving after
controlling for the other** — risk, size, and book-to-market explain neither — and, tellingly,
**analysts' own forecasts respond sluggishly to past news**, most pronounced for the worst-performing
stocks, the underreaction-by-forecasters mechanism §A.1 names, demonstrated directly. The finding that
price and earnings-revision momentum are **partially independent, both real, jointly stronger than
either alone** is the fact this seat's rejection must be read against: a **partially distinct** signal,
correlated with but not subsumed by `L3_momentum_composite`'s own construction — exactly why the
Atlas's disposition ("price momentum partially proxies it," line 110) is a **partial** proxy, not a
full substitute, and why §A.5 states the forgone alpha honestly rather than waving it away.

#### A.2.4 The anchoring mechanism, stated as a decay-survival argument

CONTRACT §5 requires, for every signal, a written answer to "why does this survive being known?" —
this cycle's honest answer sits closer to momentum's own answer (`docs/cycles/01-momentum-reversal.md`)
than to a pure information-gap anomaly's: analysts are human forecasters operating under real
institutional constraints — reputational risk in moving a number too far from the last-published
figure, career risk in being the outlier revision that turns out wrong, and genuine uncertainty about
how much of a single data point (one quarter's beat or miss) to extrapolate — all of which predict
**partial, serially-correlated adjustment** rather than one-shot Bayesian updating, for reasons that
persist regardless of how many buy-side quants have read Givoly-Lakonishok. This is a **structural/
behavioral mechanism persistent under crowding** (CONTRACT §5, category (i)) in the same family as
momentum's own diffusion-of-information argument — which is exactly why this cycle is not rejected
on mechanism grounds anywhere in this chapter. The rejection that follows (§A.4) is argued entirely
on a different axis.

### A.3 The modern record: crowding, and the decay this program's own governing principle demands

CONTRACT §5's governing principle — McLean & Pontiff (2016): anomalies decay ~26% out-of-sample,
~58% post-publication; Chordia, Subrahmanyam & Tong on attenuation as arbitrage capital arrives —
applies here with unusual force, because unlike issuance timing
(`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.2.6, an incentive no one can arbitrage away) the earnings-revision
signal **is** a pure information-gap anomaly of exactly the kind crowding is built to compress: once
"buy stocks with rising consensus estimates" is a known, mechanically-computable rule on a data feed
every institutional desk can buy, no capacity limit, compensated risk, or institutional constraint
keeps large capital from trading it toward its transaction-cost floor. Consistent with this,
estimate-revision signals sit among the oldest, most commercially packaged factor categories in the
industry — the underlying consensus infrastructure itself (the Institutional Brokers' Estimate
System, I/B/E/S, founded 1976, now distributed by LSEG/Refinitiv **[Verified: existence and 1976
founding corroborated across independent sources; exact modern ownership chain [VERIFY]]**) has been
commercially exploitable for roughly five decades — a crowding runway longer than almost any other
signal on this ladder. **The specific out-of-sample decay magnitude for the estimate-revision signal
in isolation** — distinct from price momentum, with which Chan-Jegadeesh-Lakonishok's own paper shows
it correlated but not identical — **is not separately isolated in any source this session's search
reached [VERIFY: a dedicated post-2010 decay estimate specific to analyst-revision momentum, distinct
from the generic McLean-Pontiff average and from momentum's own decay estimate at
`L3_momentum_composite`]**. What the search did surface is recent crowding-methodology work modeling
alpha decay against assets chasing a signal, finding mechanical, price-based factors (momentum,
reversal) fit a hyperbolic crowding-decay model cleanly while judgment-based factors (value, quality)
do not **[VERIFY: a December-2025 arXiv preprint, not yet peer-reviewed at the time of search;
directionally suggestive of where estimate-revision plausibly sits, not a pinned magnitude for this
factor]**. The honest position, consistent with this program's refusal to fabricate an unverified
number: the mechanism is not in question, but its **magnitude**, on 2026 data, in a market this
crowded this long, is very plausibly compressed well below its 1979–1996 published levels — the
generic McLean-Pontiff prior this register applies as a placeholder everywhere a signal-specific
estimate is unavailable (`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.2.6 uses the identical logic for L7).

### A.4 India's free-source wall: why a real cycle gets no seat here

#### A.4.1 What a "consensus estimate" actually is, and why it is a paid object by construction

The object §A.2's entire literature is built on — a **consensus estimate** — is not a single
analyst's published number; it is a constructed aggregate, computed by a data vendor from a defined,
continuously-maintained panel of contributing sell-side analysts' individual forecasts, refreshed
against every new revision, and published with a **revision history** (the object the cycle's entire
predictive content lives in — not the consensus level, but its trend). Building it from scratch
requires exactly the infrastructure I/B/E/S was built to supply in 1976: standing relationships with
contributing brokerages, a maintained analyst-to-stock coverage map, a normalization convention across
analysts who define "earnings" differently, and a continuously-updated revision time series, not
merely a current snapshot. **In India, this exact object is a commercial product, not a public one.**
Refinitiv/LSEG's I/B/E/S, Bloomberg's BEst, Visible Alpha, and S&P Capital IQ's consensus-estimates
modules all carry India coverage — every one a paid institutional subscription with no free, public
equivalent at comparable granularity or history. Domestic aggregators (Trendlyne, Capitaline) occupy
the same territory, publishing analyst-estimate summaries behind a paywall beyond a shallow snapshot
**[VERIFY: precise current free-tier limits, not independently re-tested this session]**. No NSE/BSE,
RBI, or MOSPI series compiles consensus estimates, because doing so is not a statistical-agency
function anywhere — it is everywhere a private commercial service built on standing broker
relationships, exactly the kind of paid aggregator CONTRACT §1's free-source list (bhavcopy, RBI
DBIE, MOSPI, CCIL, BIS, IMF, FRED, World Bank, AMFI, NSDL, WGC, exchange filings, Kaggle/HuggingFace)
was never going to include. This is not a gap this program failed to find a workaround for; it is a
category of data with no free substitute anywhere, by the nature of what it is.

#### A.4.2 The two free partial proxies, and why each measures a DIFFERENT object

Atlas row 3.5 itself names the honest partial workaround: "price momentum partially proxies it." Two
free, bhavcopy-adjacent constructions deserve to be examined directly, and both, examined honestly,
measure something related but **not the same object** as the revision path itself.

**(i) Result-day price reactions.** NSE/BSE bhavcopy freely records the price reaction on and after a
company's quarterly results — an "earnings surprise" reading, computable at zero cost, already
partially inside `L3_momentum_composite`'s own realized-return-drift response. But an earnings
*surprise* is a **point measurement**: a one-time reaction to a single release against whatever
expectation (often a crude proxy — year-over-year growth, a trend extrapolation — in the absence of a
true consensus) the market is presumed to have held. The earnings-**revision** cycle's predictive
content, by contrast, lives in a **process**: a professionally-intermediated consensus being revised,
then revised again in the same direction, over the weeks *between* results — precisely the continuing
drift Stickel (1991) isolates **after** the initial revision, not the reaction to it. A surprise
measure answers "did this quarter beat or miss," a different question from "is the consensus still
trending," and no bhavcopy engineering converts the first into the second, because the second requires
observing the forecasting community's own sequential behavior, which bhavcopy — a trade record, not a
forecast record — was never built to capture.

**(ii) Guidance tone and management commentary.** A second free-adjacent proxy: scoring the
qualitative tone of earnings-call transcripts and management commentary, freely available from
exchange filings, as a sentiment-direction signal correlated with subsequent analyst moves. Genuinely
interesting research in its own right, but it fails the substitution test from the opposite side:
guidance tone is **unstructured commentary from one interested party** (the company, with every
incentive to manage tone strategically), not a **cross-sectional aggregate of independent professional
forecasts** — the object whose serial correlation §A.2's literature actually tests. A tone score might
correlate with subsequent revisions (a plausible, untested India question, outside this chapter's
scope), but correlating with the revision path is not measuring it; substituting one for the other
would smuggle a different signal in under this cycle's name — the "different object" failure mode
CONTRACT §5's decay-survival discipline and this program's own house style (`docs/cycles/
29-issuance.md` §A.2.5's care to keep the calendar-time construction honest) exist to catch.

### A.5 The honest cost-of-constraint accounting

CONTRACT §12 instructs research agents to state provenance and confidence honestly; the same
discipline applies in reverse to a rejection — stating what is **forgone**, not merely avoided.
§A.2–A.3 establish a signal with a five-decade literature, a mechanism this program's decay-survival
framework accepts (§A.2.4), and a partial overlap with the momentum composite this ladder already
runs — so the honest position is not "no cost," but **"a real, stated cost, paid anyway."** What is
forgone: (i) the **incremental** alpha Chan-Jegadeesh-Lakonishok
show survives *after controlling for price momentum* — the paper's own central finding is that
earnings-surprise-based momentum is not fully subsumed by price momentum, so whatever share of that
incremental effect India's own market carries is left entirely unharvested, not merely under-
harvested; (ii) the **faster signal-refresh** a true revision-trend state would offer relative to
price momentum's own construction, since a consensus estimate can move on new information (a
competitor's results, a sector data point, a currency move) between a covered company's own results
dates, while price momentum and bhavcopy-based surprise measures are necessarily anchored to the
covered company's own trade prices and results calendar; and (iii) the **cross-sectional breadth**
argument — a true consensus-revision state could in principle be computed for every name with
analyst coverage, including names where price momentum's own signal is weak or noisy for idiosyncratic
reasons, a genuinely separate information channel this design simply does not have access to. None of
these three is a large number in absolute terms — this is a Band-3 intra-cycle state (τ½ 12–24 months
by analogy to L7's own seat, `config/ladder.yaml`), never a book-defining signal even in markets where
it is fully tradable — but stating "small" honestly is different from stating "zero," and this
chapter's job is the former.

### A.6 Why the desk keeps the rule anyway

Given a real, if modest, forgone-alpha cost, CONTRACT §1's free-source rule is worth defending on its
own terms. Three reasons, stated as the Contract itself frames them (§1, §12) rather than invented
here: **(i) auditability.** Every signal must be independently reconstructible by anyone this program
trains — the "Cycle School" premise the partDH ledger's Part F names directly — from sources that do
not require a standing subscription this desk's team (principal + Claude, CONTRACT §3) may not always
hold, and that a future auditor should be able to re-pull without negotiating a vendor contract first.
A signal whose entire input is a paid feed cannot be checked by anyone without that feed — a fragility
this program's evidentiary discipline (purged CV, pre-registration, provenance tables) is built to
avoid. **(ii) replicability by design, not by accident.** The free-source list was chosen so the
*design itself* survives a change in vendor relationships, budget, or team composition — a program
quietly depending on one paid feed's continued pricing is a bet on a counterparty CONTRACT §3's frozen
team was written to avoid needing. **(iii) where paid-feed edges concentrate.** The capital best
positioned to arrive fastest against any signal is, definitionally, the capital that can afford the
feed the signal requires — a signal gated behind a subscription every well-capitalized desk already
holds is, by construction, traded by exactly the crowd most likely to have compressed it to its cost
floor, and this cycle's own fifty-year commercial-availability runway (§A.3) is circumstantial evidence
of precisely that dynamic. Keeping the rule is a bet that the free-data constraint steers this program
away from the crowded end of the anomaly spectrum CONTRACT §5 warns against — stated here, not assumed.

### A.7 A new verdict type: REJECT FOR DATA, distinguished from what came before

This register has, before this pairing, produced two other ways for a cycle to leave the ladder
without a seat, and this entry is neither of them — the distinction is worth stating with precision,
because conflating the three would corrupt the register's own audit trail.

**Evidence-based REJECT** — a claim tested against data or theory and **found wanting on its own
terms.** `docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md`'s verdict on Dalio's Big Cycle, Modelski's long cycles, and
Turchin's structural-demographic theory is exactly this: "REJECT as any input" — not because the
phenomena are uninteresting, but because none clears CONTRACT §4's clock test (n≈3–5 contested counts,
decade-wide targets, a different mechanism per instance), and the register's own frequency sweep
(`research/cycles/kitchin-juglar/partAB-theory-cases.md` §A.4, §B.2 — KJ1/KW1/KW2/CS1/BC1) empties
Kondratieff's claimed 45–60-year band just as directly. In both cases, **the claim itself is the thing
that fails**, tested on its own terms and found not to hold the shape it claimed. **FOLD** — an object
real, well-evidenced, and **already seated** under a different, modern name, so a new claim wearing an
old label buys no new budget line. Juglar's 1862 priority claim (the sibling monograph, Part B) is the
clean example: the 7–11-year credit-investment fluctuation Juglar dated qualitatively is not false —
it is precisely what `L10_credit_block` and `L11_capex_cycle` already measure on modern evidence, so
the correct disposition is "recognize the duplicate and retire the second name," not reject or admit
as new. **REJECT FOR DATA**, this entry's own contribution, differs in kind from both: **the claim is
not tested and found wanting — it is not tested at all, by policy, because the instrument required to
test it honestly is not free.** Nothing in §A.2–A.4 argues post-revision drift fails a clock test, a
mechanism test, or duplicates an existing seat; the argument is entirely that CONTRACT §1's data
constraint, applied as written, forecloses this signal's construction in India today. This is a
constraint-based exclusion, not a merit-based one — the honesty §A.5 supplies: state the forgone
alpha, do not disguise a data-availability decision as an evidentiary one, and keep a concrete
**revisit trigger** — a free, auditable India consensus-estimate source appearing (checked at the
annual loop per `docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` §17) — rather than the vague "someday" a merit-based rejection
has no need of, since it does not expect the claim to change; a data-based one waits for the
constraint, not the claim, to change.

## B. The retail-participation wave (entry 3.6, candidate H57)

### B.1 The mechanism: new-cohort enthusiasm as pro-cyclical capital

#### B.1.1 The behavioral base rate — Barber & Odean's individual-investor record

The retail-participation wave's mechanism does not need to be invented for India; it is a
specialization of one of the most thoroughly documented findings in household finance. **Barber, Brad
M. & Odean, Terrance (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth: The Common Stock Investment
Performance of Individual Investors,"** *The Journal of Finance* 55(2): 773–806 **[Verified]**,
studies 66,465 households at a large US discount brokerage, 1991–1996, and finds a stark, monotonic
relationship between trading activity and net performance: the households that traded most actively
earned an average annual return of roughly **11.4%**, against the market's own roughly **17.9%** over
the same window, and individual investors overall underperformed a value-weighted market index by
roughly **3.7% annually [Verified]** — a gap the paper attributes centrally to **overconfidence**
driving excessive trading, with transaction costs alone tracing to a further roughly **1.1%** of
annualized underperformance **[Verified]**. Two companion papers extend the same finding along the
two axes this cycle's own mechanism needs. **Odean, Terrance (1998), "Are Investors Reluctant to
Realize Their Losses?,"** *The Journal of Finance* 53(5): 1775–1798 **[Verified]** — studying roughly
10,000 accounts at the same brokerage — documents the **disposition effect**: individual investors
show a strong, systematic preference for realizing winning positions and holding losing ones, exactly
backwards from the tax-efficient rule and directly informative for reading how a losing cohort behaves
under pressure (§B.1.3). **Odean, Terrance (1999), "Do Investors Trade Too Much?,"** *The American
Economic Review* 89(5): 1279–1298 **[Verified]** formalizes the excessive-trading claim directly:
individual investors, overconfident about the precision of their own information, trade more than a
rational-expectations model predicts, and pay for that overconfidence in lower net returns — the
US-market foundation this program treats, per CONTRACT §12, as a cross-country Tier-B-at-best prior
pending India-specific confirmation, not a settled India fact.

#### B.1.2 The flow side — Frazzini & Lamont's "dumb money," graded honestly

Where Barber-Odean read individual accounts directly, **Frazzini, Andrea & Lamont, Owen A. (2008),
"Dumb Money: Mutual Fund Flows and the Cross-Section of Stock Returns,"** *Journal of Financial
Economics* 88(2): 299–322 **[Verified — also NBER Working Paper 11526, 2005]**, reads the same
behavior at the level of aggregate mutual-fund flows: money flows into funds holding stocks with high
recent returns and valuations, and — the central finding — this flow-chasing **reduces long-run
wealth** for the retail investors reallocating, because the stocks flows chase subsequently
underperform the stocks flows exit. The provocative title deserves grading, not just repeating:
"dumb money" is a claim about **average, aggregate** flow-timing behavior, not that every investor is
unsophisticated, and the paper's own robustness checks (separating uninformed chasing from flows that
correlate with manager skill) are what make the finding durable rather than a caricature. The honest
reading is narrow: **in aggregate, retail capital reallocates toward recent strength and away from
recent weakness, at a cost to the investors doing it** — the flow-side mirror of Barber-Odean's
account-level finding, and together this cycle's US-literature foundation for treating "new cohorts
chase what has just worked" as documented, cross-country-corroborated behavior, not a stereotype.

#### B.1.3 Mean reversion after losses — the cohort learns by losing

The mechanism this atlas entry names as its second leg — "mean-reverts after losses" — is where this
cycle's own alpha-decay logic takes an unusual, almost literal form: **the decay operates on people,
not on a signal.** Every anomaly on this ladder eventually decays as informed capital arbitrages it
away (CONTRACT §5); this cycle's analogous decay mechanism is that **the specific cohort of retail
participants who entered during a wave's euphoric phase experiences realized losses, and a meaningful
share of that cohort subsequently reduces or exits participation** — not because anyone published a
paper telling them the strategy does not work, but because they personally lived the drawdown. This is
directly, repeatedly documented in India's own regulatory record (§B.2.3 below): SEBI's own studies
find the overwhelming majority of individual F&O participants lose money, and — the honest, two-sided
complication this program's own house style demands (`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.2.5's insistence
on stating counter-evidence) — **a majority of loss-making traders nonetheless continue trading rather
than exiting immediately**: SEBI's own FY22–FY24 study finds more than **75%** of loss-making traders
continued participating even after two consecutive loss-making years **[Verified — SEBI's September
2024 updated study, per §B.2.3]**, while new-entrant cohorts specifically show sharper attrition —
first-time entrants fell by roughly **40%** from FY25 to FY26 following the 2024–25 curbs
**[VERIFY: precise cohort definition and the causal share attributable to curbs versus the cohort's
own experienced losses, which this session's search could not cleanly separate]**. The honest
synthesis: the mean-reversion mechanism is real but **slower and less complete than a simple
"burned once, gone forever" story would predict** — existing participants are stickier than new
entrants, and the wave's own contraction (§B.2.4) shows up more clearly in new-account growth **rates**
and aggregate turnover than in a clean, one-for-one cohort exodus. This is precisely why H57's own
design (§B.3) reads **growth rates**, not levels, and treats the mean-reversion claim as a documented
tendency rather than a mechanical clock.

#### B.1.4 The VRP-compression channel

The cycle's third leg — flagged already in `docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` §12's volatility-cycle table
("retail-F&O compression watch = H57") — concerns the **variance risk premium** (VRP): implied
volatility (what options buyers pay) tends to exceed subsequently realized volatility on average, a
persistent premium option **sellers** collect. A large wave of new retail participants entering the
options market changes who sits on each side of that premium, in ways this program's house style
requires reading carefully rather than asserting by narrative. Two threads, held distinct. First,
**India-specific evidence the VRP itself is real and priced even under heavy retail participation**:
"Dynamics of Variance Risk Premium: Evidence from India," *Journal of Economics and Business* (2020)
**[Verified: title, venue, July-2020 date; full author list [VERIFY]]**, tests and rejects the
hypothesis that variance risk might go unpriced in a retail-heavy market, finding realized (continuous)
variance does the forecasting work — evidence the premium exists, not yet evidence its **magnitude
compresses or expands with the participation cycle specifically**, which remains H57's own open
question. Second, a separate **US-market** finding worth reading for mechanism, not magnitude:
**Dew-Becker, Ian & Giglio, Stefano (2025), "The Decline of the Variance Risk Premium: Evidence from
Traded and Synthetic Options,"** Chicago Fed Working Paper 2025-17 **[Verified]**, documents US
equity-index options' historically negative CAPM alpha compressing to near zero over roughly 15 years
— but the paper's preferred explanation is **intermediary balance-sheet constraint**, not retail flow,
which argues against reading it as evidence of a retail-driven channel. **The honest position:** a
retail wave compressing or expanding the VRP is *plausible in direction*, consistent with a VRP that
genuinely exists to be compressed and a retail options explosion (§B.2.3) large enough to matter to
someone's risk-bearing capacity — but no India-specific study links the two directly, and the US
evidence that exists points to a different mechanism. H57's VRP-compression watch (§B.3) is therefore
registered as a **monitored hypothesis**, not a demonstrated channel.

### B.2 India's record, with verified figures

#### B.2.1 The 2004–07 wave — the cash-market precedent

India's retail-participation cycle did not begin with demat accounts or F&O; the 2003–08 cash-market
bull run is its first fully-observed instance in the post-liberalization era, and its shape — euphoric
entry, a sharp terminal crash, a slow account-opening hangover — is the template every later wave
repeats. The Sensex rose from a 25 April 2003 low of **2,924** to an all-time high of **21,206.77 on
10 January 2008 [Verified]** (the 2007–08 IPO top-tick, including Reliance Power's record-subscribed
listing, is L7's own case record, `docs/cycles/29-issuance.md`, cross-referenced not repeated). The
euphoria's account-opening signature is directly measurable: NSDL alone added roughly **12.78 lakh**
new demat accounts between June 2007 and January 2008 — the final run-up to the peak — against
roughly **2 lakh** in the five months after the crash began **[Verified — a roughly 85% collapse in
the opening rate within one calendar year]**; CDSL shows the identical shape, almost **10 lakh** fresh
accounts over the same window **[Verified]**. The crash was severe: the Sensex fell roughly **63%
during 2008** to under **7,700**, not durably reclaiming its January-2008 high until **2013 — roughly
five years later [Verified]**. This is the mechanism (§B.1.1–B.1.3) observed directly, a decade before
SEBI's own F&O loss studies (§B.2.3) formalized the same pattern at trader level: euphoric entry
concentrated at the top, followed by a collapse in new participation tracking the market's own
collapse almost exactly — not a full account-level exodus (demat accounts are rarely formally closed),
but a clear, dateable slowdown in new entrants precisely when the wave's own logic predicts one.

#### B.2.2 The 2020–24 demat surge — verified figures

The 2020–24 wave dwarfs 2004–07 in scale and is far better measured, since NSDL and CDSL both publish
granular, free, monthly account-count data throughout. Total demat accounts stood at roughly **4.1
crore as of March 2020 [Verified]**, immediately pre-pandemic — a base essentially unchanged from the
slow growth regime prevailing since the 2008 hangover **[VERIFY: precise pre-2020 annual growth rate,
recalled from secondary sources, not re-derived from a primary series]**. From that base, the total
surged to **15.13 crore by 31 March 2024** (CDSL: over **11.56 crore**; NSDL: over **3.57 crore**)
**[Verified]**, with FY23–24 alone adding over **3.69 crore** new accounts, a **32.71%** single-year
growth rate **[Verified]**, and the total crossing **17.1 crore by August 2024 [Verified]** — a roughly
**4×** expansion of the national account base in under five years, concentrated overwhelmingly at
CDSL, which overtook NSDL's earlier leadership across this window **[Verified]**. This is the
cleanest, most directly free-data-observable instance of §B.1.1's mechanism operating at national
scale: pandemic-era at-home time, a V-shaped recovery from the March-2020 low, zero-commission
discount-broker entry (Zerodha, Groww, Upstox and peers), and smartphone-app/social-media distribution
converting India's traditionally low household participation rate (§B.2.6) into the fastest four-year
account-growth run in the market's history.

#### B.2.3 The F&O boom — retail share, and the regulator's own loss arithmetic

The 2020–24 wave's most consequential expression was a parallel explosion in retail participation in
**equity index options** — the segment §B.1.4's VRP-compression leg is most directly concerned with.
Individual investors' average daily traded value in index options grew at a compound annual growth
rate of roughly **82% over five years [Verified]**, and by September 2025 individuals accounted for
roughly **39.1% of the premium paid** to trade equity options nationally — a **17-year high
[Verified]**. SEBI's own studies on the resulting outcomes are the single most load-bearing,
regulator-published figures this seat can build on, and both vintages deserve stating precisely rather
than blended. The **January 2023 study**, covering **FY22**, found **89%** of individual F&O traders
incurred losses, averaging roughly **₹1.1 lakh** per lossmaking trader **[Verified]**. The **updated
September 2024 study**, extending to the full **FY22–FY24** period across over **1 crore** individual
traders, found the loss rate **risen to 93%**, averaging roughly **₹2 lakh** per trader with
**aggregate losses exceeding ₹1.8 lakh crore over three years [Verified]**; the top **3.5%** of
loss-makers (roughly **4 lakh** traders) averaged losses of roughly **₹28 lakh each**, while only about
**1%** earned profits exceeding ₹1 lakh after costs **[Verified]**, against proprietary desks and
FPIs, which recorded gross FY24 trading profits of roughly **₹33,000 crore and ₹28,000 crore
respectively [Verified]** — the same "institutional desks on the other side of this book" framing D12
(§2) already used against an options-harvesting sleeve. **New-trader detail, directly relevant to
§B.1.3's mean-reversion mechanism:** SEBI classified roughly **42 lakh** traders as first-time FY24
entrants (nearly half of all active traders that year), of whom **92.1% incurred losses [Verified]** —
the freshest cohort losing even above the segment's already-high aggregate, precisely the population
this cycle predicts should be most exposed and most likely to reduce participation afterward.

#### B.2.4 The 2024–25 SEBI curbs and the post-curb contraction — verified figures and dates

SEBI's response arrived through a specific, dated circular rather than diffuse policy drift, and the
program's own house style (`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.3.3: "a regulator acting IS a froth
reading") applies with unusual directness, since this curb set was explicitly framed around investor
protection against exactly the loss pattern §B.2.3 documents. **SEBI's circular dated 1 October 2024**
(SEBI/HO/MRD/TPD-1/P/CIR/2024/132) **[Verified]** introduced six measures, two dated precisely:
**lot-size rationalization** to a minimum **₹15 lakh** contract value (new contracts from **21
November 2024**) **[Verified]**; and **weekly-expiry rationalization** to one benchmark index per
exchange — NSE dropping Bank Nifty, Nifty Financial Services, Nifty Midcap Select and Nifty Next 50;
BSE dropping BANKEX and Sensex 50 — effective **20 November 2024 [Verified]**; further measures removed
calendar-spread margin benefits on expiry day from **1 February 2025** and began intraday
position-limit monitoring from **1 April 2025 [Verified]**. **The post-curb contraction is large and
multiply corroborated.** Index-options contract volumes across NSE and BSE fell **52% during FY2025–
26** (roughly **62.8bn contracts against 131.4bn** the year before) **[Verified]**; total options
turnover fell **15% to roughly $1,909bn in 2025 from $2,247bn in 2024 [Verified]**; annual index-
options premium turnover recorded its **first decline in eight years in FY25** (roughly **₹136tn from
₹138tn**), with monthly average premium turnover falling from roughly **₹12.4tn** (FY25's first eight
months) to **₹9.1tn** (Dec 2024–Mar 2025) **[Verified]**; individual investors' own F&O premium
turnover fell roughly **11% year-on-year** (Dec 2024–May 2025), with unique individual traders down
roughly **20% year-on-year [Verified]**. NSE's own F&O turnover fell roughly **18% during FY26**, even
as combined notional turnover rose modestly (**4.3%**, to **₹110.4 quadrillion**) on continuing
institutional/proprietary activity **[Verified]** — a genuine, dated contraction concentrated in the
retail-heavy premium and contract-count metrics H57 (§B.3) is built to read, not a notional-turnover
story institutional flow could be masking.

#### B.2.5 SIP culture as the STRUCTURAL, non-wave component

This chapter's — and H57's algorithm's (`partDH-verdict-routing.md` Part E, Step 1) — single most
important design discipline is refusing to blend two genuinely different retail phenomena into one
state. The **Systematic Investment Plan (SIP)** channel — monthly, recurring, mandate-driven
mutual-fund contributions, reported free and monthly by AMFI — is **structural**, not cyclical: it has
grown essentially without interruption through both boom and bust, a durable behavioral/institutional
shift (payroll-linked, auto-debited, advisor-recommended) rather than a cohort chasing a hot market.
The scale is now a macro liquidity fact in its own right: SIP contributions averaged roughly **₹24,113
crore/month in FY2024–25**, up from **₹16,602 crore** the year before **[Verified]**; March 2025 grew
**34.53% year-on-year to ₹25,926 crore [Verified]**; by mid-to-late 2025, monthly inflows repeatedly
set fresh records — **₹28,464cr (July)**, **₹29,361cr (September)**, **₹29,529cr (October)**, alongside
total mutual-fund AUM reaching an all-time high of roughly **₹79.9 lakh crore** that same month
**[Verified]**; SIP AUM reached roughly **₹13.35 lakh crore by March 2025** (**20.31%** of industry
AUM), with contributing accounts at roughly **8.11 crore** rising to **9.11 crore by July 2025
[Verified]**. **The design consequence, stated as a rule:** SIP counts and AUM are tracked as free
**context** but **never blended into H57's own wave-state percentile** (`partDH-verdict-routing.md`
Part E, Step 1) — because SIP flow does **not** spike and crash with sentiment the way direct-equity
account openings and F&O turnover demonstrably do (§B.2.1–B.2.4); letting a rising SIP trend inflate a
"retail euphoria" percentile would mistake durable formal-savings growth for the wave this seat exists
to catch.

#### B.2.6 Where participation stands, 2025–26

Stated honestly rather than assumed: despite the 2020–24 surge, India's household equity-participation
rate remains structurally low. A SEBI-AMFI household survey (with Kantar, roughly 90,000 households,
400 cities, 1,000 villages) found only roughly **9.5%** of Indian households — about **3.21 crore** —
actively participate in securities markets, despite **63%** reporting awareness of at least one
product **[Verified]**; urban participation (roughly **15%**) runs ahead of rural (roughly **6%**),
Delhi (**20.7%**) and Gujarat (**15.4%**) leading **[Verified]**. Roughly **22%** of aware
non-investors intend to begin investing within a year, and SEBI's leadership has floated the investor
base **doubling within three to five years [Verified]** — treated here as directional, not a forecast
this program relies on. Read against the 2024–25 contraction, the honest 2025–26 picture is two-sided:
the F&O-specific euphoria cohort has visibly cooled under regulatory pressure and its own realized
losses, even as the much larger, still-shallow aware-but-non-participating universe represents a
structural growth runway distinct from any wave's boom-bust rhythm — the same distinction §B.2.5 draws
between the wave and the structural (SIP) channel.

### B.3 The H57 design

**(i) The construct, as already registered.** Per `partDH-verdict-routing.md` Part E, H57 is a two-leg
state, read as expanding percentiles on a monthly cadence: **new-demat growth-rate** (NSDL+CDSL
combined, the **rate** of new-account additions, never the level — §B.1.3's own failure-mode warning
against demat-count inflation from multiple accounts per person applies directly here) and **retail
F&O share** (SEBI/NSE's own published individual-investor share of index-option turnover, §B.2.3). AMFI
new-SIP registrations are tracked as a separate, explicitly **structural** series per §B.2.5's own
discipline — read for context, never blended into the wave percentile. **RT1**, the design's own first
validation step (`partDH-verdict-routing.md` Part E, Step 2), checks the state's shape prior against
exactly the public record §B.2 already establishes: a top-decile reading through the 2021–24 euphoric
run, falling sharply through 2025 as the curbs and the loss record bite (§B.2.4) — a sanity check
against the state manufacturing a reading the visible record does not support, in the same spirit as
this program's own resolution-floor discipline (`research/cycles/kitchin-juglar/partAB-theory-cases.md`
§A.4's KJ1 finding).

**(ii) Consumption: a sub-input, never a standalone seat.** The ladder's atlas line is explicit —
"sentiment sub-input to L7 + VRP-compression watch for the option budget" — and this chapter's opening
distinction (never duplicate what `docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` already owns) governs the relationship.
L7's own construct (`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.4) is a two-leg VOLUME/RECEPTION state built around
**issuance and IPO-reception** sentiment — its §A.3.2/§G.1 already document the IPO-specific retail
psychology (allotment-lottery FOMO, subscription chasing, grey-market-premium tracking), the
**narrowest, IPO-specific slice** of the broader wave argued here. H57 is deliberately **not** a
re-derivation: it reads the much broader cash-and-derivatives cohort (demat openings across the entire
market, not merely IPO applicants; F&O turnover share, a separate instrument class from issuance) and
feeds its own wave-percentile into L7's sentiment state as a **second, complementary corroboration** —
a market simultaneously receiving hot paper (L7) **and** drawing a fresh wave of first-time
participants (H57) is a stronger joint reading than either alone, the same "double-confirm" logic L7
already uses with L8's value-spread leg (`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.4.iii), applied here to two
sentiment-adjacent states. H57 draws **zero** independent regime-score budget; its allocation authority
runs entirely through L7's existing `valuation_sentiment` seat (`config/ladder.yaml`), consistent with
CONTRACT §4's Tier-C rule that a state below the clock-test's ≥4-observation bar (this wave has,
honestly, roughly two full India cycles observed with any granularity) may only **reduce** risk, never
add it independently.

**(iii) The VRP-compression watch.** Per §B.1.4's honest grading, H57's second consumption path feeds
the option budget (CONTRACT §3's ≤50%/≤75% notional caps, the swept hedge-ratio parameter) as a
**monitored watch, not a demonstrated pricing input**: when the retail F&O share leg sits at an
expanding-percentile extreme, Stage-2 briefings and the option-desk's own VRP measurement work
(`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` §12's own R2.7 study, cross-referenced and never re-derived here) are flagged to
examine whether realized VRP is compressed relative to its own history — a hypothesis this chapter
registers as **RT2** (`partDH-verdict-routing.md` Part E/F), explicitly not yet confirmed by any
India-specific study linking the two (§B.1.4), and explicitly gated behind that confirmation before it
could ever condition sizing rather than merely inform a briefing.

**(iv) What's data-gated, and what is genuinely free.** Every observable this seat requires is free:
NSDL/CDSL publish monthly account-count data without charge (§B.2.2's figures are drawn entirely from
this reporting); SEBI/NSE publish individual-investor turnover and premium-share splits in periodic
bulletins (§B.2.3); AMFI publishes SIP registration and AUM data monthly (§B.2.5); and SEBI's own loss
studies (§B.2.3) are the free, dated confirmation this program's house style treats as a fresh data
point each recurrence, not a stale citation (`docs/cycles/29-issuance.md` §A.3.3's identical treatment
of SEBI's issuance-side enforcement history). The one item explicitly
**not** admitted at this stage is **RT-D3** — the cohort-loss half-life design (`partDH-verdict-
routing.md` Part F: "how long does a losing cohort stay away? design only") — registered as a future
research question rather than a component of the live construct, because answering it honestly
requires panel-level trader data at a granularity SEBI's own published studies aggregate away, and
this program does not admit a design element it cannot yet build from what is actually public.

## C. The verdict pair

**3.5 — Earnings-revision cycle: REJECT FOR DATA.** The mechanism is real, old, well-evidenced:
Givoly-Lakonishok (1979, 1980) found the founding result, Stickel (1991) isolated the drift, and
Chan-Jegadeesh-Lakonishok (1996) showed the effect survives independently of, and jointly strengthens,
the price momentum this ladder runs as its central EDGE. Nothing in §A.2–A.3 argues the anchoring
mechanism is weak or India-inapplicable; five decades of commercial availability (I/B/E/S since 1976)
have plausibly compressed its tradable magnitude the way crowding compresses any information-gap
anomaly, but the specific decay figure is unverified and flagged as such. The rejection is entirely a
data-constraint finding: a genuine consensus estimate is a paid commercial product everywhere, India
included (I/B/E/S, Bloomberg BEst, Visible Alpha, Capital IQ, and paywalled domestic aggregators), with
no free substitute, because compiling one is a private commercial service by nature. The two free,
bhavcopy-adjacent proxies — result-day surprise and guidance-tone scoring — each measure a genuinely
**different object** than the consensus-revision **process** the literature tests, and substituting
one would smuggle a different signal in under this cycle's name. The forgone alpha is stated, not
hidden: the incremental share CJL show survives after controlling for price momentum, the faster
refresh a true revision state would offer between results dates, and the cross-sectional breadth a
consensus feed could reach where momentum is weak. The rule is kept anyway for auditability,
replicability independent of any vendor, and the Contract's own observation that paid-feed edges
concentrate where crowding lives. **Revisit trigger**: a free, auditable India consensus-estimate
source appearing, checked at the annual loop. This is the register's **fourth verdict type** — distinct
from an evidence-based reject (Kondratieff, war cycles), a fold (Juglar into L10/L11), and context
(zero budget by design): a claim the desk does not dispute, declined because CONTRACT §1's constraint,
applied as written, forecloses honestly building it.

**3.6 — Retail-participation wave: CANDIDATE H57, instrumented, Tier C.** The mechanism specializes
the best-documented finding in household finance — Barber-Odean's underperformance record, Odean's
disposition-effect and overtrading findings, Frazzini-Lamont's "dumb money" result, all US Tier-B-at-
best priors, directionally corroborated by India's own regulatory record. India's chronology is
public at every step this design needs: 2004–07 (Sensex 2,924→21,207; account-opening collapsing
~85% post-peak), 2020–24 (4.1cr→17.1cr demat accounts), the F&O boom (individual premium share a
17-year high of 39.1%; SEBI's studies finding 89% of FY22 F&O traders lose and 93% over FY22–FY24,
₹1.8 lakh crore aggregate losses, 92.1% of new entrants losing), the dated 2024–25 curbs (1 October
2024 circular; changes effective 20–21 November 2024) and the corroborated post-curb contraction
(contracts down 52% in FY26, premium turnover down 15% in 2025, trader counts down ~20% year-on-year).
SIP (₹24,113cr/month FY25 average, still setting records through late 2025) is carried as explicitly
STRUCTURAL, never blended into the wave percentile. Household participation remains structurally
shallow (9.5%, 3.21cr households) even after the surge. H57 enters as a two-leg (new-demat growth-rate;
retail F&O share) expanding-percentile state, a **sentiment sub-input to L7 only** (zero independent
budget, CONTRACT §4's Tier-C reduce-only rule), plus a monitored **VRP-compression watch** for the
option budget — every observable free (NSDL/CDSL, SEBI/NSE, AMFI), with the cohort-loss half-life
question (RT-D3) registered design-only pending panel data this program does not yet have free access
to build.

# Parts D–H — verdicts + routing (atlas 3.5 REJECT-FOR-DATA + 3.6 CANDIDATE H57)

## Part D — The register's verdict taxonomy, completed

3.5 introduces the FOURTH verdict type: REJECT FOR DATA. The others: evidence-based reject
(war cycles, K-waves — the claim fails its bars), fold (the object is real and already seated
under another name), context (real, datable, unbudgeted). A data-reject is different in kind:
the CLAIM MAY WELL BE TRUE (post-revision drift has decades of literature) and the desk
declines it anyway because its inputs violate the free-source rule. The honesty requirement
is the COST ACCOUNTING: the forgone alpha is stated in the chapter, not hidden behind the
rule; and the revisit trigger is concrete (a free consensus source appearing — not "someday").
Why keep the rule: auditability, replicability by anyone the desk trains (the Cycle School's
premise), and the Contract's observation that paid-feed edges concentrate exactly where
crowding lives. The rule is a design choice with a price; both are on the record.

3.6's discipline is the SUB-INPUT rule: H57 feeds L7's sentiment state (with issuance) and
the option-budget's VRP watch — it never stands alone, because participation waves and
issuance froth are the same crowd measured at different counters (the de-dup argument).
