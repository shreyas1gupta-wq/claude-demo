## A. Kondratieff, strongest form first

### A.1 What Kondratieff actually claimed

Nikolai Dmitrievich Kondratiev — director of Moscow's Institute of Conjuncture from 1920, and
by his early thirties one of the Soviet Union's most internationally read economists — published
"The Major Economic Cycles" (Russian, 1925, in the Institute's own journal *Voprosy Konjunktury*)
and a German-language restatement, "Die langen Wellen der Konjunktur," in the *Archiv für
Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik* in 1926 **[Verified: existence, author, 1925/1926 dates,
and the German venue via multiple independent bibliographic sources this session]** — the German
paper became the more widely cited version internationally and was later translated into English
as "The Long Waves in Economic Life" (*Review of Economic Statistics*, 1935) **[recall-verified
on the translation's existence; exact volume/issue — VERIFY]**. The empirical base was, for its
time, unusually careful: wholesale price indices, nominal interest rates, wages, foreign-trade
volumes, and coal and pig-iron production and consumption, assembled across England, France, the
United States and (for some series) Germany, from the 1780s to the early 1920s **[Verified: the
country coverage and series list via multiple independent summaries this session; exact
per-series country coverage — VERIFY]**. From this Kondratiev read **three long waves**, dated by
his own analysis as follows:

| Wave | Rising phase | Falling phase |
|---|---|---|
| I | late 1780s/early 1790s → 1810–17 | 1810–17 → 1844–51 |
| II | 1844–51 → 1870–75 | 1870–75 → 1890–96 |
| III | 1890–96 → 1914–20 | not dated — Kondratiev wrote before it resolved |

**[Verified: this three-wave dating, phase by phase, via multiple independent summaries of the
primary text this session]**. Two features of the original claim are routinely lost in the
label's later life and are worth stating precisely, because Part A.5 needs them precisely.
**First, the third wave has no dated downswing** — Kondratiev was writing in the mid-1920s, before
the wave he himself proposed had completed; every one of his critics since has had to supply that
missing fourth data point from outside his own text, which is a different epistemic act than
confirming a prediction. **Second, and more consequentially, Kondratiev's own tone was
hedged, not prophetic.** He presented the long wave as a working hypothesis inferred from a
statistical regularity, explicitly weaker in evidentiary standing than the shorter business
cycles he also studied — he offered several *candidate* mechanisms without committing firmly to one, and ranked them
rather than picking a winner. His leading candidate was the **periodic renewal and expansion of
long-lived basic capital** — canals, then railways, then the metallic infrastructure of heavy
industry are durable enough that their replacement bunches in time, and that bunching, not any
external shock, was his best guess at why upswings began when they did. Gold-discovery shocks and
the incorporation of new countries and colonial territories into the world market he treated as
reinforcing but secondary; wars and social upheavals he treated as *consequences* of a wave's own
turning points rather than as independent causes of them — an ordering later long-wave writers
(Mandel among them, A.4/A.5(a)) would reverse, treating shocks as the trigger rather than the
symptom **[recall-verified: the hedged tone, the candidate-mechanism list, and the capital-renewal
mechanism's priority among them are a well-established characterization in the secondary
literature; not independently re-extracted from the primary 1926 text this session]**. This is the single most
important fact this chapter can establish before anything else: **the man who gave the wave its
name was less certain about it, and more careful about distinguishing hypothesis from mechanism,
than almost everyone who has invoked his name since.** What the label became — a confident,
50-year, callable clock, sold in market newsletters with specific forward dates attached — is a
different intellectual object from what its author actually put on paper, and A.6 below argues
that gap is not incidental but is close to the whole reason the label survives.

### A.2 His fate

Kondratiev's academic and institutional standing collapsed with startling speed once Stalin's
consolidation began. He was arrested in June or July 1930 **[Verified: arrest timing via
multiple independent sources this session, which disagree narrowly on the exact month]** as part
of a fabricated OGPU case against a supposed "Working Peasants' Party" — a conspiracy invented
by the Soviet secret police, not a real organization — sweeping up Kondratiev alongside other
senior non-Bolshevik economic planners including Vladimir Groman **[Verified: the fabricated
nature of the case and the Groman connection via multiple independent sources this session]**.
Six months into detention, in January 1931, Kondratiev pleaded guilty under pressure to
membership in the invented party and was sentenced to eight years' imprisonment **[Verified via
multiple independent sources this session]**. That sentence did not run its course: on 17
September 1938, at the height of the Great Terror, he was retried and executed by firing squad
at the Kommunarka shooting ground outside Moscow, aged 46 **[Verified: exact execution date,
location, and age via multiple independent sources this session]**. He was rehabilitated twice,
posthumously — first against the 1938 verdict in 1963, and only in 1987, a full 24 years later,
against the original 1931 "Working Peasants' Party" conviction itself **[Verified via multiple
independent sources this session]**.

**Some popular retellings of this story name a specific ideological antagonist: a young Mikhail
Suslov, then training at the Institute of Red Professors and, from 1931, sitting on Party control
commissions that supervised purges in the Urals and Ukraine, is sometimes credited with a role in
the campaign against the Conjuncture Institute's "bourgeois" economists [Verified: Suslov's
biography and his 1931 control-commission role, independently, via multiple sources this session
— but no primary or mainstream secondary source located this session directly connects Suslov by
name to Kondratiev's specific case; treat the "Suslov connection" as unverified narrative
color, not an established fact, and flag it explicitly rather than repeat it as settled — VERIFY]**.
What is well established, and does not need Suslov's name to carry the point, is the **specific
ideological content of the charge against Kondratiev's economics itself**: his finding that
capitalist economies moved through self-renewing long cycles — downswings that eventually gave
way to new upswings through the same mechanisms that produced the prior wave — was in direct
tension with the Marxist-Leninist doctrine, then hardening under Stalin's "Third Period" line,
that capitalism's crises were cumulative and terminal, building toward a final, revolutionary
collapse rather than resolving and renewing **[Verified: this characterization of the doctrinal
conflict via multiple independent sources this session]**. The irony is exact and worth stating
plainly: **the theory that a fifty-year swing "always comes back" was politically dangerous
precisely because Soviet orthodoxy needed the crisis of the moment to be the last one, not one
more turn of a wheel that had turned before.** A cyclical economist, however careful his
hedging, was reporting evidence for capitalism's resilience at the exact moment the state needed
evidence for its exhaustion. Kondratiev's data did not save him from that collision; if anything,
its rigor made the collision harder to paper over.

### A.3 Schumpeter's adoption

The wave's second life began with Joseph Schumpeter's *Business Cycles: A Theoretical,
Historical, and Statistical Analysis of the Capitalist Process* (McGraw-Hill, 1939)
**[recall-verified; widely-cited two-volume work]**, which did two things simultaneously: it
proposed **naming** the long wave after Kondratiev — the first use of "Kondratieff cycle" as a
term of art **[Verified via multiple independent sources this session]** — and it **nested**
three cycle lengths into one multiplicative schema: the short Kitchin inventory cycle
(3–4 years), the medium Juglar investment cycle (7–11 years, roughly the atlas's own credit-cycle
band, 2.1/2.5), and the long Kondratieff wave (which Schumpeter himself dated at roughly 54–60
years) **[Verified: the three-tier nesting and Schumpeter's own period figures via multiple
independent sources this session]**. Schumpeter dated his own first two waves at **1787–1842**
and **1843–roughly the early 1910s** **[Verified: the 1787–1842 figure via multiple independent
sources this session; the second wave's exact terminal year is reported inconsistently across
secondary sources, ranging c.1897–1913 — VERIFY]**, and associated a third wave beginning around
the turn of the century with electricity, steel, and heavy engineering. Unlike Kondratiev,
Schumpeter supplied a confident, named **mechanism**: not price statistics but **clusters of
entrepreneurial innovation** — a "swarm" of interrelated new technologies (cotton textiles and
steam power for the first wave; railways and steel for the second; electricity, chemicals and
the internal-combustion engine for the third) that diffuse through the economy in a burst, driven
by the "creative destruction" of old capital by new. This is the mechanism that gave the
Kondratieff wave its modern intellectual respectability — a real economic story, not just a
statistical artifact — and it is also, Part A.5(b) will show, the mechanism that quietly concedes
the whole case, because a different swarm of innovations explains every wave. Schumpeter also,
notably, dropped Kondratiev's hedge: where the original 1925/26 papers offered candidate
mechanisms tentatively and declined to commit, *Business Cycles* asserts the innovation-cluster
story with the confidence of a settled theoretical result, baptizing what its namesake called a
working hypothesis into something closer to doctrine — a shift in registered certainty this
chapter's A.6 argues is itself part of why the label outlived the caution.

### A.4 The later literature — the PRO case at its modern best

Four further contributions carry the torch, each strengthening a different leg of the claim, and
each deserves to be stated at its strongest before Part A.5 turns on them. **W.W. Rostow**
reworked the dating using relative prices of primary products against manufactures rather than
Kondratiev's broader price/production mix, producing upswings of **1790–1815, 1848–1873, and
1896–1920**, with downswings filling the gaps between (1815–1848, 1873–1896, 1920–1936)
**[Verified: Rostow's price-based dating scheme via this session's search]** — a genuinely
independent re-derivation using a narrower, cleaner series, and one whose turning points land
within a few years of Kondratiev's own for waves I and II. **Jay Forrester's** MIT System
Dynamics group took the boldest step: rather than fit a wave to historical data, they built a
structural model — the "National Model," first published 1976 — in which capital-goods-sector
accelerator dynamics *endogenously generate* a 40–60-year long wave from the physical/investment
structure of a modern economy alone, no external shock required **[Verified: the National Model's
1976 publication and its explicit generation of a 40–60-year cycle via this session's search]**.
The mechanism is specific and worth stating because it is the most falsifiable version of
Schumpeter's story anyone has built: the capital-goods sector must invest in its *own* productive
capacity to meet orders from every other sector, including the surges in its own replacement
demand, and that self-referential ordering process, run through a system-dynamics model with
production and inventory delays, generates an overshoot-and-correct rhythm on a multi-decade
period without needing wars, gold, or any exogenous shock — the closest thing to a rigorous formal
engine under the innovation-cluster story rather than a narrative gesture at one. **Christopher
Freeman and Francisco Louçã's** *As Time Goes By: From the Industrial Revolutions to the
Information Revolution* (Oxford University Press, 2001) **[Verified: title, authors, publisher,
year via this session's search]** is the modern academic synthesis — Schumpeter's technology-
cluster mechanism updated with a **"techno-economic paradigm"** framework: each new cluster of
interrelated technologies demands a matching set of institutions, skills, and regulatory norms,
and the *mismatch* between an old institutional order and a new technology's requirements is what
produces the turbulence at each transition, not the technology alone. That single idea —
institutional mismatch as the transition's driving friction, not the technology cluster by
itself — is Carlota Perez's own direct intellectual inheritance (Part B below), and it is also
Freeman-Louçã's most defensible answer to A.5(b)'s mechanism-heterogeneity critique: if the
claim is "each new paradigm produces institutional turbulence until society catches up," the
mechanism *is* consistent across waves even though the technology is not — a genuinely stronger
formulation than Schumpeter's raw innovation-clustering story, and the one strand of the PRO
case this chapter's Part B treats as worth retaining, in Perez's more developed form, rather than
rejecting alongside Kondratieff's period claim. And **Andrey Korotayev and
Sergey Tsirel's** "A Spectral Analysis of World GDP Dynamics" (*Structure and Dynamics*, 2010)
**[Verified: title, authors, journal, year via this session's search]** is the best *quantitative*
modern defense: applying spectral/periodogram methods to pooled world GDP growth rates,
1870–2007, they report a detectable Kondratieff-length periodicity (roughly 52–53 years)
**significant at the 4–5% level**, alongside weaker but present Juglar and Kitchin signatures
**[Verified: the ~52–53-year period estimate and the 4–5% significance figure via this session's
search]**. That is the PRO case's best modern shot in one sentence: a formal spectral test, on
138 years of the broadest economic series that exists, clears a conventional significance
threshold — barely, but it clears it. Part A.5(b) and A.5(d) explain why a single, barely-passing
periodogram result on a non-independent world series is much thinner evidence than it looks, and
why the desk's own machinery, applied to two different price panels this month, does not find
even that much.

### A.5 The case against, in full

**(a) Dating chaos.** The most basic test any recurring-period claim must pass is that
independent analysts, working with comparable data and honest methods, converge on similar
turning points. The Kondratieff-wave literature fails this test by margins that dwarf any
reasonable measurement uncertainty:

| Wave | Kondratieff (1925/26) | Schumpeter (1939) | Rostow (1978/79, price-based) | Mandel (1980, profit-rate + shocks) | Modern techno-economic (Freeman-Louçã/Perez lineage) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | up 1780s/90s→1810–17; down →1844–51 | 1787–1842 | up 1790–1815; down →1848 | up ~1793–1825 [VERIFY]; down →1847 [VERIFY] | Industrial Revolution, 1771 start (Perez) |
| II | up 1844–51→1870–75; down →1890–96 | 1843→c.1897–1913 [VERIFY] | up 1848–1873; down →1896 | up 1848–1873 (gold discoveries); down →1893 | Age of Steam/Railways, 1829 start |
| III | up 1890–96→1914–20; down undated | begins c.1898, ongoing at time of writing | up 1896–1920; down →1936 | up 1896–1913 (colonization); down 1914–1940 (ends with WWII) | Age of Steel/Electricity, 1875 start |
| IV | (not proposed by K.) | (not proposed by S.) | (not proposed by R. in this scheme) | postwar boom to ~1966; "long downturn" from late 1960s | Age of Oil/Auto/Mass Production, 1908 start |
| V | — | — | — | — | Age of ICT, 1971 start; contested "6th wave" debate live since 2008 |

**[Verified: Kondratiev, Rostow, and the Freeman-Louçã/Perez-lineage figures via this session's
search, as cited in A.1–A.4; Schumpeter's wave II terminal year and Mandel's exact wave-I dates
carry explicit VERIFY flags above where sourcing this session was inconsistent; Mandel's wave-II/
III figures — gold-discovery-led 1848–1873 upswing, 1896–1913 colonization-led upswing, 1914–1940
downswing ending with WWII — independently confirmed this session]**. Waves I and II are
reasonably close across authors — within a decade at either end, arguably inside honest
measurement noise on 19th-century data. **Wave III is where the disagreement becomes decisive**:
Kondratieff leaves the downswing undated; Rostow closes it in 1936; Mandel folds the downswing
into a single 1914–1940 span covering two world wars and the Depression, driven (in his account)
by a falling profit rate that exogenous shocks then interrupt; Schumpeter's third wave, on most
readings, does not close until well into the 1940s or later. That is a **20-plus-year spread** on
where a single wave ends — more than a third of the claimed period itself — before even reaching
the genuinely contested question of how many waves there have been since. The modern
techno-economic lineage adds a wave Kondratieff never saw (ICT, from 1971) and cannot yet agree
whether a sixth has begun. A claim whose most careful proponents cannot agree on the boundaries
of the very instances they are counting is not a dated hypothesis under refinement; it is a
label in search of a fixed referent.

**(b) n≈3, unfalsifiable, and a different mechanism every time.** Even granting the most generous
count — five waves across 250 years, the modern techno-economic tally — the clock test (Contract
§4, ≥4 completed periods) is nominally cleared only by the newest, least-tested reading of the
data, and it is cleared in name only. The deeper problem, identical in structure to the one
`docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md` B.1–B.2 already proves for hegemonic-cycle claims, is that **the
instances are not draws from one process**. Schumpeter's own account supplies the proof: wave I's
mechanism is cotton textiles and steam power; wave II's is railways and steel; wave III's is
electricity, chemicals and the internal-combustion engine; the modern extension adds oil/mass
production and then ICT. Five different technology clusters, five different institutional and
monetary regimes (metallic standard through wave III; managed and then floating fiat money for
waves IV–V), five different geographies of leadership (Britain through wave II, the US
thereafter) — sharing nothing but a common label and a roughly-50-year duration that itself, per
(a), does not hold constant. A sample of five events under five different generating processes is
not a sample of five; as `docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md` B.1 puts it for an exactly analogous
claim, it is five samples of one each. And unlike Turchin's dated 2010 forecast — the one genuine
advance claim in the entire hegemonic-cycle literature that same monograph steelmans — nothing in
the Kondratieff literature offers a comparable pre-registered, dated, falsifiable prediction:
Forrester's National Model came closest, forecasting a depression-scale downturn "probably of the
1990s" **[Verified via this session's search]**, a decade that instead delivered, in the US, the
longest peacetime expansion on record to that point. A framework whose most formal, most
mechanistic modern instantiation produced a specific, dated, and subsequently wrong forecast is
not a framework whose failure to predict is merely unproven — it is a framework with a scored
failure on the books.

**(c) The price-wave-to-growth-wave bait-and-switch.** Kondratieff's own empirical object was
**prices** — wholesale price levels in a metallic-standard world where, over multi-decade
horizons, price levels were roughly stationary: they rose across a wave's upswing and fell across
its downswing, but did not carry a persistent one-directional trend the way post-1971 fiat price
levels do. That stationarity is precisely what made a "price wave" a coherent object to look for
in 1780–1920 data. It is also precisely what the post-1971 fiat era destroys: `docs/cycles/
09-inflation-regimes.md`'s own IR1–IR3 results, run independently of this chapter and cited here
without re-derivation, find not a symmetric, mean-reverting price wave but **two completed
one-directional arcs** — a 52-year rise, 1930 to 1982, and a 38-year fall, 1982 to 2020 — with
81% year-on-year persistence in the underlying inflation *state*. That is not the same statistical
object Kondratieff was measuring; it is a regime, not a wave, and `09-inflation-regimes.md`
itself draws the consequence explicitly: two arcs fail the clock test by an order of magnitude,
and the honest treatment is a persistence gauge feeding L15 and L6, never a timing signal. Modern
long-wave proponents, confronted with fiat-era price data that will no longer cooperate,
routinely and quietly swap the claimed object from a **price wave** to a **growth wave** or an
**innovation wave** — GDP growth rates, patent counts, capital-formation series — a different
empirical claim requiring its own independent evidentiary case, presented under the same 50-year
label as though nothing had changed. Korotayev-Tsirel's own spectral defense (A.4) is itself an
instance of this switch: it tests world GDP growth, not price levels, which is intellectually
legitimate as a *new* hypothesis but dishonest as a *defense of Kondratieff's original claim* — it
is a different theory wearing the original's name and borrowed credibility. The desk's own KW2
trial (below) measures the object Kondratieff actually used — inflation, not growth — and finds
the honest answer directly.

**(d) The desk's own adjacent evidence.** This program does not have to take any side of (a)–(c)
on priors alone; it has now measured the claim twice this month, against two different data
objects, with pre-registered bars set before either run. `research/cycles/commodity-deep/
commodities-RESULTS.md`'s **CS1** trial, run for the commodity-supercycle entry (atlas 1.3),
identified real commodity-price troughs on 165 years of Jacks-panel data at a minimum 15-year
spacing and found **eight troughs since 1870, spaced 15–21 years (median 18)** — twice as many
arcs as the 30–40-year "supercycle" literature claims, because a coarser filter simply cannot
return anything finer than the resolution it is built to find. This chapter's own **KW1** trial
(`research/cycles/longwaves/longwaves-RESULTS.md`), run on the identical chained Jacks index with
the minimum trough-to-trough gap widened to 25 years — a resolution chosen specifically to give
the 45–60-year Kondratieff band room to appear — instead returned troughs at **1855, 1886, 1913,
1939, 1970, 2001**, spacings of **31, 27, 26, 31, 31 years**, with **zero of five spacings**
landing inside the claimed [45, 60]-year window. The pattern across both trials is the point:
**spacing tracks the minimum-gap floor the analyst imposes, not any structural period in the
data** — 15-year floor returns 15–21-year arcs, 25-year floor returns 26–31-year arcs, and no
floor this program has pre-registered ever lands inside Kondratieff's own band. The wave is not
hiding at a resolution the desk has not yet tried; on real commodity prices, across 165 years, it
is not there. This chapter's **KW2** trial went further, testing Kondratieff's *actual* object —
ten-year mean CPI inflation on the JST panel's UK, US and France series, the closest modern
equivalent to his own wholesale-price data — and found something genuinely interesting: **real,
synchronized generational price waves**, peaking within a few years of each other across all
three countries near 1920, 1948–50, 1981–83 and 2008–13, but spaced **25 to 41 years (pooled
median 36), with only one of ten pooled spacings landing in [45, 60]**, and — decisively — each
era's inflation surge carries a **different named mechanism**: metallic-standard supply shocks
for the earliest arcs, war finance for the mid-century ones, fiat-era policy and oil-shock
dynamics for the most recent. Kondratieff saw something real; it decomposes, on the desk's own
numbers, into exactly the inflation-regime arcs `09-inflation-regimes.md` already measures and
already seats at L6/L15 — not a fixed 50-year clock, a set of shorter, differently-caused,
already-budgeted regime states wearing one borrowed label. This is the atlas's **de-duplication
argument** made concrete rather than asserted: a K-wave seat would not add information the desk
lacks, it would double-count content L10 (the credit-cycle block), L12 (the medium
financial/real-estate cycle), and L15 (the long-wave fiscal/monetary state, including the
inflation-persistence input) already carry, at a coarser resolution and under a more mystical
name.

### A.6 The psychology of long-wave belief

Four reinforcing mechanisms explain why a claim this thoroughly broken on the data keeps
recruiting serious believers, each generation anew, and naming them is the standing
countermeasure. **First, a 50-year prophecy is a career-length irrefutability.** Nobody trades a
full Kondratieff wave — an analyst who calls a downswing at 35 will be, at best, evaluating the
call at 85, if the call is even scored against a fixed date rather than quietly re-dated as the
years pass (per A.5(a)'s own dating flexibility). A forecast horizon longer than a working
lifetime is, functionally, a forecast that cannot be marked wrong within the forecaster's own
professional existence — the single structural property this chapter's companion monograph
(`docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md` B.5, G) already identifies for Dalio's Big Cycle, Modelski's naval
succession, and Strauss-Howe's saeculum, and it operates here without modification. **Second,
doom-marketing economics is a durable business model, not a fringe curiosity** — the long-wave
literature sits on the same shelf, and is sold to the same audience, as *The Fourth Turning*'s
saeculum theory (`docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md` A.5), and the family resemblance is not
coincidental: both convert an unmanageably disorderly historical record into a legible plot with
a beginning, middle, and implied crisis, and both monetize the reader's anxiety about where "we"
currently sit in that plot more reliably than they monetize any tradable prediction. **Third,
apophenia on smoothed centuries is the mechanism, not a metaphor** — D07's own load-bearing
methodological section (finding #18, Slutzky 1937) demonstrates constructively that summing or
smoothing a sequence of *purely random* shocks manufactures a series that looks, to the naked eye,
like a genuine multi-decade wave; every chart in the long-wave literature that shows a smooth
50-year sine curve traced through 200 years of noisy price data is vulnerable to exactly this
artifact, and Granger's complementary 1966 finding (D07 finding #20 — economic spectra show no
distinct peak at any business-cycle frequency, only persistence concentrated at low frequencies)
is the formal reason Korotayev-Tsirel's borderline 4–5%-significant periodogram result (A.4) is
much less impressive than a single passing test statistic suggests: on a near-unit-root
persistent series, a spurious low-frequency peak is close to the *expected* finding, not a
surprising confirmation. **Fourth, survivor citation does the rest of the marketing work**: every
long-wave newsletter that gestures at 2008 as "the K-wave winter, called in advance" is citing a
retrodiction dressed as a forecast — precisely the failure mode `docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md` B.4
scores honestly, even generously, for Turchin's genuinely dated 2010 claim, and finds wanting on
stricter terms than most K-wave "calls" would survive at all, since none of the widely-circulated
2008-predicting K-wave commentary this chapter is aware of carries a pre-2008, specifically dated,
falsifiable public record comparable to Turchin's *Nature* piece. The pattern across all four
mechanisms is the one this program's sibling monographs keep re-finding: a genuinely well-sourced
partial observation — technology does cluster, prices do move in multi-decade arcs, crises do
recur — converted, under the emotional and commercial pull of a legible plot, into license for
timing authority the underlying evidence has never earned.

## B. Perez, and why she gets CONTEXT instead of rejection

### B.1 The framework, properly

Carlota Perez's *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital: The Dynamics of Bubbles and
Golden Ages* (Edward Elgar, 2002) **[Verified: title, publisher, year via this session's search]**
inherits Schumpeter's innovation-cluster mechanism through the Freeman-Louçã "techno-economic
paradigm" lineage (A.4) and gives it a second, independent axis Kondratieff never had: **a
financial mechanism, not merely a technological one**. Perez identifies five surges since the late
18th century, each anchored to a specific, dated "big-bang" triggering event: **the Industrial
Revolution**, Britain, from 1771 (Arkwright's mill at Cromford); **the Age of Steam and
Railways**, Britain, from 1829 (the Rocket's trial run for the Liverpool–Manchester line); **the
Age of Steel, Electricity and Heavy Engineering**, the US, from 1875 (Carnegie's Bessemer plant at
Pittsburgh); **the Age of Oil, the Automobile and Mass Production**, the US, from 1908 (the first
Ford Model T); and **the Age of Information and Telecommunications**, the US, from 1971 (Intel's
microprocessor announcement) **[Verified: all five surges, their dates, geographies and triggering
events via this session's search]**. Each surge, in Perez's account, runs through an identical
two-phase sequence. The **installation period** is dominated by **financial capital**: new-
technology infrastructure gets built fast and speculatively, funded by capital chasing the
narrative rather than earnings, wealth concentration rises, and the period ends in a **frenzy** —
a bubble whose crash or associated crisis (a **turning point**) forces institutional and
regulatory adjustment. The **deployment period** that follows is dominated by **production
capital**: the survivors of the crash, now operating under reformed rules, diffuse the
technology's genuine productivity gains broadly through the economy — the **golden age** — before
the surge matures and the next installation period's frenzy begins to displace it. Perez's own
one-line summary of the pattern, widely quoted, captures the structural claim precisely: **the
companies that lead installation are rarely the companies that lead deployment**
**[Verified via this session's search]** — infrastructure gets overbuilt by one cohort of capital,
and a different cohort captures the returns once it is running.

### B.2 What is genuinely different from Kondratieff

Part A.5(b)'s mechanism-heterogeneity critique of Kondratieff — a different technology cluster
explains every wave, so the label recurs but the mechanism never does — does not disappear under
Perez's framework, because her five surges are the same five technology clusters Schumpeter and
Freeman-Louçã already named. What changes is **the object of the claim itself**. Kondratieff (and
every author in A.5(a)'s comparison table) claimed a **fixed period**: a wave of roughly 45–60
years' *length*, dateable and, in principle, forecastable from where the clock currently sits —
exactly the claim KW1/KW2 tested and did not find. Perez claims a **recurring sequence with a
named financial mechanism**: capital rotates from speculative/infrastructure uses during
installation to productive/deployment uses after a turning point, driven by a genuinely
identifiable causal chain (asymmetric information about a new technology's true returns during
installation → overinvestment and bubble → crash exposes the overinvestment → survivors and
regulators reset the rules → capital that remains can only earn a return by actually deploying the
technology productively). This is not a period claim a periodogram could test and reject the way
KW1 rejected Kondratieff's; it is a **qualitative structural claim about capital behavior around
a new technology's diffusion**, testable episode by episode on its own terms (did installation-
phase capital behave as described? did a turning point occur? did deployment follow?) rather than
testable as a fixed-length clock. That distinction — period versus sequence — is the entire reason
this pair of atlas entries reaches opposite verdicts from what looks, at a glance, like the same
50-year label.

### B.3 The honest critique anyway

None of this exempts Perez's framework from real weaknesses, and a chapter that steelmans Part A
this hard owes Part B the same discipline. **Five observations remains five observations** — the
same small-n problem A.5(b) documents for Kondratieff applies with only modest mitigation to
Perez's surges, since her five cases are (mostly) the same five historical episodes, examined
through a different, additional lens rather than a fully independent new sample. **Dating
flexibility is real here too**: Perez's own big-bang dates are specific and well-sourced (B.1),
but the *turning points* — the crash or crisis marking each surge's pivot from installation to
deployment — are argued rather than mechanically dated, and reasonable analysts disagree on which
crisis counts (for the ICT surge, is the turning point the 2000 dot-com crash, the 2008 global
financial crisis, or — per some later commentary — a still-pending event). **The "golden age" is
sometimes hard to locate empirically**: the deployment-phase productivity boom the framework
predicts should follow a turning point is, for at least one surge (the ICT wave, post-2008), a
genuinely contested empirical question rather than an obvious historical fact the way the
post-WWII "golden age" of mass production plainly was. And — the sharpest version of the
critique, because it is the same structural flaw A.5's psychology section names for Kondratieff
— **the framework's own flexibility is what makes it resilient to disconfirmation**: if a
predicted turning point does not arrive on schedule, the honest Perez-framework response is "the
turning point can be delayed" (institutional capture, regulatory failure to intervene, a
sufficiently large state backstop), which is a genuine and often correct diagnosis of *why*
turning points get delayed in specific cases, but is also, structurally, an unfalsifiable move
available on demand. There is a subtler version of the same flaw worth naming precisely because it
looks like rigor rather than vagueness: Perez's own dating of each surge's *golden age* — the
clearest, most textbook-friendly instance being the post-WWII mass-production boom, dated with
real confidence — is argued retrospectively, after the fact of observed multi-decade prosperity,
in exactly the way Kondratieff's own wave datings were (A.5(a)); nothing in the framework commits,
in advance, to what evidence would count as *disconfirming* a golden age's arrival on schedule,
only to evidence that would explain its delay. A framework that can always explain a late turning
point, and can only ever retrospectively confirm a golden age rather than prospectively date one,
is a framework this desk should use as a lens, never as a clock — which is exactly the atlas's own
verdict, arrived at independently here.

### B.4 Why the atlas keeps it as unbudgeted CONTEXT

The honest critique in B.3 is precisely why 1.5 earns no seat, no state variable, and no budget
line — and precisely why it is not rejected outright the way 1.4 is. The distinction is
mechanical, not a matter of degree: **a REJECT verdict removes a claim from the design entirely
because using it would require betting real capital on a date or a period the evidence does not
support; a CONTEXT verdict keeps a claim as a lens because it generates good *questions* without
ever generating a *state*.** Perez's installation/deployment sequence, read against the 2020s AI-
capex buildout, is a genuinely productive interrogation tool for exactly this reason: it supplies
a structured way to ask what the desk would otherwise ask unstructured, and it does so by drawing
on three well-documented historical analogues to a capital-intensive infrastructure buildout
funded substantially by financial-capital enthusiasm ahead of demonstrated returns. **The 1840s
railway mania** in Britain saw infrastructure massively overbuilt on speculative capital; when the
1847–48 crash arrived, most **infrastructure owners** — the railway companies themselves —
were wiped out or forcibly consolidated, while the **users** of the resulting network (industry,
freight, eventually commuters) captured enormous and durable surplus for decades, and
**equipment suppliers** (iron and steel makers, civil engineering firms) captured value during the
buildout itself, largely independent of whether any specific line ever turned a profit for its
original shareholders. **The 1920s US electrification and utility-holding-company boom** rhymes
closely: overlevered utility pyramids (Samuel Insull's among the most notorious) collapsed in
1929–32, while the underlying electrification of American industry — the genuine productivity
transformation — proceeded largely independent of which specific holding company survived, with
equipment suppliers (GE, Westinghouse) again capturing durable value the original financial-
capital investors mostly did not. **The 1990s dotcom fiber-optic buildout** is the closest
recent analogue: telecom operators overbuilt fiber capacity on speculative financing (WorldCom,
Global Crossing prominent among the casualties), the infrastructure owners were wiped out in the
2000–2002 crash, and the fiber itself — purchased for pennies on the dollar out of bankruptcy by a
different cohort of capital — became the backbone the 2000s–2010s internet economy actually ran
on. **The recurring pattern across all three — infrastructure owners bear the crash risk;
equipment suppliers capture value during the buildout; users and later-stage capital allocators
capture the surplus once deployment actually happens — is the specific, transferable lens this
program keeps**, not a date, not a forecast of when the AI-capex cycle's own turning point
arrives, and not a state variable any regime score consumes.

Three concrete red-team questions follow directly, and are the form this framework is meant to
take at the desk — Stage-2 discussion material for the principal to revisit each quarter, never a
Stage-1 input:

1. **Capex-to-revenue gap, read against the installation-phase analogues.** Where does the current
   AI-infrastructure buildout — hyperscaler data-center capex, the associated debt and equity
   issuance funding it, the gap between capital deployed and revenue actually booked against it —
   sit relative to the railway-mania, electrification, and dotcom analogues at comparable points
   in their own installation phases? And, concretely for the equity universe this desk trades:
   **which NIFTY-listed names are effectively infrastructure owners in this surge (data-center
   capacity, power/transformer/cable capex-cycle plays re-rated on the AI narrative) versus
   equipment suppliers versus downstream users/beneficiaries** — and does the historical pattern
   (owners bear the crash, suppliers capture the buildout, users capture the deployment-phase
   surplus) argue for treating the first group's re-rating with more skepticism than the market
   currently prices in?

2. **What would this surge's turning point actually look like, and which existing states would
   fire first?** Not a date — the whole point of B.3's critique is that dating a turning point in
   advance is exactly the unfalsifiable move this framework must not be allowed. Instead: what
   *kind* of event historically triggers the installation-to-deployment turn (a funding-channel
   shock — credit tightening on the financiers, not a change in the underlying technology's
   promise — in every one of B.4's three analogues), and which of this program's **already-seated
   states** (L2's fast-stress triggers, L12's financial-cycle phase read, L14's FII-positioning
   extremes on the most narrative-heavy names) would be expected to fire first if that kind of
   shock arrived? This converts a Perez-flavored question into an instruction to *watch existing
   instruments harder around a specific theme*, never into a new instrument.

3. **Where does India sit — periphery-through-funding-channels, or deployment-phase beneficiary,
   and what would move that read?** B.5 below argues India's own history supplies a genuine, if
   imperfect, precedent for the first half of this question. The live version, worth revisiting
   quarterly rather than answering once: is India-listed AI exposure (services companies
   monetizing productivity gains; data-center and power capex names funded substantially by
   global hyperscaler capital) currently priced as a funding-channel periphery absorbing someone
   else's installation-phase frenzy, or as a genuine deployment-phase beneficiary capturing
   durable productivity surplus — and which recent order-book, margin, or financing data would be
   the first honest evidence that read has shifted?

No design, module, or acceptance test attaches to any of these — a context lens has no acceptance
test to pass, which is the entire point of keeping it unbudgeted rather than promoting it to a
Tier-C state variable the way, say, H53's commodity-supercycle context earned a conditioner seat.
Perez's framework does its job completely by sharpening the questions Stage 2's red team asks; the
moment it starts answering with a date or a position size, it has stopped being Perez's framework
and started being Kondratieff's, and Part A already shows where that road ends.

### B.5 India angle

**Where India sits in the current surge is, honestly, mixed rather than singular.** The dominant,
structurally defensible exposure remains **services**: India's listed IT-services majors are
positioned overwhelmingly as adapters and monetizers of AI productivity tools for global
enterprise clients — a downstream-user posture in Perez's own vocabulary — rather than as
frontier AI-model developers or, historically, as large-scale AI-infrastructure owners. That
second half of the picture is changing fast enough to matter for this quarter's read, not just as
a caveat: global hyperscalers have committed large, specific, and rapidly growing sums to Indian
data-center capacity through 2026–2030 — Google's gigawatt-scale AI hub (announced with roughly
$15bn committed, groundbreaking April 2026), Microsoft's roughly $17.5bn commitment, and Amazon
Web Services' planned spend of up to $35bn in India by 2030, alongside domestic players including
a Reliance/Jio gigawatt-scale Gujarat data-center project reported in the $20–30bn range
**[Verified: these hyperscaler commitment figures via this session's search of multiple
independent contemporaneous financial-media sources; treat the specific dollar figures as
company-disclosed or press-reported snapshots, not audited totals, and expect revision — VERIFY
against primary company disclosures at the next research refresh]** — meaning India is
simultaneously a services-side deployment beneficiary and an emerging installation-phase
infrastructure host, with the second role's own crash-risk profile (B.4's owner-vs-supplier-vs-
user distinction) only now becoming large enough on the NIFTY universe to matter for sector
tilts.

**The one history rhyme worth keeping is real, and older than railways.** India's most vivid
19th-century encounter with an installation-phase-style financial frenzy is the **Bombay share and
cotton mania of 1861–1865**. The American Civil War's outbreak in April 1861 cut off US cotton
exports to Britain's textile mills, and Bombay-region cotton stepped into the resulting demand
gap: India's cotton exports roughly **two-and-a-half-times over the period, rising from
approximately ₹16 crore to ₹40 crore between 1861 and 1865** **[Verified via this session's
search of multiple independent financial-history sources]**. The windfall did not stay in cotton
— it flowed into speculative joint-stock companies, most famously **the Back Bay Reclamation
Company**, promoted in 1864 by the era's most prominent broker, **Premchand Roychand**, whose
shares (face value ₹5,000) traded up to **₹50,000** at the mania's peak, while **Bank of Bombay**
stock (face value ₹500, and a bank Roychand had come to substantially control, exploiting rules
that let him borrow against government securities) touched **₹2,850** **[Verified via multiple
independent financial-history sources this session]**. When the American Civil War ended in April
1865 and US cotton supply resumed, Indian cotton exports and prices collapsed, and the Bombay
mania unwound with equal violence: Back Bay Reclamation shares fell from ₹50,000 to under
**₹2,000 — a decline of over 96%** — and Bank of Bombay's shares from ₹2,850 to **₹87**, triggering
the Bank of Bombay's own collapse **[Verified via multiple independent financial-history sources
this session]**. This episode is not, strictly, "railway mania" reaching India — it is a
trade-shock-driven speculative frenzy contemporaneous with, and financed through some of the same
institutional channels as, the guaranteed-return British capital then flowing into Indian railway
construction under the 1850s–60s guarantee system (the Crown guaranteed roughly 5% returns to
British investors in Indian railway companies, a genuine "installation-phase capital reaching a
peripheral market" channel in its own right, running in parallel with the cotton mania rather than
identical to it **[recall-verified on the guarantee system's existence and approximate terms; not
independently re-confirmed against a primary colonial-era source this session — VERIFY]**). The
lesson worth keeping is precisely the mechanism, not the label: **a peripheral, colonial-era
market with no domestic technology frenzy of its own absorbed and then violently unwound an
installation-phase-scale financial mania purely through funding and trade channels connected to a
metropole's own boom conditions** — cotton-trade windfall capital chasing speculative joint-stock
promises in Bombay, railway-guarantee capital chasing infrastructure returns nationally, both
routes by which a frenzy originating elsewhere reaches a market that did not generate it. That is
the honest 1850s–60s India rhyme for the 2020s question B.4's third red-team question already
poses: peripheral exposure to someone else's installation-phase capital can arrive through funding
channels long before, or entirely without, the peripheral market itself possessing the frontier
technology — a reason to read India's AI-linked capex names for their financing source and
narrative premium, not only for their fundamentals.
