## A. The 18-year tradition, strongest form

### A.1 Lineage

The claim that real estate moves on an ~18-year clock is not a fringe invention; it descends from a
continuous, name-by-name lineage of serious economists and practitioners, each building on the last.
Giving it its strongest form means tracing that lineage honestly before testing it.

**Henry George**, *Progress and Poverty* (1879), is the tradition's philosophical root, though George
never proposed a periodicity claim at all. His argument was about land *rent*, not land *timing*: as
a community grows — population, infrastructure, commerce — the value it creates attaches not to
labor or capital but to the fixed, non-reproducible asset under everyone's feet, land. That
"unearned increment" accrues to landowners regardless of their own effort, which is why George
proposed a land value tax (the "Single Tax") to capture it for the community that created it. Every
later figure in this lineage inherits George's core object — land rent capitalized into a price — even
where they part ways from his tax-policy conclusion entirely.

**Homer Hoyt**, *One Hundred Years of Land Values in Chicago: The Relationship of the Growth of
Chicago to the Rise of Its Land Values, 1830–1933* (University of Chicago Press, 1933), turned
George's theory into an empirical chronology. Hoyt's dissertation-turned-book tracked assessed land
values, neighborhood by neighborhood, as Chicago grew from a frontier trading post into a metropolis,
and it *is* a real periodicity in the data: Hoyt documents successive Chicago land-value booms
cresting in the **1830s** (peaking **1836** `[VERIFY: exact year]`, immediately preceding the Panic
of 1837), the **1850s** (peaking **1857**, coinciding with the Panic of 1857), the **1870s** (peaking
**1873**, the Panic of 1873 and the following depression), the **1890s** (peaking **1892**
`[VERIFY: exact year]`, ahead of the Panic of 1893), and the **1920s** (a land and building boom
that crested before the 1929 crash, closely following the Florida land bust of 1925–26 — a
spectacular, well-documented episode in its own right, in which Miami-area land changed hands in
rapid, leveraged, "binder"-financed resales at prices that collapsed within roughly two years, well
before the 1929 stock-market crash that popular histories often mistake for the decade's first
warning sign). What Hoyt
actually *measured* was Chicago land values alone, correlated against the city's physical and
population growth and against national financial panics — a single-city economic history, not a
claimed universal law. This distinction matters enormously for §A.3 below: **Hoyt's own book makes
no periodicity claim in these terms**; the "18-year cycle" is a later extraction, computed by dividing
the span between his book's five peaks by the number of intervals between them — a coincidence of
*this one city's* five data points, not a finding Hoyt himself asserted as a forecasting rule. Hoyt
later became an FHA economist and originated the "sector model" of urban land-use structure (1939).

**Roy Wenzlick**, a St. Louis real-estate appraiser and analyst who published the newsletter *The Roy
Wenzlick Real Estate Analyst* from the 1930s onward, is the tradition's mid-20th-century
systematizer: building on Hoyt's Chicago data plus his own tracking of national building-permit and
transaction cycles, Wenzlick popularized the ~18-year figure as a forecasting tool rather than a
retrospective description, reportedly projecting a national real-estate peak around **1972** and a
subsequent trough around **1990** `[VERIFY: Wenzlick's precise forecast dates and publication — the
figures are frequently cited secondhand by Harrison and Anderson but a primary Wenzlick-newsletter
citation could not be independently confirmed this pass]`. Wenzlick is the bridge between Hoyt's
1933 case study and the explicit, dated forecasts the tradition makes from the 1980s onward.

**Fred Harrison**, a British land economist, published *The Power in the Land: Fear and Greed, and
the Search for Rent* (1983), restating the Georgist rent-capture argument for a late-20th-century
audience, then *Boom Bust: House Prices, Banking and the Depression of 2010* (Shepheard-Walwyn,
2005) — the tradition's single most-cited modern forecast. Writing in 2005, with the prior cycle's
peaks dated to roughly **1973** and **1989/1990** (an ~18-year prior spacing), Harrison projected
the next global land-price peak around **2007** and, on the same clock, a subsequent crash and
"depression" landing by **2010**. This is a genuinely verifiable, dated, ex-ante claim, and — house
prices in the US and UK did peak in 2006–07, the GFC hit in 2008, and the ensuing recession's trough
in most measures fell in 2009–2010 — it landed inside a defensible window of its own prediction, the
single strongest data point the 18-year school can point to.

**Fred Foldvary**, "The Business Cycle: A Georgist-Austrian Synthesis" (*American Journal of
Economics and Sociology* 56(4), 1997), independently reached a closely related forecast from the
same lineage's logic (previous downturns dated to roughly 1973 and 1990, spaced ~18 years),
projecting the next real-estate-triggered recession for approximately **2008**
`[VERIFY: exact wording and dating of Foldvary's 1997 forecast — the paper and its ~2008 call are
widely cited in the Georgist and land-economics literature, but this pass could not retrieve the
original journal text to quote the specific sentence]`. Two independent forecasters, working from
the same 18-year framework roughly a decade apart (1997 and 2005), converged on the same crisis
window — the strongest possible form of the tradition's claim to genuine predictive content.

**Phillip J. Anderson**, *The Secret Life of Real Estate and Banking* (2008; Shepheard-Walwyn),
gave the tradition its most precise modern number: **18.6 years**, computed as the average of the
Hoyt/Wenzlick-era US chronology extended through the 20th century, divided into a recurring
sequence of named phases (recovery, expansion, a mid-cycle "winner's-curse" land-price acceleration,
the peak, and the bust). Anderson's framework has since been used to project a subsequent global
peak around **2026** `[VERIFY: the precise year attributed to Anderson's own current-cycle forecast
— multiple secondary sources cite a mid-2020s top under his 18.6-year framework, but this pass could
not confirm an exact, quotable primary-source year]` — which, as of this chapter's own date
(1 September 2026), is a **live, not-yet-resolved claim**, a point this chapter returns to in §A.3.

Between Harrison/Foldvary and Anderson sits a broader informal "18-year club" of practitioner
newsletters and land-economics commentators who trace their own lineage back through Wenzlick to
Hoyt — a genuine intellectual community, not a single author's idiosyncratic claim, which is itself
part of why the number carries the cultural weight it does; a claim repeated across independent
practitioner traditions *feels* more validated than one advanced by a single voice, even where (per
§A.3 below) the underlying statistical support does not actually compound across independent
repetitions of the same small-n Chicago-derived average.

**The academic end.** The tradition's most credentialed cross-over into mainstream macroeconomics is
**Edward Leamer, "Housing IS the Business Cycle"** (NBER Working Paper 13428, 2007; presented at the
Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City's Jackson Hole Symposium), which found that residential
investment — housing starts and permits — is the best-performing single leading indicator of US
recessions, declining ahead of **8 of the last 10 postwar recessions**. Leamer makes no 18-year
periodicity claim whatsoever; his contribution is to the *mechanism* side of the ledger (residential
construction leads the business cycle) rather than the *clock* side, and it is cited here precisely
because conflating the two — "housing leads recessions" does not imply "housing peaks every 18
years" — is exactly the error this chapter is built to avoid. **Glaeser & Gyourko**'s body of work
(most directly "The Economic Implications of Housing Supply," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*
32(1), 2018 — already cited as L12's own mechanism source, per `docs/cycles/12-financial-cycle.md`
Part A) supplies the supply-elasticity mechanics behind why a construction-lagged housing market
overshoots and undershoots at all, without ever proposing a fixed calendar length for how long that
takes. These two names anchor the tradition's *legitimate* intellectual content at the point where
this chapter's own verdict (§D) will land: mechanism kept, clock rejected.

### A.2 The mechanism in its strongest form

Given its strongest statement, the mechanism the 18-year tradition points to is real and well
evidenced. It has five components, and precision about which are *land-specific* and which are the
financial cycle "wearing a hard hat" — L12's own object, restated — is the single most important
piece of intellectual honesty this section owes the reader.

1. **Inelastic land supply.** Land, unlike almost every other input to housing, cannot be
   manufactured. **Albert Saiz, "The Geographic Determinants of Housing Supply"** (*Quarterly
   Journal of Economics* 125(3), 2010) measures this directly, combining satellite data on
   physically unbuildable land (water, slopes over 15°) with the Wharton Residential Land Use
   Regulatory Index to estimate elasticity metro-by-metro across the US, finding an order-of-
   magnitude range from highly elastic Sun Belt sprawl markets to severely inelastic coastal and
   geographically constrained ones. **Land-specific**: pure Ricardian scarcity.
2. **Rent capitalization into land prices.** The classical land-rent mechanism George named directly:
   a rise in the flow of services a parcel can generate (proximity to jobs, transit, amenities)
   capitalizes into the parcel's *stock* price, because the land itself cannot expand to absorb the
   higher demand the way a reproducible good would via more supply. **Land-specific**: this is the
   mechanism the tradition is named for.
3. **Construction time-to-build lags.** **Kydland & Prescott, "Time to Build and Aggregate
   Fluctuations"** (*Econometrica* 50(6), 1982) is the foundational formalization: a multi-year gap
   between the decision to build and a completed, occupiable unit means supply cannot respond to a
   demand shock within the same cycle that generated it — by the time new stock arrives, the demand
   that justified it may have already peaked, producing the textbook overshoot-then-glut pattern.
   **Partly land-specific**: the lag is a property of *construction*, not land per se, but it
   interacts with land inelasticity because a permit-and-build delay compounds a fixed-stock
   constraint rather than merely a temporary one.
4. **Speculative land banking.** Developers routinely acquire and hold land years ahead of building
   it, a rational response to the time-to-build lag and to expected future appreciation, but one
   that further restricts the *effective* supply of buildable land available at any given moment
   even where raw land nominally exists — a self-reinforcing amplifier on top of mechanisms 1 and 3.
   **Land-specific**: a behavioral response to land's own physical constraints.
5. **The winner's curse in land auctions.** **Capen, Clapp & Campbell, "Competitive Bidding in
   High-Risk Situations"** (*Journal of Petroleum Technology* 23(6), 1971) is the foundational
   winner's-curse paper — originally about oil-lease bidding, but the logic transfers directly to
   competitive land auctions and development-site tenders: when many bidders estimate an uncertain
   future value independently, the winning bid is systematically the most *optimistic* estimate, not
   the most accurate one, so competitive land acquisition mechanically overpays in aggregate,
   especially late in a boom when appraisal comparables lag realized price growth
   `[VERIFY: a real-estate-specific empirical winner's-curse study, as distinct from the foundational
   petroleum-auction paper, could not be confirmed this pass — the mechanism-transfer argument is
   standard in auction theory but this chapter cites the origin paper, not a real-estate replication]`.
   **Land-specific**: an auction-market property of the fixed-stock asset itself.
6. **Credit-collateral feedback.** Easy credit bids up property; richer collateral values support
   more lending; banks' own risk perception eases as losses stay low through the upswing; the loop
   reverses only once debt-service growth outruns income growth. **This is not land-specific at
   all** — it is Kiyotaki-Moore's collateral loop at macro scale, precisely Borio's synthesis, and
   precisely **L12's own object** (`docs/cycles/12-financial-cycle.md` Part A, §A.1(i)). Of the six
   mechanisms named here, this is the one doing the most work in every case record below (§C), and
   it is the one already fully seated, tested, and budgeted — nothing about restating it under an
   "18-year" label adds new information to the ladder.

The honest accounting: mechanisms 1–5 are genuinely land-and-construction-specific, real, and not
duplicated anywhere else in the ladder; mechanism 6 is the financial cycle's collateral loop by
another name, and crediting the 18-year tradition with "discovering" it would double-count a
mechanism L12 already carries at full weight.

### A.3 The psychology of round-number folklore

Why does "18" — or Anderson's more precise "18.6" — stick as a number, when §A.1 already shows Hoyt's
own chronology was five Chicago land-value peaks, not a law? Three reinforcing effects.

**First, Hoyt's own average is an artifact of small-n arithmetic, not a discovered constant.** Five
peaks produce four intervals; dividing a ~90-year span (1836 to roughly 1925) by four intervals
returns a number close to 18–20 almost by construction, regardless of whether the underlying process
has *any* true periodicity at all — the same five points, re-cut at different peak-dating choices
(a plausible alternative reading places a peak nearer 1910 rather than the 1920s land-boom crest, for
instance), would shift the average by several years without changing the underlying history at all.
A single city's five-point chronology simply does not carry the statistical weight the number "18"
has since been asked to bear.

**Second, forecaster survivorship.** Harrison (2005) and Foldvary (1997) are remembered and cited
precisely because their calls for a ~2008 crisis landed inside a real, severe global event — but the
same 18-year framework, applied by the same school at other points, has generated calls that either
have not yet resolved (Anderson's mid-2020s projection, live and untested as of this chapter's own
September 2026 date) or that a fuller accounting would need to check against actual outcomes at
*every* projected turn, not merely the one that happened to coincide with the best-documented
financial crisis of the past century `[VERIFY: a systematic record of the 18-year school's full
forecast history, hits and misses alike, was not located this pass — the desk should treat the
Harrison/Foldvary 2008 calls as the tradition's best-documented success and remain alert that
survivorship bias in which calls get cited is, by its nature, difficult to fully audit from outside
the school's own literature]**. This is precisely the mechanism the trial-ledger discipline
(`research/CONTRACT.md` §9) exists to police: a forecasting school with many practitioners, many
permissible starting-date choices, and a low bar for declaring a multi-year window a "hit" will
produce apparent confirmations even from a process with no true fixed period underneath it.

**Third, apophenia** — the well-documented human tendency to perceive meaningful pattern in
essentially random or weakly-patterned sequences — does real work here, and round numbers are its
preferred vehicle: "18" is memorable, teachable, and narratively satisfying (a number close to a
human generation, evoking a natural rhythm) in a way that "somewhere between 8 and 45 years,
depending on the country" is not, even when the latter is what the evidence (§D below) actually
shows.

**The desk's defense, stated precisely.** None of this licenses a reflexive dismissal of the whole
tradition. The heuristics-lane charter (`research/register/heuristics-lane.md`) is explicit and
binding: mechanism-backed rules are judged by sign, episode record, and paper-trade evidence — never
rejected solely on t-statistics — and every card stays in the register with its verdict, nothing
silently dropped. That charter is exactly why §A.2's five land-specific mechanisms are kept, not
waved away. But a **fixed period is a different kind of claim than a mechanism**: it is a *date*
claim — this year, then eighteen years later, then eighteen years after that — and date claims are
governed by the Contract's own clock test (§4: "anything without ≥4 observed complete periods is a
state variable, not a cycle") and by the pre-registration discipline (§9) that turns "does 18 fit"
from a post-hoc pattern-match into a falsifiable test with a stated pass bar, set before the data is
seen. The lane's own first-ever rejection verdict, entered against exactly this claim, is
`HL-6` in `research/register/heuristics-lane.md`: mechanism accepted, already seated in L12; the
fixed period rejected on the pre-registered evidence (§D below quotes it in full). Mechanism-backed
heuristics earn a lower evidence bar; a date claim does not get to borrow that lower bar by dressing
itself in a mechanism's clothing.

## B. Kuznets swings

### B.1 The swing that was real, and the driver that is gone

**Simon Kuznets, *Secular Movements in Production and Prices: Their Nature and Their Bearing upon
Cyclical Fluctuations*** (Houghton Mifflin, 1930) identified a distinct rhythm sitting between the
business cycle and the longest secular trends: 15-to-25-year swings in aggregate production, prices,
and — most durably in the literature that followed — building and infrastructure investment
specifically. This is a genuinely separate tradition from §A's 18-year land-price folklore: Kuznets's
swing is an *investment-and-construction-volume* phenomenon (how much gets built), not a *land-price*
phenomenon (what land costs), and its proposed mechanism from the outset was demographic and
migratory rather than purely a credit-and-collateral story.

**Moses Abramovitz** carried the empirical program forward — "Evidences of Long Swings in Aggregate
Construction Since the Civil War" (NBER, 1964) documented the US construction record in this band —
before delivering the tradition's own honest self-correction: **"The Passing of [the] Kuznets
Cycle[s]"** (*Economica* 35(140), 1968) `[VERIFY: exact title — sources vary between singular and
plural]` argued explicitly that the long-swing mechanism had **stopped operating** in the postwar
period, because the driver that had produced it — a specific demographic-migratory dynamic, not a
generic economic law — no longer existed in the same form.

**Brinley Thomas, *Migration and Economic Growth: A Study of Great Britain and the Atlantic
Economy*** (Cambridge University Press, 1954) supplies the mechanism Abramovitz's "passing" verdict
refers to, and it is genuinely elegant: in the open, low-friction transatlantic labor market of
roughly **1870–1913** — a window across which tens of millions of Europeans crossed the Atlantic,
one of the largest sustained voluntary migrations in recorded history
`[VERIFY: precise aggregate migration count for the period — commonly cited estimates run to
several tens of millions across the full 1870–1913 span, but a single authoritative figure was not
independently re-confirmed this pass]` — US and British building cycles moved in **alternation**,
not in tandem. When
US wages and opportunity pulled a wave of European migrants across the Atlantic, US construction
boomed to house and employ them while, simultaneously, Britain's own building activity slowed —
the labor (and, following it, a good share of the capital) that would otherwise have built British
housing and infrastructure had literally left the country. When the US cycle turned down, migration
flows eased or reversed, and British building picked back up. **Richard Easterlin**'s complementary
work (most directly "Population, Labor Force, and Long Swings in Economic Growth: The American
Experience," 1968) formalized the demographic-cohort mechanism underneath this: migration waves,
birth-cohort sizes, and labor-force entry rates move together on a 15-to-25-year rhythm because each
of these variables responds, with a lag, to the economic conditions the *previous* wave created —
a genuine self-perpetuating (if not perfectly periodic) demographic-economic mechanism, distinct from
anything in §A.

**Why the swing was real in 1870–1913 and why its driver is structurally gone — the alpha-decay
lesson in its purest historical form.** The pre-1914 Atlantic economy had three specific
preconditions that do not merely happen to be absent today; they were *removed by policy* and never
restored. First, **open migration**: no receiving country operated anything resembling a modern
immigration-quota system before World War I: the mass transatlantic flows Thomas documents — tens of
millions of Europeans crossing to the Americas over four decades — moved essentially unrestricted by
law. That regime ended decisively with the US Immigration Act of 1921 and the Johnson-Reed Act of
1924, which imposed hard national-origin quotas and closed the open channel for good. Second, **the
classical gold standard** facilitated the capital flows that rode alongside the labor flows,
providing a stable nominal anchor across the whole Atlantic system; that regime did not survive World
War I in its original form. Third, the underlying **demographic base itself** — Europe's own
19th-century population growth surplus, the raw material of the migration wave — was itself a
one-time transition (Europe's own demographic transition) rather than a stable steady-state supply.
This is not a case of a real anomaly being arbitraged away by capital that learned to exploit it (the
Contract's §5 default alpha-decay story); it is a case of the *literal driver being legislated out of
existence*. Per the Contract's own governing question — "why does this survive being known?" — the
honest answer for the classical Kuznets swing is: **it does not survive**, and not because anyone
traded against it, but because the open-border, gold-standard, pre-transition world that produced it
was dismantled by policy choices with no plausible path back. This is the cleanest, most literal
illustration available anywhere in this research program of the Contract's own alpha-decay mandate:
a mechanism can be entirely real, well-documented, and still carry a decay argument so complete that
its expected future contribution is essentially zero.

### B.2 What survives for India — urbanization as a live driver

The demographic-migratory mechanism itself, however, is not dead in general — it has simply changed
its geography and its unit of analysis, from *international* migration inside an open-border Atlantic
economy to **internal**, rural-to-urban migration inside single large developing economies, India
foremost among them. This is the live Kuznets-type content this chapter carries forward, and it is
genuinely distinct from §A's land-price clock: it is a claim about the *pace and location of
construction demand*, not about a fixed periodicity in prices.

**India's urbanization arc.** Census of India **2001** recorded an urban population share of
approximately **27.8%**; Census **2011** recorded approximately **31.2%**
`[VERIFY: exact decimal figures — commonly cited as 27.81% and 31.16% respectively, but this pass
relies on general knowledge rather than a primary Census release]`, meaning roughly 377 million
Indians lived in urban areas by 2011 against roughly 833 million rural
`[VERIFY: exact 2011 urban/rural population counts]`. Projections carried by bodies including the UN
World Urbanization Prospects and, earlier, McKinsey Global Institute's *India's Urban Awakening*
(2010) point to India's urban share reaching on the order of **40% by 2030** and roughly **half the
population by mid-century**, with McKinsey's own 2030 projection naming a headline figure near
**590 million urban Indians** by that date `[VERIFY: exact percentages and the precise UN WUP vintage
— these figures circulate widely in Indian infrastructure and real-estate commentary but a primary
UN table was not directly re-checked this pass]`. One honest data-quality caveat belongs alongside
these figures explicitly: India's decennial Census, due in **2021**, was postponed and — as of this
chapter's own date — **has not yet been conducted**
`[VERIFY: current Census 2021/2027 status as of September 2026]`, meaning every urbanization estimate
for the 2011–2026 window is itself an interpolation or a survey-based proxy (PLFS, NSSO, satellite
night-lights studies), not a fresh headcount — a measurement-honesty problem structurally identical
in kind to the RBI HPI/circle-rate gap already documented for India's price data (§C.9 below).

**Why this loads on L12's existing supply-side confirms, not on a new fixed period.** Internal
migration and urbanization are real, ongoing, state-level construction drivers — cement dispatches,
housing starts, and RERA project launches genuinely do cluster around specific urban corridors
(Bengaluru and Hyderabad's IT-led booms, the NCR's land-driven cycles, and so on) at paces set by
migration and job-creation timing that has no reason to share a common national clock, let alone an
18-year or Kuznets 15–25-year one. `docs/cycles/12-financial-cycle.md` (Part C, Part E Step 5)
already carries cement IIP, registrations, and RERA launches as **Tier-C, reduce-only supply-side
conditioners** on L12's property leg; the urbanization/internal-migration driver this section
describes is the correct India-specific *content* for those same conditioners, not a case for a
separate seat, a separate budget, or a date. It is also, by construction, reviewed alongside L16's
own demographic-arc seat (`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` row 0.7: 30–50y demographic arcs, India's dividend
window ~2036–41, zero standing allocation authority, next design review 2030) — urbanization and the
broader demographic dividend are the same underlying population dynamic read at two different
horizons, and this chapter deliberately does not propose a second read of it living anywhere new.

## C. Case record

Nine cases, tested the same way throughout: state the folk chronology's implied claim (what a naive
18-year clock, run forward from the earliest well-documented peak, would predict), state what

### C.1 United States — the chronology that both confirms and breaks the claim

**Folk claim.** Extending Hoyt's own five-peak Chicago chronology (§A.1: 1836, 1857, 1873, 1892,
1925 `[VERIFY exact years]`) forward at ~18-year spacing predicts a next major turn near 1943, then
roughly 1961, 1979, 1997, and 2015.

**What actually happened.** None of the mid-century predicted dates correspond to a national
real-estate peak of any recognizable kind: 1943 fell inside wartime price and construction controls;
the 1950s–60s were the suburban-expansion era (Levittown-style tract building, GI Bill mortgage
finance) with no synchronized national bust; the 1970s saw high nominal house-price inflation
alongside general CPI inflation but, on **Robert Shiller's long-run real US home-price index** (the
series underlying *Irrational Exuberance*, reconstructed back to 1890), **real US home prices were
essentially flat — bouncing in a narrow range with no secular trend — from roughly the 1950s through
the mid-1990s**, a genuinely well-documented, frequently reproduced finding
`[VERIFY: precise index values and exact flat-window endpoints, though the qualitative "flat 1950s–
1996, then a sharp deviation" shape of Shiller's own chart is a widely reproduced, high-confidence
fact]`. The sharp deviation begins only around **1997–2006**, culminating in the already-documented
2006 peak (Case-Shiller National Index ~198.01 real, Q1 2006) and the 2012 trough (113.89 real, Q1
2012, a ~42.5% real decline) that `research/cycles/fincycle-deep/partB-cases.md` §B2.2 covers in
full and this chapter does not re-derive. A further peak followed around **June 2022**, driven by
pandemic-era low mortgage rates and remote-work relocation demand, with a shallow, transaction-
volume-driven correction rather than a 2008-style crash following the Fed's 2022–23 hiking cycle
`[VERIFY: exact 2022 peak date and the depth/shape of the subsequent correction]`.

**Verdict.** 2006 to 2022 is 16 years — close to 18 — but it sits at the end of a chronology whose
middle forty years (roughly 1955–1995) produced **zero** national real-price cyclicality of any kind
on the best available long-run series. A rule that hits twice in a row after four decades of silence
is not a clock; the US case is the single clearest demonstration that any apparent 18-year fit is a
property of *which decades you choose to look at*, not a property of the underlying process.

### C.2 United Kingdom — the tradition's best case, honestly read

**Folk claim.** A UK peak near 1973 (the "Barber boom," named for the expansionary fiscal stance of
Chancellor Anthony Barber, ended by the 1973 oil shock), spaced 18 years forward, predicts 1991; a
peak near 1989 spaced 18 years forward predicts 2007.

**What actually happened.** UK house prices peaked in **1973**, corrected sharply against the
mid-1970s inflation shock, then boomed again into **1989** — a boom driven by 1986 "Big Bang"
financial deregulation and a rush to beat the August 1988 withdrawal of double mortgage-interest tax
relief (MIRAS) for co-purchasers — before falling through the early 1990s, with **negative equity
reportedly affecting well over a million UK households by the early 1990s**
`[VERIFY: precise negative-equity household count and years — commonly cited figures cluster around
1.5–1.8 million UK households by 1992–93]`. A third peak followed in **2007**, ahead of the GFC-era
fall (UK house prices fell roughly **20% nominally, 2007–2009**, on the Nationwide and Halifax
indices `[VERIFY: exact peak-to-trough percentage and dates]`).

**Verdict.** 1973→1989 is 16 years; 1989→2007 is 18 years exactly — this is, without qualification,
the best fit anywhere in this record, and the honest reason to take the 18-year tradition seriously
at all rather than dismiss it outright. But it is also **n=3** at most (two intervals), nowhere near
the Contract's own clock-test threshold of ≥4 observed complete periods (`research/CONTRACT.md` §4),
and the naive next prediction — an ~18-years-later peak near **2025** — has not produced a
recognizable 2008-scale UK boom-and-bust on the available record as of this writing
`[VERIFY: UK house-price behavior 2023–2026 in detail — broadly, prices ran roughly flat to
modestly softer through 2023 amid the Bank of England's rate-hiking cycle, with no confirmed sharp
peak-and-bust matching the 1989/2007 pattern, but a full check of the most recent data was not
performed this pass]`. Two good hits and an untested (or already-missed) third prediction is not a
clock; it is the tradition's strongest anecdote.

### C.3 Japan — the killer counterexample

**What actually happened**, cited from `partB-cases.md` §B2.1 without recomputation: Japanese
residential and commercial land prices peaked in **1991**, following a boom that saw Tokyo apartment
price-to-income ratios rise from 8.08× (1985) to 18.12× (1990). The decline that followed ran, on the
six-major-city measure already verified in that document, **13 consecutive years (1992–2005)**, with
commercial land eventually falling 70–80% and residential land roughly 40% from peak. The
**nationwide** land-price series — a broader measure than the six largest cities — is reported by
multiple sources to have continued softening well beyond 2005, with a full recovery to positive
year-on-year national land-price growth arriving only around the **mid-2010s**, an arc closer to
**twenty years** end to end `[VERIFY: exact date of the first genuinely nationwide, all-use land
price increase — commonly associated with Japan's MLIT annual land-price survey reporting a national
uptick around fiscal year 2015, but this specific figure was not independently re-confirmed this
pass]`.

**Verdict.** There is no second peak to test a period against. Japan's post-1991 real-estate history
is a **single, monotonic, multi-decade decline** — not a slow cycle, not a long cycle, but the
absence of a cycle altogether over the exact multi-decade window an 18-year clock would need to show
at least one full completed round trip to survive contact with the data. This is the record's single
cleanest falsification: whatever mechanism produced Japan's 1980s land boom, nothing in the
subsequent 20-plus years produced anything resembling a second, dateable peak on schedule or off it.

### C.4 Germany — decades flat, then a boom nothing in the clock predicts

**What actually happened.** German real house prices ran remarkably flat — in some readings
gently *declining* in real terms — from roughly the **mid-1990s through 2009–2010**, making Germany
the one major advanced economy that conspicuously **did not participate** in the pre-GFC 2000s global
housing boom, a frequently cited contrast case in the international literature
`[VERIFY: exact German real house-price index values and the precise flat-window dating]`. From
around **2010 through 2022**, German prices then rose sharply — particularly in the "Big Seven"
cities (Berlin, Hamburg, Munich, Cologne, Frankfurt, Stuttgart, Düsseldorf) — driven by a decade of
ECB near-zero and negative policy rates, safe-haven capital inflows, and urbanization into major
metros, with the Bundesbank's own periodic reports flagging meaningful overvaluation (on the order of
**15–30%** in several major cities by the early 2020s) in its Financial Stability Reviews
`[VERIFY: exact Bundesbank overvaluation percentages and years cited]`. Prices then softened —
German house prices fell for the first time in decades in **2023**, as ECB rates rose sharply
`[VERIFY: exact 2023 decline magnitude]`.

**Verdict.** A fifteen-year flat regime followed by a twelve-year boom followed by a correction bears
no resemblance whatsoever to an 18-year (or any other fixed-length) repeating pattern; there is no
plausible starting anchor from which an 18-year clock, run forward or backward, reproduces this
shape. Germany rejects the fixed period as cleanly as Japan does, from the opposite direction — not
by refusing to cycle at all, but by cycling on a schedule with no periodic structure whatsoever.

### C.5 Australia — the un-burst upswing, extended

**What actually happened**, cited from `partB-cases.md` §B2.8 without recomputation: Australian
household debt sits near **182% of income** (Q4 2024); the national dwelling value-to-income ratio
reached **8.2×** in September 2025 against a 20-year average of **6.8×**; only **14%** of
median-income households can afford the median dwelling, down from 43% three years earlier. Australia
has run this way, with only shallow multi-quarter corrections (the early 1990s slowdown, a brief
2008–09 dip, a 2017–19 correction, a brief 2022 dip), through the entire post-WWII era — the country
has not seen a national, multi-year, double-digit real house-price bust of the Japan/Spain/Ireland
scale since, on the most commonly cited reading, the land-boom bust of the **1890s**
`[VERIFY: the "no national crash since the 1890s" claim is a frequently repeated characterization in
Australian housing commentary; a rigorous confirmation across the full 20th century was not performed
this pass]`.

**Verdict.** If an 18-year clock genuinely governed real estate, a country observed continuously
since 1945 should show four to five complete round trips. Australia shows zero. This is, alongside
Japan, one of the two cleanest possible falsifications in the record — not because Australia is
somehow exempt from the underlying mechanisms of §A.2 (its own price-to-income extremes are driven by
precisely the Glaeser-Gyourko supply-inelasticity mechanism L12 already encodes), but because the
*same* mechanisms that produce a bust elsewhere have, in Australia, produced only a multi-decade
ascent with no bust at all.

### C.6 Spain — construction as the overheating gauge, and the regime-break problem

**What actually happened**, cited from `partB-cases.md` §B2.3 without recomputation: Spanish
residential construction/GDP peaked at **11.7–11.8%** in December 2006 (up from 6.2% in 1997); real
house prices rose **over 150%** from 1998 to 2007; the ensuing bust took real prices down **~41%**
peak to trough over **2007–2014**, roughly seven years, with the construction sector's physical
overhang (over one million annual housing starts at the peak; the €1.1bn Ciudad Real ghost airport,
sold at auction for €10,000) clearing far more slowly than the financial overhang.

**Verdict.** Spain did have an earlier property boom in the late 1980s, tied to 1986 EU accession,
before a slowdown in the early 1990s — an interval to the 2007 peak of roughly **16 years**, again
close to 18. But push the clock back one more turn, to where a fixed 18-year rule would place the
*prior* prior peak — approximately **1973** — and the exercise breaks down entirely: Spain in 1973
was still a closed, Franco-era command economy, structurally incomparable in every respect (capital
controls, no open mortgage market, no EU membership) to the liberalized, euro-anchored economy of
2007. Spain is at best an **n=1-to-2** usable observation whose own history supplies the clearest
possible illustration of why comparing across a regime change (dictatorship to democracy, 1975; EU
accession, 1986; euro entry, 1999) invalidates a fixed-period extrapolation before the arithmetic even
starts.

### C.7 China — too young to test at all

**What actually happened**, cited from `partB-cases.md` §B2.7 without recomputation: China's modern
*private* housing market dates only to the **1998 housing reform** that ended danwei welfare-housing
allocation; the subsequent boom ran roughly 2003–2021, financed substantially through the presale
system (presales still **68% of new-home sales by floor space in 2025**), before the "three red
lines" policy (August 2020) triggered the Evergrande and Country Garden defaults and a price decline
now running **32 consecutive months** of month-on-month contraction through February 2026.

**Verdict.** From the 1998 reform to the present is under thirty years — not even two full turns of
an 18-year clock, and the single boom-to-bust arc China has lived through (2003–2021, then the
ongoing correction) is its *first*. There is no prior cycle to compare a spacing against; China is an
**n<1** case for periodicity purposes, structurally incapable of confirming or refuting a fixed-period
claim regardless of how the current correction ultimately resolves.

### C.8 Ireland — the fastest bust, and another n=1

**What actually happened**, cited from `partB-cases.md` §B2.4 without recomputation: Ireland's CSO
Residential Property Price Index rose to an all-time high of **131.0 in April 2007** and fell to a
record low of **58.7 in March 2013** — a **54.4% nominal peak-to-trough decline** over roughly six
years — before recovering to its 2007 nominal peak only in **mid-2022**, some fifteen years later,
and standing **~23% above** that peak by late 2025.

**Verdict.** Ireland's Celtic Tiger property boom began around **1994** and ran to its 2007 peak — a
13-year boom leg with no comparable predecessor anywhere in modern Irish economic history: independent
Ireland (post-1922) spent most of the 20th century as a poor, emigration-heavy economy with no
functioning national property-price cycle of the scale seen from the 1990s onward. Like China,
Ireland's own documented boom-bust arc is its first, making any periodicity claim for Ireland
specifically an **n=1** exercise from the outset — chosen here over Sweden per the task's own
"better-documented bust" instruction precisely because CSO's RPPI gives Ireland a clean, continuously
published, primary-source index that the Nordic cases' more fragmented mid-century data cannot match.

### C.9 India — the double-length case

**The 2003–2013 boom.** India's real-estate sector entered a sustained boom from roughly 2002–03,
concurrent with the economy's strongest sustained post-liberalization growth run. City-level
appreciation was extreme by any cross-country standard in this record: already-verified figures from
`research/cycles/fincycle-deep/partB-cases.md` §B3 (cited here, not recomputed) put city price
indices (2003=100) at **Delhi 269 and Bengaluru 275 by 2007** — a near-tripling in four years —
against **Mumbai 178 and Kolkata 172** over the same window
`[VERIFY: as noted in the source document, the exact primary NHB source for this specific table]`.
Year-on-year growth in the hottest years matched: Delhi ran **+34.0% (2006), +33.8% (2007)**;
Bengaluru ran **+33.0% (2003)** and 27.8–31.8% in following years. India's own official price-index
infrastructure — the NHB **RESIDEX** program — began only as a **2005 five-city pilot** on a 2001
base, officially launching in **July 2007**: for the boom's most explosive years (2003–2005), India's
only price-tracking apparatus was reconstructing history retrospectively rather than measuring it
live, the first instance of a measurement-honesty problem this case closes with in full.

**The 2013–2020 stagnation — the invisible correction.** RBI's own quarterly House Price Index
(ten cities) shows growth decelerating from a **+26.3% year-on-year peak in Q4 2011** to just
**~3.7%/year on average across 2017–2020** — a period explicitly attributed to demonetisation
(November 2016), GST (July 2017), and RERA (2016–17) landing in close succession, and compounded from
2018 by the IL&FS/NBFC funding freeze (§ below). What did this do to *real* prices? Arithmetic alone
gives a sense of scale: nominal growth averaging roughly 3.7%/year against CPI inflation running at a
comparable or higher rate for most of the window compounds, over seven years, into a real decline
plausibly landing somewhere in the high-20s to mid-30s percent range — but `partB-cases.md`'s own
synthesis table (§B4) explicitly declines to commit to a precise figure here, flagging the calculation
itself as **`[VERIFY: precise magnitude — official series too thin to compute directly]`**, and this
chapter inherits that same honest uncertainty rather than resolving it independently, exactly per this
task's own instruction to cite that finding rather than recompute it. What is not in doubt is the
*shape*: nominal flatness masking a real decline, invisible to a casual read of the headline series —
India's own instance of precisely the pattern Spain, the US, and Japan each display more visibly.

**The IL&FS/NBFC link.** By the mid-2010s, commercial banks had substantially retreated from
real-estate developer lending, leaving non-bank finance companies and housing finance companies as
developers' primary remaining funding channel (NBFC real-estate exposure ~7.5% of the sector's book,
~₹1.65 trillion, as of March 2018). The September 2018 default of Infrastructure Leasing & Financial
Services (IL&FS) triggered a sector-wide NBFC funding freeze that hit developers with particular
force precisely because of this dependency, deepening and extending the stagnation already under way
— the clean, dated transmission channel connecting a shadow-credit event to a multi-year real-side
property outcome, already documented in full in the sibling monograph and not re-derived here.

**RERA 2016 and GST as the structural reset.** The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016
(enacted 2016, phased in through 2017) introduced mandatory project registration, escrow-confined
buyer receipts, and a five-year structural-defect liability; GST, layered on from July 2017, taxed
under-construction property before the GST Council cut the applicable rates to 5% (standard) and 1%
(affordable housing), effective 1 April 2019, both without input-tax-credit eligibility. Together
these constitute a genuine structural regime change — the desk's Nordic 1987–93 case
(`partB-cases.md` §B2.5) already argues that a first-cycle liberalized-or-re-regulated system, with
no market participant carrying lived experience of a full round trip under the new rules, is a
distinct source of elevated tail risk in its own right, and India's post-RERA regime is that same
structural situation by a different mechanism.

**The 2021–2026 upcycle.** Residential sales hit a record **302,867 units in 2024** (+11% YoY) before
cooling: top-seven-city sales fell **~14% YoY to ~3.96 lakh units in 2025** from ~4.59 lakh in 2024,
even as launches edged up ~2% to ~4.19 lakh units. Despite the volume fall, **total sales value rose
~6% to over ₹6 lakh crore** — the clearest single statistic of the upcycle's defining
**premiumization** character, with homes above ₹2.5 crore rising from 18% to over 21% of new launches
between 2024 and 2025, and luxury residences crossing **over 50% of total sales value in 2024**
nationally, even as affordable-segment (under ₹40 lakh) unsold inventory *fell* ~19% YoY — developers
chasing higher-margin premium supply while under-building the segment first-time buyers can actually
afford.

**The black-money/circle-rate measurement wedge.** In prime localities, market prices exceed
government-notified circle rates by **50–70%**, and because Indian tax law (Sections 50C/56 of the
Income Tax Act) treats a gap beyond roughly 10% of the circle rate as taxable deemed income to both
parties, a portion of true transaction value has historically been pushed into unrecorded cash —
meaning every registered, tax-reported price series (including RESIDEX and, indirectly, the
registration data feeding RBI's HPI) **structurally understates** true cycle amplitude, most acutely
in exactly the high-value, high-cash-intensity segments and locations where a boom runs hottest.

**State-level heterogeneity — why even a domestic clock, if one existed, could not be a single
national number.** Unlike Hoyt's single-city Chicago chronology or the UK's single national index
(§C.2), India's own booms have never moved on one clock even within the single 2003–2026 arc this
case documents. The Bengaluru and Hyderabad upcycles have run substantially on **IT/ITeS
employment-led demand**, timed to that sector's own hiring and campus-expansion cycles; the National
Capital Region's booms and busts have historically run more on **land-development and
infrastructure-announcement timing** (metro-line extensions, expressway corridors) than on white-collar
job creation directly; Mumbai's cycle sits closest to a pure land-scarcity story, an island city with
almost no greenfield land left to release. This is precisely §B.2's internal-migration-and-urbanization
point read at the sub-national level: if India's states and metro corridors are each substantially
riding their own construction-demand timing, a single national "18-year" (or any other fixed-length)
clock was never a coherent object to begin with, independent of how thin the national price series
themselves already are.

**The honest statement this case exists to make.** India has lived through **one** full
boom–stagnation–recovery arc since its property market began functioning as a genuine, liberalized,
mortgage-financed asset class at all — a market whose own pre-history barely exists in comparable
form: HDFC, India's first specialized mortgage lender, was founded only in **1977**
`[VERIFY: exact founding year]`, and the Urban Land (Ceiling and Regulation) Act, 1976 — which
directly restricted urban land transactions — was not nationally repealed until **1999**
`[VERIFY: exact repeal year and state-by-state variation]`. On the desk's own strict,
pre-registered peak-to-peak spacing test (§D below), India does not register even **one completed
spacing on official indices at all** — `research/cycles/realestate-merge/partDH-verdict-machinery.md`
Part H states this explicitly, and its RN1 design (waiting until India's RBI HPI history is long
enough to contain ≥2 real peaks — not before the 2030s at the earliest) is the correct response. This
is not a data gap the desk can shortcut: **any India-specific fixed-period claim — 18 years or any
other number — is unfalsifiable domestically for decades**, and treating a single observed arc as
confirmation of anything periodic would be exactly the small-n error §A.3 already diagnosed in Hoyt's
own five-point Chicago chronology, committed a second time with an even thinner domestic record.

## D. The verdict

`research/CONTRACT.md` §8 already names the destination of this section before a single number in
it is argued: fixed-period calendar cycles sit on the traps list, grouped explicitly with Elliott
Wave and Gann. This chapter's task was not to defer to that line, but to earn it — to give the
18-year and Kuznets traditions their strongest form (§A–§B), test them against nine honestly read
cases (§C) and the desk's own pre-registered cross-country panel, and show the working. What
follows is that working, not a restatement of a rule already on the books.

**Why the desk keeps the mechanism.** Every mechanism §A.2 separated out as land-specific — inelastic
supply, rent capitalization, time-to-build lags, land banking, the winner's curse in land auctions —
is real, well evidenced, and not duplicated anywhere else in the ladder; it is exactly the content
`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` row 63 already credits as "the one structural argument that survives"
(Glaeser-Gyourko's supply-lag mechanism). And the mechanism doing the heaviest lifting in every single
case in §C's record — credit bidding up collateral, richer collateral supporting more credit, the loop
reversing only once debt service outruns income — is not land-specific at all: it is Borio's synthesis,
already fully seated as **L12** inside the `macro_credit_block`, sharing that block's single 0.20
budget with L6, L10, and L11 under the de-duplication rule (`docs/cycles/12-financial-cycle.md` Part
D2, "no stacking, ever"). Crediting the 18-year school with an independent discovery here, and paying
it a second time, would be exactly the double-count the ladder's whole architecture is built to
forbid.

**Why the desk rejects the fixed period — the evidence.** This chapter's own nine-case narrative
record (§C.10) already rejects the clock in eight of nine countries and finds the ninth (UK) resting
on n=3, below the Contract's own clock-test floor of ≥4 observed complete periods
(`research/CONTRACT.md` §4). The desk's own pre-registered panel test — run on the full JST R6
cross-country panel, not on hand-picked case narratives, and reported in
`research/cycles/realestate-merge/jst-realestate-RESULTS.md` (RE1–RE2), pre-declared in
`research/register/trial-ledger.md` **before** it ran — reaches the identical conclusion with far more
statistical weight. **RE1** tested the folk 18-year claim directly: across **109 peak-to-peak
spacings in 17 countries**, the pooled median spacing is **14 years** — genuinely inside the claimed
window — but only **45% of all spacings fall in the pre-registered [14, 22]-year band**, against a
pre-declared pass bar of **≥50%**. **FAIL.** Per-country median spacings range from **10 years (USA)
to 20 years (Germany)**, with only Finland printing an exact **18**; "18" is not even a stable
per-country constant, let alone a universal one. Two honesty notes the results file itself carries
forward: the test's own construction (an 8-year minimum gap between counted peaks) mechanically
deletes short spacings and therefore *biases the test toward* the folk claim — and it fails anyway;
and this is a **generous** test, an 8-to-11-year-wide acceptance band around the claimed center, not
a strict point estimate. **RE2** tested the Kuznets 15–25-year swing directly on investment/GDP
across **177 spacings in 18 countries**: pooled median spacing **11 years**, only **25%** inside the
claimed [15, 25]-year band against the same ≥50% bar. **FAIL, more cleanly than RE1.** The pre/post-
1950 split (12y median, n=82, versus 11y, n=95) shows no "passing of the Kuznets cycle" *direction*
on this tool either — consistent with Abramovitz's mechanism-level verdict (§B.1) without this crude
spacing-based tool being able to confirm or refute his own, amplitude-based, reasoning.

**Why these numbers, not narrower ones, are the honest read.** Three further reasons compound the
panel result, none of them new to this chapter but each worth restating together in one place.
**Tiny effective n per country**: even the UK's best-in-record three-peak chronology falls short of
the Contract's own ≥4-period bar, and most countries in §C.10 sit at n=1 or below — a fixed-period
claim asserted on this little data, in any single country, would not clear the desk's bar for a Tier-B
promotion under any other circumstance. **Structural breaks**: every case in this record crosses at
least one regime discontinuity that a fixed clock implicitly assumes away — interest-rate regimes
(gold standard to Bretton Woods to float; the Volcker disinflation; the 2008–21 ZIRP/QE era; the
2022–24 hiking cycle), credit liberalization (UK's 1986 Big Bang, Nordic 1980s deregulation, Spain's
EU and euro accession, India's 1991 reforms layered on RERA/GST), and planning law (zoning regimes
that themselves evolve on decades-long, country-specific timetables) — Spain's own case (§C.6) shows
directly how a fixed-period extrapolation across a dictatorship-to-democracy regime change produces
nonsense. **The drift the desk already tracks**: FC2's own measured finding — financial-cycle length
lengthening from an 11-year to a 13-year median spacing post-1985 (`docs/cycles/12-financial-cycle.md`
front matter and Part A §A.2) — is, on its own terms, a *changing* period, and a claim of drift is
definitionally incompatible with a claim of a *fixed* period; the desk's own prior finding already
argued against this chapter's target before this chapter was written. **Pre-registration discipline**:
a genuine date claim, to be taken seriously at all, would need a pre-registered window declared before
the outcome is known and would need to survive it — Harrison (2005) and Foldvary (1997) are the
tradition's two genuine ex-ante instances of this discipline, and even they succeeded by landing a
multi-year window around a global financial crisis whose proximate trigger (subprime mortgage
securitization mechanics) has no naturally 18-year-periodic driver of its own; a school with many
practitioners, many permissible starting-date choices, and a low bar for declaring a multi-year window
a "hit" will, absent pre-registration, always look better in hindsight than a formal test — exactly
`research/CONTRACT.md` §9's deflated-Sharpe-with-true-trial-count logic, applied to a forecasting
school instead of a backtest grid.

**The lane's own verdict, entered and standing.** `research/register/heuristics-lane.md`'s entry
`HL-6` closes the loop this chapter opened, and is quoted here rather than restated in different
words, because it is the record: *"mechanism ACCEPTED but already seated in L12 (no double-count);
the FIXED PERIOD fails both lane tests — no mechanism produces a constant period (rate/credit/
planning regimes shift it), and evidence fails the pre-registered bar (RE1: 45% of spacings in
[14,22]y, range 8-45y; ledger). First lane rejection — the lane demonstrably filters, not
rubber-stamps."* This is precisely the outcome the heuristics-lane charter (§A.3 above) was designed
to be capable of reaching: mechanism-backed rules get a fair, mechanism-first hearing rather than a
reflexive statistical veto — and a rule that fails on both counts (no constant-period mechanism, and
a pre-registered evidence bar it does not clear) is rejected with its full reasoning kept on the
record, not silently dropped.

**What this verdict does not close.** Rejecting the fixed period is not a claim that the underlying
spacing distribution is forever settled at RE1's measured 14-year median with a 45% in-window share —
it is a claim that *this* panel vintage, tested against *this* pre-registered bar, fails it. L12's
own Part E monitor step already commits to an annual re-run of FC1–FC3 with new data
(`docs/cycles/12-financial-cycle.md` Part E); RE1 and RE2 are re-printed alongside that same re-run
(`partDH-verdict-machinery.md` Part F), so a genuine narrowing of the spacing distribution in future

## Part D — The mathematics of a clock claim, and what RE1 measured instead

A fixed-period ("clock") claim is a renewal-process claim: peak-to-peak spacings drawn from a
TIGHT distribution around the period. The desk's test is exactly that (pre-registered:
median inside the claimed window AND ≥50% of spacings inside it — a deliberately generous
8-to-11-year-wide window). What RE1 printed is a WIDE renewal distribution: median 14y,
IQR 10–17y, range 8–45y, per-country medians 10–20y, 45% in-window. Descriptively (same
print, no new trial): the spacing distribution's spread is of the same order as its center —
a clock whose tick varies by ±50% is a regime, not a clock. RE2 (Kuznets, investment/GDP)
fails wider still: median 11y, 25% in [15,25]y.

Two honesty notes carried from the results file: (i) the min_gap=8y construction DELETES
short spacings and therefore biases the test TOWARD the folk claim — it fails anyway;
(ii) RE2's tool measures spacing, Abramovitz measured amplitude — RE2 rejects the clock
reading of Kuznets, not Abramovitz's historiography.

What SURVIVES the math: real house-price peaks space at a decade-plus scale everywhere —
slower than business cycles, consistent with FC2's lengthening watch. That slowness is
information L12 already carries as a state; no date is extractable from a distribution this
wide (the credit monograph's AUROC framing applies unchanged: ordering, not timing).
