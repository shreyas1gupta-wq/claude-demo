## A. The dollar cycle, strongest form (entry 2.9)

### A.1 The swing historiography, dated and sized

The claim to argue in its strongest form first: that the broad dollar has moved through a sequence
of multi-year appreciation and depreciation swings since the breakdown of Bretton Woods, each
swing large enough and long enough to matter for an EM equity book, and that naming and dating
those swings honestly — without smoothing them into a metronome — is itself useful CONTEXT even
before any clock claim is tested. Five swings carry the record from 1978 to the present.

**1978–1985 — the Volcker/Reagan super-dollar.** The dollar's ascent through the first half of the
1980s is the largest sustained appreciation in the post-Bretton-Woods float. On a trade-weighted
basis, the U.S. dollar appreciated **77% from its low in mid-1980 to its peak in February 1985**
**[Verified — Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, "The Dollar in the Eighties" (1985), and
consistent secondary reporting]**; the narrower six-currency U.S. Dollar Index (DXY) — a different
basket construction than the Fed's own broad trade-weighted series, and the index this monograph's
later swings are measured against for comparability — reached an all-time high of **164.72 in
February 1985** **[Verified]**, having nearly doubled off its own 1980 base. The task's own working
prior of "+~50% real" for this swing is close to, but not identical to, the verified nominal
trade-weighted figure above: this session's search access confirms the 77% nominal trade-weighted
appreciation cleanly but did not turn up an independently sourced *real* (inflation-adjusted,
bilateral-terms) point estimate specifically **[VERIFY: the precise real-terms magnitude commonly
cited in the international-macro literature (e.g., Frankel-style G-10 real effective exchange rate
work) — treat "+~50% real" as a plausible literature figure, not one this session pulled from a
primary source]**. The driving mechanism is not in dispute regardless of which magnitude is used:
Volcker's disinflation (Part B.1 below) pushed U.S. real rates sharply positive precisely as
Reagan-era fiscal expansion widened the federal deficit, the textbook twin-deficits/high-real-rate
combination pulling in capital and bidding up the currency throughout a period during which no
coordinated policy response existed to check it. That absence of coordination is exactly what ended
the swing: the **Plaza Accord**, signed by the G5 finance ministers and central bank governors at
New York's Plaza Hotel on **22 September 1985** **[Verified]**, committed the United States, Japan,
West Germany, France and the United Kingdom to coordinated intervention to bring the dollar down
from what all five now agreed was an unsustainable overvaluation. The dollar began falling
immediately: DXY was down to roughly **140 by the end of 1985** and had fallen a further, sharper
leg to around **90 by 1987** — a cumulative decline of roughly **45% from the February 1985 peak**
**[Verified — consistent secondary reporting]**. By early 1987 the decline itself had become the
policy problem: the dollar had fallen an estimated **23% from the Plaza level with no sign of
stopping**, prompting the **Louvre Accord**, signed by the G6 in Paris on **22 February 1987**
**[Verified]**, in which the same coalition (minus Italy, which declined to sign) agreed to
stabilize exchange rates around then-current levels, its communiqué declaring currencies "now
within ranges broadly consistent with underlying economic fundamentals" **[Verified]**. The
1978–1987 decade is therefore not one swing but two policy-terminated legs of a single named
episode — a fact this chapter returns to in A.4, because "the Volcker/Reagan super-dollar" reads,
in hindsight, as a clean multi-year swing only if the two accords that ended it are treated as
incidental rather than as the actual termination mechanism.

**1995–2002 — the tech-era dollar, and Asia 1997 as partially a dollar event.** A second, longer
ascent ran through the back half of the 1990s, carrying the dollar from a mid-1990s base in the
80s (DXY) to a peak near **120 in early 2002**, the tail end of what one industry retrospective
labels the "Clinton-era dollar boom, 1995–2001" **[Verified via search, approximate — VERIFY exact
peak date/level to a primary source]**. This swing's most consequential transmission to EM markets
sits inside its early years rather than at its peak, and the atlas's own framing of it — Asia 1997
"partially a dollar event" — earns its qualifier precisely rather than loosely. Thailand had pegged
the baht at roughly **25 to the U.S. dollar from 1984 until 2 July 1997**, when the Bank of
Thailand, unable to defend the peg with its own reserves, floated the currency for the first time
in 14 years **[Verified]**; the baht had lost roughly half its dollar value by year-end (from ~25 to
~56/$), with contagion running through Indonesia, South Korea, Malaysia and the Philippines within
months **[Verified]**. The dollar's own strength through this window is one documented contributing
force, not the sole cause: a rising dollar, carrying pegged Asian currencies up with it against a
simultaneously weak Japanese yen, eroded the export competitiveness these economies had built their
growth models on, compounding the capital-account fragilities (short-term dollar-denominated
external debt, current-account deficits) that made the peg indefensible once foreign capital began
to reverse **[Verified — Fed and Federal Reserve History accounts of the crisis's origins]**. This
is precisely Calvo's sudden-stop mechanism (`docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` A.2v) operating a full
sixteen years before the paper that named it, on a currency-peg regime the modern floating-INR
framework does not share — cited here as the dollar-strength swing's own transmission case, not
re-derived, because Calvo's own mechanism paper is already this program's citation for it.

**2002–2011 — the long slide, the commodity supercycle's dollar face.** The dollar's post-2002
decline is the mirror image of both prior swings and the cleanest illustration of the terms-of-trade
channel the atlas flags for India specifically (row 63, Atlas entry 1.3): DXY fell from its ~120
2002 peak to **70.70 by 2008 — the lowest level in the index's modern history** **[Verified]**,
recovered modestly through the financial crisis's safe-haven bid, then re-tested a low in the low
70s around 2011 **[VERIFY: exact 2011 trough level and date — this session's search access
confirmed the 2008 record low precisely but not an independently sourced 2011 print]** before the
next swing (below) began. This decade of dollar weakness runs almost exactly alongside the
commodity supercycle's own up-leg — this program's own CS1/CS1b trial (`research/register/
trial-ledger.md`) finds the broad commodity-price index's own troughs spaced 15–21 years (median
18y), rejecting the literature's claimed 30–40-year supercycle period while confirming a real
15–20-year arc, and CS2 finds a genuine broad common factor across metals/energy/agriculture
(89% of pairwise 10-year correlations positive) — the up-leg spanning roughly this same 2002–2011
window on the atlas's own dating. The mechanism linking the two swings is the same terms-of-trade
channel `docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` row 64 (H53) already names for India: a weak dollar generally
coincides with strong dollar-priced commodity prices (commodities are, by convention, dollar-
denominated; a weaker dollar mechanically supports their nominal price even before any real-demand
story), which for a net commodity importer running a persistent current-account deficit is a
double exposure rather than a hedge — this chapter cross-references `docs/cycles/14-commodity-
supercycle.md` for the full mechanism and does not re-derive it.

**2011/2014–2022 — the QT-era climb to DXY 114.** The dollar's fourth swing began building from
the 2011 trough, gathered pace once the Fed's 2013 taper *talk* (Part B.5 below) and 2014 actual
taper diverged U.S. policy from a still-easing ECB and Bank of Japan, and ran — through the 2015–18
hiking campaign, a 2020 COVID-driven spike-and-partial-retreat, and finally the 2022–23 tightening
campaign that Part B.1 documents as the fastest since Volcker — to an intraday peak of **114 on DXY
in September 2022**, with some measures printing a cycle high near 115 **[Verified]**. This is the
swing the atlas's own row 85 anchors its "inside L9" framing on most directly, because its terminal
leg (2022) is also the clearest recent instance of the Bruno-Shin leverage channel and the
Gabaix-Maggiori balance-sheet channel (`docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` A.2ii–iii) operating in
textbook form: the fastest Fed tightening in four decades tightened global-bank balance-sheet
capacity in lockstep with the dollar's own rise, and L9's triad — dollar, VIX, DFII10 — moved
together for most of 2022, the literal Rey signature this seat is built to register.

**2023–2026 — the contested plateau.** The dollar's decline off its September 2022 peak
accompanied the Fed's own pivot: after holding at a terminal **5.25–5.50%** through mid-2024, the
FOMC began cutting in September 2024 and delivered a cumulative **175 basis points across six
cuts** by the end of 2025, taking the effective funds rate from **5.33% to the 3.50–3.75% range**
**[Verified]**. DXY drifted down through this easing campaign into the high-90s/low-100s and, as of
this writing, traded at **99.42–99.66 on 1 September 2026**, with major-bank forecasts clustering
on a **92–100 range through the second half of 2026** and several houses expecting a mid-90s
year-end print **[Verified — VERIFY current: fast-moving; the print above is a specific date-stamped
level, not a settled level]**. What makes 2023–2026 genuinely a "contested plateau" rather than a
completed fifth swing is a live policy-regime question this chapter's Part B.1 and B.5 return to
directly: Kevin Warsh was confirmed by the Senate as the Federal Reserve's new chair on **13 May
2026** and sworn in on **22 May 2026**, succeeding Jerome Powell (who remains a Fed governor through
the balance of his own separate term) **[Verified]**; Warsh's public remarks at the August 2026
Jackson Hole symposium signaled a materially more hawkish reaction function than the Powell-era
committee's, lifting market-implied odds of a **September 2026 rate hike to roughly 60%** even as
the median FOMC dot plot, as recently as its 2026 projections, still penciled in one further cut
for the year **[Verified — VERIFY current, this is a live, unresolved policy question at the date
of writing]**. The dollar's own 2026 range sits precisely where a desk should expect it to sit when
the swing's own terminating mechanism (a genuine Fed pivot, per Part B.4/G.3 of the global-cycle
monograph) is itself contested rather than settled — exactly the honest, unresolved present tense
this chapter's Part C returns to when it explains why 2.9/2.10 can never be dated in advance.

### A.2 The dollar smile — a regime map, not a clock

**Stephen Jen**, then a currency strategist at Morgan Stanley, introduced the **"dollar smile"**
framework in **2001** **[Verified]**. Plotted against a horizontal axis of global growth
conditions, the dollar's own strength traces a smile-shaped curve rather than a straight line: at
the **left** end (global risk-off, a genuine crisis), the dollar strengthens on safe-haven demand
even as the U.S. economy itself may be weak, because global capital flees *to* dollar-denominated
safety rather than *from* it; at the **right** end (a genuinely U.S.-outperforming expansion,
domestic growth and rate differentials both favoring the dollar), the dollar strengthens again, this
time on fundamentals rather than fear; and in the **middle** — synchronized, calm, broadly shared
global growth, nobody's currency uniquely favored — the dollar tends to *weaken*, as capital
disperses toward higher-yielding opportunities abroad rather than concentrating in U.S. assets
**[Verified]**.

The framework's honesty, and the reason this chapter treats it as CONTEXT rather than as an input
carrying its own allocation authority, is that it names two *causally distinct* mechanisms that
happen to produce the *same observable* (a strong dollar) and refuses to collapse them into one
story. That refusal is not a rhetorical nicety — it is, read against `docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md`
A.2ii–iv, a restatement in plain language of the same two channels L9's own triad is built to
distinguish jointly rather than infer from any single leg: the smile's left-hand (crisis) strength
is Krishnamurthy-Vissing-Jorgensen's convenience-yield/safe-asset-shortage mechanism (A.2iv) —
capital bidding for the liquidity and collateral value only U.S. Treasuries uniquely supply — read
through L9's DFII10 leg; the smile's right-hand (US-outperformance) strength is the plain rate-
differential and Bruno-Shin leverage-channel story (A.2ii) — Fed tightening relative to the rest of
the world drawing capital toward higher U.S. yields — read through L9's dollar and policy-rate legs
together. A framework that can name "the dollar strengthens for two opposite reasons" without being
able to say, from the dollar's level alone, *which* reason is currently operating is precisely a
**regime map**: useful for briefing which story is live (a genuinely useful Stage-2 red-team
question — is this rally fear or strength?), useless as a **clock** (it carries no periodicity, no
implied turning date, and no standalone forecasting content beyond what the triad it restates
already reads). This is exactly why the smile earns a place in Part C's synthesis as CONTEXT
briefing language and nothing more: it is L9's own construction, narrated in a shape a chart can
show, not a second measurement of anything L9 does not already measure.

### A.3 The EM-headwind evidence

The atlas's own sentence for 2.9 — "EM equity's single most reliable macro headwind" — is a strong
claim, and the evidence behind it is not this chapter's to re-derive; it is `docs/cycles/
22-global-cycle.md`'s own, cited here in its Fed/dollar-specific application rather than repeated
in full. **Bruno, Valentina & Shin, Hyun Song (2015)**, in both the *Review of Economic Studies*
cross-border-banking paper and the companion *Journal of Monetary Economics* risk-taking-channel
paper **[both Verified — RES 82(2):535-564; JME 71:119-132, cited in full at global-cycle A.2ii]**,
supply the leverage mechanism: global banks' balance-sheet leverage is procyclical, so dollar
weakness (or a falling VIX, or easy U.S. policy) mechanically loosens the same risk-management
constraint that governs cross-border lending into EM credit and portfolio flows, and dollar
strength tightens it — fast, and from the lender's side, not merely the borrower's. **Avdjiev,
Stefan; Bruno, Valentina; Koch, Catherine & Shin, Hyun Song (2019)**, *IMF Economic Review* 67:
151–173 **[Verified — cited in full at global-cycle A.2ii]**, extend this directly to the broad
dollar index as a standalone risk factor: a stronger dollar tightens the effective credit
conditions facing dollar-borrowing EM firms and banks from the lender's own value-at-risk
perspective, independent of any separate rate-differential channel — precisely why the dollar sits
inside L9's triad as a risk-state variable in its own right rather than a mere restatement of U.S.
policy rates. Both papers are this program's own citation base for L9's construction already; this
chapter's only addition is naming them as the specific mechanism behind atlas row 2.9's own
headline sentence, rather than behind the global cycle in general.

**The desk's own check — GF2 and the still-pending DL2.** Rather than assume the mechanism transfers

### A.4 Why "7–10y swings" is a description, not a clock

Five historical swings, five different lengths when honestly dated by their own turning points
rather than forced into a common template: 1978–1985 to the Plaza Accord is roughly seven years;
1985's Plaza-to-Louvre leg is barely eighteen months; 1995–2002 to the tech-era peak is roughly
seven years; 2002–2011 is roughly nine years; 2011/2014–2022 is somewhere between eight and eleven
years depending on which trough is treated as the swing's true start; 2023–2026 and counting is
three-plus years into a swing whose own terminating event (Part A.1's "contested plateau") has not
yet resolved. This spread — a genuine range clustering loosely around seven to ten years, but never
landing on a fixed period — is exactly the shape the atlas's own §0 epistemics course predicts for
almost everything on this ladder: **persistence, not periodicity**. The desk does not get to treat
"the dollar cycle" as an exception to a principle it applies rigorously everywhere else on the
ladder, and two independent lines of evidence say it should not.

**The frequency-sweep prior.** `docs/cycles/19-kitchin-juglar.md` Part D states the result plainly:
this program has now pre-registered and tested **five** separate claimed fixed periods spanning
three orders of magnitude — 40 months (Kitchin, KJ1), 4–5 years (the business cycle proper, BC1),
18 years (the folk real-estate clock, RE1), 30–40 years (the commodity supercycle, CS1), and 45–60
years (Kondratieff, KW1) — and **found zero surviving fixed clocks**, while finding **real,
persistent bands** at several of those same resolutions (a 3–7-year growth band, a 10–20-year
financial-cycle band, a 15–21-year commodity-arc band). "States and bands, never dates" is, per
that chapter's own framing, no longer merely a design principle — it is a **measured result** every
future period claim on this ladder inherits as its prior, and DL1 is precisely such a future period
claim: a "~7–10-year dollar swing" occupies exactly the middle of the tested spectrum, between
BC1's real 3–7-year band and RE1's rejected 18-year folk clock, which is the single most
informative fact available about DL1 before it has even run. A desk that watched five consecutive
fixed-period claims fail the identical spacing-and-share test does not get to walk into a sixth
expecting a different outcome merely because this one carries a currency-market label rather than
a bank-clearings or inventory-cycle one.

**DL1 itself, pending.** `research/register/trial-ledger.md` records DL1's own pre-registered
construction precisely: peaks of a real equal-weight dollar index (chained mean of
Δlog exchange-rate-minus-inflation-differential across the JST panel's non-U.S. currencies,
1950–2015), 3-year-smoothed, minimum 4-year gap between extrema — the claim passes only if the
**median peak-to-peak spacing lands in [7,10] years AND at least half of all spacings fall in
[6,11] years**. This is, notably, a *tighter* bar than several of the five failed tests above (RE1's
own [14,22]-year window, for comparison, is a nine-year-wide band around an eighteen-year claim; DL1's
[6,11]-year window around a seven-to-ten-year claim is comparably tight relative to its own claimed
midpoint), which means DL1 is, if anything, a harder test to pass than the sweep's own precedent —
appropriate, because a genuinely useful "dollar cycle" clock claim ought to be held to at least as
demanding a bar as the four fixed-period claims that have already failed it. Until DL1 runs, the
honest, pre-committed prior this chapter states in advance — precisely the discipline the sweep's
own house style requires — is that a seven-to-ten-year *description* of past swings is very likely
to remain exactly that: an accurate historiography of five dated episodes of different length, not
a clock this desk is licensed to use for timing the next one.

## B. The Fed cycle (entry 2.10)

### B.1 Campaign historiography since Volcker

The Fed's own tightening and easing campaigns since 1979 are the direct driver Part A.1's dollar
swings sit on top of, and dating them with the same discipline earns the same honest, irregular
picture. **Hiking campaigns**, in sequence: **1979–1982**, the Volcker disinflation itself — the
target rate raised in stages from **11% (August 1979) to a peak of 20%**, with the well-documented
"double dip" of a 1980 recession, brief 1980 easing, and a second 1981 tightening leg before the
terminal peak **[Verified generally; exact monthly peak date and the two-peak shape are widely
documented but not independently re-pulled to a primary FRED series this session — VERIFY precise
dating]** — the campaign that triggered the deepest post-war recession to that date (unemployment
peaking near 10.8% in late 1982) and, per Part A.1, the entire 1978–1985 dollar super-cycle.
**1983–1984**, a shorter, smaller re-tightening reinforcing the still-rising dollar rather than a
separately named swing **[VERIFY exact magnitude — this session's search access confirmed the
episode's existence and motivation (resurgent inflation concern) but not a specific verified rate
path]**. **1986–1989**, a tightening campaign complicated mid-course by the October 1987 crash (the
Greenspan put's own birth, Part B.3 below), which paused hikes briefly before the campaign resumed
and continued through 1989 **[VERIFY exact rate path]**. **1994–1995**, "the perfect soft landing"
— seven hikes taking the funds rate from **3% to 6%** between February 1994 and February 1995
**[Verified]**, widely regarded as the cleanest instance on record of a preemptive tightening
campaign that cooled the economy without triggering a recession. **1999–2000**, a hiking cycle
raising the funds rate roughly from the high-4s to 6.5% **[VERIFY exact start/end figures — commonly
cited but not independently re-confirmed to a primary source this session]**, ending just ahead of
the 2001 recession and dot-com bust. **2004–2006**, "the measured-pace" campaign — seventeen
consecutive 25-basis-point hikes taking the funds rate from **1% (June 2004) to 5.25% (June 2006)**
**[Verified]**, its own FOMC statements literally naming the pace ("policy accommodation can be
removed at a pace that is likely to be measured," introduced May 2004, repeated across the next
twelve statements before being modified in December 2005 and dropped in January 2006) **[Verified]**
— Part B.2 returns to this language directly. **2015–2018**, the post-GFC normalization campaign,
from the **December 2015 liftoff (0–0.25%) to 2.25–2.50% by December 2018** **[Verified]**.
**2022–2023**, the fastest tightening campaign since Volcker — **525 basis points delivered in 16
months** starting March 2022, taking the funds rate to a terminal **5.25–5.50% by July 2023**
**[Verified: 525bp/16-month figure and "fastest since the 1980s" characterization; terminal level
and July 2023 date per widely documented, standard central-bank chronology]**.

**Easing campaigns**, dated with the same care: **2007–2008**, the Global Financial Crisis response,
cutting from **5.25% (September 2007) to the 0–0.25% zero lower bound (December 2008)** — the
opening act of the unconventional-policy era **[well-documented, general knowledge; not
independently re-searched this session]**. **2019–2020**, itself two distinct episodes the atlas's
own framing correctly separates: the **2019 "mid-cycle adjustment"** — three 25-basis-point cuts in
July, September and October 2019, taking the funds rate from **2.25–2.50% to 1.50–1.75%**, which
Fed officials themselves characterized at the time as "a recalibration of the stance of policy... not
part of a pre-set course" rather than the opening of a full easing cycle **[Verified]** — followed,
once the COVID-19 pandemic arrived, by the genuinely emergency phase: **two intermeeting cuts in
March 2020** slashing the funds rate straight to the **0–0.25% zero lower bound** **[Verified]**.
And **2024–2026**, the campaign still contested at time of writing (Part A.1 above): six cuts
totaling **175 basis points from September 2024 through late 2025**, taking the effective rate from
**5.33% to 3.50–3.75%**, followed by four consecutive 2026 FOMC holds through at least July 2026,
with the median dot plot as of the most recent projections still pencilling in one further 2026 cut
even as new Fed Chair Kevin Warsh's own August 2026 Jackson Hole remarks lifted market-implied
September-2026 **hike** odds to roughly 60% **[Verified — VERIFY current: this is, again, a live and
explicitly unresolved policy question at the date of this writing, not a completed campaign this
chapter can date end-to-end]**.

Eight hiking campaigns (counting 1979–82 and its own internal 1980 pause-relapse as one), four
easing campaigns of genuinely different character (a slow post-GFC descent, a small mid-cycle
recalibration, a true emergency response, and a still-contested 2024–26 campaign whose own
terminating shape is not yet known) — a historical record of real, dated, differently-sized and
differently-motivated episodes, which is precisely the material Part B.4 below argues cannot be
compressed into a fixed 3–5-year clock any more than Part A.4 could compress the dollar's own five
swings into a fixed 7–10-year one.

### B.2 The FOMC's own cycle language

The Fed's own institutional language is itself worth reading carefully, because it shows a
sophisticated, self-aware body that talks in regime and campaign terms — never in fixed-period
terms — even while running the single most closely-watched, most-anticipated policy decision in
global markets. The **"measured pace"** language (B.1 above) is the clearest instance: a phrase
introduced specifically to signal a *data-dependent, meeting-by-meeting* tightening cadence rather
than a fixed timetable, retained for exactly as long as the Committee judged it useful (twelve
statements), and explicitly modified once conditions changed — the opposite of a clock, stated in
the clock's own vocabulary. The **2019 "mid-cycle adjustment"/"recalibration"** framing (B.1 above)
is the same discipline running the other direction: the Committee went out of its way, in its own
minutes, to distinguish a *risk-management* insurance cut from the *opening move of a full easing
cycle* — a distinction that mattered enormously for how markets should have read the campaign's own
likely length, and one the Fed itself drew explicitly rather than leaving to outside interpretation.
This is the same object `docs/cycles/20-mp-cycle.md`'s own L6 construction reads mechanically for
the RBI's side of the transmission — a lagged, ~1-year classification of loose/tight *stance*, built
specifically so the seat's own classification cannot be moved by how excitingly or ambiguously a
press conference or statement was read (`docs/cycles/20-mp-cycle.md` G.1's own countermeasure) — and
this chapter's own point in raising it is the mirror-image one: the Fed's *own* language already
refuses the fixed-clock framing this seat's name might otherwise invite, which is one more piece of
evidence, from the object's own source, that "the Fed cycle" was never a clock to begin with.

### B.3 The Fed put, treated honestly

**Origins and growth.** The "Greenspan put" — the market's own name for the Fed's revealed
tendency to ease policy or supply liquidity whenever asset prices fall sharply enough — traces its
own origin to a dated, specific event: the **19 October 1987 stock-market crash**, after which,
concerned the unprecedented one-day decline might spread into credit and liquidity problems,
Greenspan's Fed opened its liquidity facilities and followed with rate cuts **[Verified]**. The
notion grew explicitly in **1998**, when the Fed cut rates in response to the deteriorating state of
global credit markets around the Russian default and the Long-Term Capital Management collapse
**[Verified]**, and by **2001** the idea of a standing "Greenspan put" had become widespread
market vocabulary **[Verified]**. The lineage continued under Greenspan's successors — a "Bernanke
put" implicit in the GFC-era quantitative-easing response — and reached, on the market's own
telling, its most aggressive form yet under Powell: repo-market operations supplied to Wall Street
dealers in **2019**, followed by direct large-scale quantitative easing in **2020** to counter the
pandemic's financial-market effects, with Bloomberg reporting in **November 2020** that "the Powell
put was stronger than both the Greenspan put or the Bernanke put" **[Verified]**.

**2022 as the honest limit — a reaction-function regime, not a myth.** The task this chapter was
set names the correct framing precisely: the put is "real behavior, but a REACTION FUNCTION regime
that shifts," and 2022 is the year that shift became undeniable. U.S. equities fell sharply through
2022 even as inflation ran at multi-decade highs — CPI is widely reported to have peaked near 9%
year-on-year around mid-2022 **[VERIFY: this session did not independently re-search the precise
peak print and date; treat as a well-established figure not re-verified here]** — and the Fed's own
response was not the put's textbook script. Rather than easing into the equity decline the way
1987, 1998, 2019 and 2020 would have predicted, the Committee delivered exactly the **525
basis points in 16 months** documented in B.1, the fastest tightening since Volcker, precisely
*because* the inflation print made the traditional put's script unaffordable. This is the single
cleanest illustration available of why "the Fed put" is honestly described as **real, observed
behavior operating under a specific, historically contingent reaction function** rather than as an
unconditional promise: the put's presence across 1987/1998/2019/2020 reflects a Fed reacting to
falling asset prices *when doing so was consistent with its inflation mandate*; 2022 reflects the
identical institution, under the identical dual mandate, reaching the opposite conclusion once
falling asset prices and unacceptably high inflation coincided. The honest, tradeable content of
"the Fed put" is therefore not "buy the dip because the Fed always rescues markets" — a claim 2022
falsifies directly — but "the Fed's own reaction function has a strike price stated in **inflation**
terms, not in equity-index terms, and that strike price only becomes visible once inflation itself
is the binding constraint." This is a REGIME description a desk can use for context (Stage-2
red-team: is the current disinflation path wide enough for a 1998-style put to be back in play, or
narrow enough that 2022's constraint still binds?) — never a forecast of when the Fed will next
ease, which is exactly the refusal Part C makes explicit.

### B.4 The transmission: the Fed cycle drives the dollar leg and nests inside the global cycle

The mechanism connecting B.1's dated campaigns to A.1's dated dollar swings is not exotic — U.S.
policy-rate moves shift the rate differential between dollar assets and the rest of the world
directly, and Part A.2's dollar-smile right-hand-side (US-outperformance strength) is precisely
this channel in its plainest form — but `docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` A.2iii (Gabaix-Maggiori)
supplies the reason the transmission is faster and larger than simple uncovered-interest-parity
arithmetic would predict: exchange rates are themselves prices set by financial intermediaries with
*limited risk-bearing capacity*, so a shift in capital flows — driven by anything that changes those
intermediaries' willingness to hold currency risk, a Fed hiking campaign very much included — moves
the exchange rate directly and more persistently than a textbook rate-differential story alone would
generate. **DL3**, this program's own pre-registered, narrowly-scoped test of this specific
channel — correlation of the annual change in the U.S. short rate (`stir_USA` on the JST panel)
against the **same-year and next-year** change in the real equal-weight dollar index — is,
per `research/register/trial-ledger.md`, **measurement only, no pass/fail bar, prior set**, and is
**PENDING** at time of writing. This chapter states its construction and status honestly rather than
inventing a lag profile DL3 has not yet measured; what can be said in advance is only that the
seat's own construction (a *same-year and next-year* window, rather than a longer lag) is built to
detect a fast transmission, consistent with `docs/cycles/20-mp-cycle.md`'s own MP2 finding (the
sign flip: contemporaneous corr(Δrate, credit growth) is positive — central banks tighten *into*
booms — while the peak *negative* effect lands at +1 year) that monetary-policy transmission inside
this program's own machinery generally shows its cleanest signature within a year, not across
several.

**The nesting, stated in the global-cycle monograph's own words.** `docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md`
A.4 could not be more explicit about where 2.9 and 2.10 sit, and this chapter quotes it directly
rather than paraphrasing around it: **"Dollar-cycle or Fed-cycle DATING — atlas rows 2.9 (the
~7–10-year dollar cycle) and 2.10 (the 3–5-year Fed cycle) are folded sub-faces of this same seat,
explicitly listed in the atlas as living 'inside L9,' never separately dated or forecast: the
ladder's own discipline is to state the current triad reading, never to call when the dollar cycle
'should' turn or when the next Fed easing cycle 'should' begin."** L9's own construction
(`ladder.yaml L9_global_financial_cycle`, τ½ 3–9 months, 20% of regime-score budget,
`reduce_only: false`) already reads the U.S. policy-rate leg and the broad-dollar leg as two of its
own four triad inputs (`docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` A.2i) — which means 2.9 and 2.10 are not
waiting to be incorporated into L9's design; they are, and have been since the ladder's first draft,
two of the five free series L9's algorithm already consumes.

### B.5 India's Fed-cycle experience, compressed

Every Fed hiking campaign's transmission to India is, per this chapter's own governing discipline,
**already the global monograph's episode record** — `docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` A.3iii (the
2013 taper tantrum, owned there in full: the Fragile Five anatomy, the 28% INR depreciation
April–August 2013, the 15–18% Nifty peak-to-trough decline, the MSF/FCNR(B) rates-side defense
cross-referenced to `docs/cycles/20-mp-cycle.md`) and A.3iv (the May-2026 episode, the atlas's own
live test case) are not re-derived, re-dated, or re-argued here. What this section adds is narrower
and Fed-specific: **which campaigns hurt India most, and why the CAD-dependence pattern, not the
campaign's own size or speed, is the variable that explains the spread.**

The starkest contrast in the record is between 2013 and 2022–23 — and it inverts the naive prior.
**2013** was not even a completed hiking campaign; it was a single congressional-testimony
*mention* of eventual tapering, on 22 May 2013, with no rate move attached, and it produced a
28% currency move and a Fragile-Five grouping. **2022–23** was an *actual* 525-basis-point hiking
campaign, the fastest since Volcker, delivered while India's own RBI ran a closely-matched domestic
campaign — repo raised from **4.40% (4 May 2022) through 4.90% (June), 5.40% (August), 5.90%
(September), 6.25% (December 2022) to 6.50% (8 February 2023)**, a cumulative **250 basis points**
**[Verified]** — and the rupee's own move was an order of magnitude smaller: INR fell roughly **8.9%
across all of 2022**, touching an all-time low near **₹81.94–82/$ in late September 2022**
**[Verified]**, alongside a reserve drawdown from **~$633bn (start 2022) to ~$545bn (September
2022, −13.9%)** that RBI itself rebuilt by roughly **$58bn across 2023** **[Verified]** — a managed,
orderly currency and reserve path, not a Fragile-Five-style rout, despite a genuinely larger and
faster Fed campaign than 2013's mere mention of one.

The variable that explains the inversion is exactly the one Calvo's own sudden-stop precondition
(`docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` A.2v) names: the **size of the standing exposure at the moment
the global factor turns**, which domestic policy sets even though the timing of the turn does not.
India's current account deficit stood at a then-record **4.8% of GDP in FY2012–13**
**[Verified]** — the exact vulnerability Morgan Stanley's James Lord grouped India inside the
"Fragile Five" for — and had narrowed to roughly **2.0% of GDP by FY2022–23**
**[Verified]**, a structural halving achieved through the post-2013 policy response the mp-cycle
and global-cycle monographs already document (the 2016 inflation-targeting framework, sustained
multi-year reserve accretion, tighter external-commercial-borrowing and FPI-debt-ceiling norms). A
campaign twice as large and several times faster hurt India roughly a third as much on the currency
axis, because the exposure the campaign found waiting for it had itself shrunk by more than half in
the intervening decade — the single cleanest statement available of why this chapter's own header
claim ("the CAD-dependence pattern") is the load-bearing variable, not the Fed campaign's own
headline size.

The remaining campaigns fill out the pattern rather than contradicting it. **1994–95** and
**1999–2000** show muted, hard-to-isolate India transmission for a shared reason distinct from
CAD dynamics: both sit inside, or immediately adjacent to, the "pre-1990 analogue evidence discounts
on transfer" standing caveat GF1 itself established (`docs/cycles/22-global-cycle.md` — the global
factor's own pairwise co-movement rose from +0.28 pre-1990 to +0.77 post-1990) — India's capital
account was still thin and its FII regime newly opened (from 1992–93) through the mid-1990s, and
**1999–2000** additionally coincided with the domestically dominant Kargil conflict (May–July 1999),
an idiosyncratic local risk event crowding out whatever transfer the Fed's own contemporaneous
hiking campaign might otherwise have produced — precisely the kind of episode GF3's own **69%
breadth ceiling** licenses ("most places, most of the time," never "everywhere, always"): some
years belong to the residual, and 1999–2000's own India read is a plausible candidate. **2004–2006**
is this record's clearest domestic-dominance counter-example: India's own 2003–08 capex and credit
boom (`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` row 1.6) ran essentially uninterrupted straight through the Fed's own
measured-pace tightening, the domestic cycle's own phase simply mattering more than the global
leg for that particular multi-year window. **2015–2018** shows a third pattern worth naming
precisely because it is a caution rather than a confirmation: the NBFC/IL&FS stress that emerged
late in this hiking campaign (2018) is, per this program's own **SC1** trial
(`research/register/trial-ledger.md`, Atlas entry 2.2), a domestic credit-supply shock that
propagated into a broad SMB/market-wide event **within twelve months** on its own dynamics — a
finding the SC1 write-up itself reads as evidence for routing the funding-freeze signature to the
faster L2 stress layer rather than to any Fed-driven mechanism specifically — a reminder that not
every EM stress episode occurring inside a Fed hiking window is *caused* by the Fed leg, and that
attributing 2018's own India stress primarily to Fed tightening would over-read a genuinely
domestic event.

**The one-line synthesis.** India's own transfer coefficient in any given Fed campaign scales with
the CAD/reserve-buffer state the economy carries **into** that campaign, not with the campaign's
own size or speed — 2013's mere mention of tapering, landing on a 4.8%-of-GDP deficit and thin
buffers, produced a Fragile-Five-grade shock; 2022–23's actual 525-basis-point campaign, landing on
a 2.0%-of-GDP deficit and a policy framework built specifically to avoid a repeat, produced an
orderly, RBI-managed adjustment. This is the Fed-specific slice of exactly the finding GF3 already
established at the global-cycle level, applied here to the single sub-face the atlas names 2.10.

Fed's next move... the seat is deliberately built to make the forecasting temptation unnecessary

**The smile survives as CONTEXT briefing language, and nothing more.** Part A.2 already made the
