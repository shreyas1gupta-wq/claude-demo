**A scoping note that governs everything below.** DS1 defines "the fiat era" as **post-1971** — the
world after Bretton Woods' gold-convertibility peg ended — and finds only **4 of 18** JST-panel
countries completed a lasting ≥30pp deleveraging with a post-1971 peak (Belgium, Denmark, Ireland,
Netherlands, Sweden are the "YES" rows; Denmark's peak is 1998). Several of the case studies below —
the UK 1815–1914, the US 1946–1974, the UK 1946–1990s, France, and Japan's 1944–1950 arc — are **not**
tested by DS1 at all, because their peaks and their descents sit **before** or **straddle** the 1971
boundary. That is not a contradiction of DS1; it is the reason DS1 exists in this specific form. The
20th century's most dramatic successful descents happened under gold-standard or Bretton-Woods-era
monetary orders that made repression and pegged rates far easier to engineer than they are in an
open-capital-account, floating-rate, fiat world. Once the world crossed into the regime DS1 actually
measures, only five countries — all small, all open, all bound by EU/Eurozone convergence discipline
or a comparably hard external anchor — managed to complete the arc. That asymmetry is itself the
single most important synthesis finding of this Part, and it recurs throughout B2 and B3 below.

## B1. The resolution menu, with the panel numbers

The debt identity from Part D (Δb = (r−g)·b − pb + sfa) has exactly five channels through which a
sovereign has ever brought b down for good: **growth**, **primary surpluses**, **repression/inflation**,
**default/restructuring**, and **wealth levies**. History's verdict on how often each channel is
actually used, and how large its effect is when it works, is now reasonably well quantified.

### 1. Growth

Growth reduces b for free whenever r<g — DS3 already establishes that this was the operative
arithmetic for **83% of country-years in the 1945–80 repression era** and **73% of country-years
post-GFC (2008–2020)**, versus only **37–41%** of years in the two liberalization-era regimes
(1870–1944 gold/interwar; 1981–2007). Growth alone, however, is the weakest evidenced channel in
isolation: every episode below in which growth gets credit for delevering (UK 1815–1914, the "growth"
half of the US and UK WWII stories) turns out on closer inspection to have been growth **combined**
with negative real rates, primary surpluses, or both — a pattern **Acalin & Ball (2024, NBER WP 31577
/ IMF WP 2024/005, "Did the U.S. Really Grow Out of Its World War II Debt?")** make explicit for the
canonical growth case: their counterfactual removing surprise inflation and the 1942–51 rate peg shows
US debt/GDP would have fallen only to **74% by 1974**, not the actual **23%** — i.e. growth and primary
surpluses on their own explain roughly two-fifths of the historical decline, and the rest is
repression. "Growing out of it" alone is close to a myth even in its best-documented instance.

### 2. Primary surpluses

**Eichengreen & Panizza (2014), "A Surplus of Ambition: Can Europe Rely on Large Primary Surpluses to
Solve its Debt Problem?"** (*Economic Policy* 31(85), NBER WP 20316) is the definitive rarity study.
Across **54 advanced and emerging economies, 1974–2013** (235 five-year country-periods), they find:
- primary surpluses of **≥3% of GDP sustained for ≥5 years**: **36 episodes**, or **15%** of all
  five-year periods;
- the same **≥3% of GDP threshold sustained for ≥10 years**: only **12 instances** in the entire
  sample;
- surpluses of **≥4% of GDP sustained for ≥10 years**: "extremely rare" — effectively a handful of
  cases, always under exceptional circumstances (oil windfalls, post-crisis IMF programs, currency-
  union entry requirements);
- their own verdict on the general policy prescription: **no historical episode** shows a
  debt-stabilizing surplus **above roughly 3–3.5% of GDP for a prolonged period** without
  extraordinary, non-repeatable conditions attached.
The primary-surplus channel is real (it is exactly what delevers Belgium, Italy's 1990s, and Ireland
below) but it is the rarest of the five channels to sustain, and never sustainable at the magnitude
(3–5% of GDP for a decade) that would resolve a >100%-of-GDP debt stock on its own.

### 3. Financial repression and inflation

**Reinhart & Sbrancia (2011), "The Liquidation of Government Debt"** (NBER WP 16893; also IMF WP
2015/07) is the direct ancestor of DS2. Their 12-advanced-economy sample finds average annual
"liquidation" — the fiscal saving from negative real interest rates on the domestic-currency debt
stock — of **1–5% of GDP per year over 1945–80**, with the **US and UK specifically averaging 3–4% of
GDP per year**, and **Australia and Italy** (which ran hotter inflation) as high as **~5% per year**.
DS2's own replication confirms the mechanism at the country-year level: **44% of 1945–80 country-years**
had negative real short rates (mean real short rate **−0.8%**), against only **10%** in the
liberalization era (1981–2007, mean **+3.2%**) — and the **76%** post-GFC share (mean **−0.7%**) is,
as Part D already notes, the modern echo of exactly this mechanism. Repression is the *largest*
single-channel magnitude of the five (3–5% of GDP/year, sustained for **35 years** in the canonical
episode) precisely because it is the hardest for the person paying it to see or resist in real time —
an "unlegislated tax," in Reinhart-Sbrancia's own phrase, rather than a vote.

### 4. Default and restructuring

**Reinhart & Rogoff's "This Time Is Different" chronology** (2009 book; NBER WP 13882) counts
**250 sovereign external-debt default episodes since 1800** — roughly **12 defaults per decade** on
average across their up-to-66-country sample — plus a further **~70 episodes of domestic public debt
default or restructuring** identified separately in their companion "Forgotten History of Domestic
Debt" work, a category most of the earlier sovereign-debt literature had ignored entirely. External
defaults cluster: regionally (Latin America and southern Europe after the Napoleonic Wars), and
internationally (the 1930s Depression wave, the 1980s Latin American wave). The chronology's other
headline finding, directly relevant to the inflation channel above: **inflation in the three years
following a domestic default episode averages nearly 120%** across the sample — domestic default and
inflationary liquidation are not separate channels in practice so much as two points on one
continuum. Default is thus the *second most frequent* channel by episode count (250+ in two centuries)
but the *most concentrated in time* — it resolves the debt stock in one discrete event (PSI 2012 below
is the modern template) rather than the multi-decade grind of repression.

### 5. Wealth levies

The rarest, most discrete channel, and the one furthest from anything an Indian desk needs to model
as a recurring input — but it belongs on the menu because two of this Part's cases used it explicitly.
Post-WWII capital levies were enacted in **Belgium, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Japan,
Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Norway**. Japan's 1946–47 progressive property levy (*zaisan zei*)
fell on the richest 2–3 centiles of Japanese society and **raised over 10% of national income in the
single year it was collected**. Germany's 1952 *Lastenausgleich* ("Burden Equalization") required
undamaged property-holders to pay **one-half of their assessed 1948-reform property value**, in
installments over 30 years, and is credited with cutting Germany's top-1% wealth share by **~5.5
percentage points** — more than the physical destruction of the war itself. Both episodes required a
society-wide sense of shared postwar sacrifice that has no obvious peacetime analogue; the channel
appears in the historical record almost exclusively in the direct aftermath of total war.

**Which channels are even available depends on the r−g regime a country is in.** DS3 supplies the
governing fact: in the **1945–80 repression era**, mean (r−g) was **−4.3pp** and r<g held in **83% of
years** — debt could melt via growth+repression with primary balance doing almost no work. In the
**1981–2007 liberalization era**, mean (r−g) flipped to **+1.1pp**, r<g held in only **37% of years**
— and in that regime, per Eichengreen-Panizza, only sustained surpluses or outright default can bring
b down; growth and quiet repression stop being available for free. Case 8 below (Canada, Sweden,
Ireland) is precisely the set of countries that ran successful, non-repression consolidations *inside*
the liberalization regime — the hard version of the problem, solved through the primary-surplus and
expenditure channels rather than the repression channel that dominates every pre-1980 case.

## B2. Ten case studies

### 1. United Kingdom, 1815–1914 — the gold-standard-era, surplus-and-growth century

**Setup.** Twenty-two years of war against Revolutionary and Napoleonic France left Britain with the
highest debt/GDP of any country that has ever fully repaid its debt: outstanding debt of roughly
**£1 billion**, representing **~213% of GDP** at the 1815 peace and continuing to climb to **roughly
260% of GDP around 1819** as wartime obligations were finally settled. **The descent.** Over the
following century, without a single default and with only very brief episodes of deflation rather
than inflation, debt fell to **30% of GDP by 1914** — a **~230-percentage-point** decline, the largest
sustained deleveraging in the modern historical record, reaching a **200-year low of 29% of GDP** on
the eve of WWI in 1913–14. **Which terms did the work.** This was overwhelmingly a **growth-and-modest-
surplus** descent, not a repression or inflation descent: British consols paid a **3% coupon** against
**near-zero average inflation (~0.28%/year, 1692–1914)**, so gilt holders earned a real return close to
the nominal coupon throughout — the opposite of every 20th-century repression case below. Population
growth alone — Britain's population grew **2.4×** between 1815 and 1913 — mechanically reduced the
debt/GDP ratio by an estimated **~125 percentage points** of the total decline, with real per-capita
growth and modest primary surpluses (the gold standard's implicit commitment to eventual budget
balance) doing the rest. Consol price volatility itself **fell by more than half** after 1815, and
Dimson-Marsh-Staunton's 20th-century UK equity series shows **5.8%/year real** returns over
1900–2000 — this was, in every dimension, the friendliest possible environment for both bondholders
and equity holders to sit through a sovereign deleveraging. **Lesson for L15.** This is the proof that
a "painless" descent is possible without repression — but it required a full century, a monetary
regime (gold, with essentially zero structural inflation bias) that no longer exists anywhere, and a
demographic tailwind India cannot count on repeating on the same terms. It is the historical floor
case for how good the growth-and-surplus channel can be; it is not a template that transfers.

### 2. United States, 1946–1974 — the canonical repression case, decomposed

**Setup.** US federal debt held by the public reached **119% of GDP in FY1946** (some series report a
close but not identical **106%** figure depending on the exact debt concept and GNP-vs-GDP base
`[VERIFY: reconcile the 119% vs 106% series definitions]`), up from **45–50% of GDP in FY1941** — a
**~70-percentage-point** wartime run-up in five years. **The descent.** By **1974** the ratio had fallen
to roughly **23–32%** depending on series (Hall & Sargent's own 2011 AEJ:Macro paper puts the 1946→1974
move as an **~83-point** fall; the Fed's debt-held-by-public series shows **80.1%→25.3%** across the
slightly wider 1950–1975 window). **Which terms did the work — the decomposition.** Hall & Sargent
(2011, *AEJ: Macroeconomics*, "Interest Rate Risk and Other Determinants of Post-WWII US Government
Debt/GDP Dynamics") attribute a substantial share of the fall to **negative real returns via surprise
inflation** operating on the government's fixed-rate debt stock `[VERIFY: primary paper's exact
point-estimate split was not independently retrieved this pass]`. The more recent and more precisely
sourced reassessment, **Acalin & Ball (2024, NBER WP 31577 / IMF WP 2024/005)**, runs the sharpest test
available: a counterfactual removing (i) the **surprise inflation** of 1946–48 (CPI inflation reached
**~17.6%** in 1946–47) and (ii) the **1942–51 Fed-Treasury interest-rate peg** (short bill rates held
at **0.375%** throughout the war and Korea) shows debt/GDP would have fallen only to **74% by 1974**
— not the actual 23%. In other words: of the roughly 83-point actual fall, **growth and primary
surpluses alone explain only the ~32–45 points that would have happened anyway; the remaining
~40–51 points are repression** (surprise inflation plus the administered-rate peg). Acalin-Ball's own
words: "only a small amount of debt reduction has been achieved through growth rates that exceed
undistorted interest rates" — the US did **not**, on their reading, simply grow out of its WWII debt.
**Investor outcomes, in real terms.** US Treasury bonds recorded **four consecutive negative-real-return
decades starting with the 1940s**: **−2.63%/year real in the 1940s**, **−1.80%/year real in the 1950s**
— the worst and second-worst Treasury decades of the entire 1900–2020 record, coinciding exactly with
the peg years and its immediate aftermath. **Lesson for L15.** This is the highest-resolution evidence
in the entire dossier that the repression channel's magnitude is large (tens of percentage points of
debt/GDP over ~15 years) and falls specifically and disproportionately on **long-duration nominal
bondholders** — precisely the DS2/DS4 mechanism the gold floor is built to hedge, now decomposed down
to the instrument level.

### 3. United Kingdom, 1946–1990s — repression plus two devaluations

**Setup.** UK net public debt peaked at **~252% of GDP in FY1946/47** (some series cite a slightly
higher **~270%** shortly after). **The descent.** By **1971/72** debt/GDP had already fallen to
**62.1%**, and it continued down to **~50% of GDP** by the early 1990s. **Which terms did the work.**
Nominal GDP grew **8.8%/year on average** across the descent — **2.3%/year real growth plus 6.5%/year
average inflation** — while the interest rate on government debt sat **below** the inflation rate in
**24 of the 30 years** from 1950, the direct UK analogue of the US peg-and-surprise-inflation
mechanism, run for longer and at higher average inflation. Two step-devaluations punctuate the period:
**1949** (sterling cut ~30.5% against the dollar) and **1967** (a further ~14.3% cut, "another
devaluation and economic humiliation" in the contemporary phrase) — each one a discrete real write-down
of sterling-denominated external claims, layered on top of the continuous domestic repression. Budget
deficits **averaged only 1.2% of GDP** through the 1950s–60s: this was **not** a primary-surplus-led
descent at all — it is repression and growth doing nearly all of the work, with fiscal policy close to
balance rather than actively retiring debt. **Lesson for L15.** The UK case is the clean "double dose"
version of the US story: the same repression mechanism, run over a longer window (45 years vs the
US's ~28), reinforced by two devaluations that a country on a fixed peg (the US, effectively, in this
period) did not have available — a reminder that currency flexibility widens, not narrows, a sovereign's
menu for debasement.

### 4. Germany, 1919–1923 hyperinflation and 1948 currency reform — the hard-reset path, twice

**Setup.** Weimar Germany entered peace owing **98 billion marks in war bonds** to its own citizens on
top of Allied reparations obligations, with government revenue covering **never more than 35% of
outlays, 1919–23**. **The descent — hyperinflation.** The Reichsbank's financing of the deficit by
printing drove the mark from parity to **4.2 trillion marks per US dollar by November 1923** — a
default on the entire domestic debt stock via currency destruction rather than a legislated
repudiation. **Who paid.** War-bond holders and cash savers were **wiped out**; some revalued debts
(mortgages) allowed lenders partial recoupment, but the overwhelming transfer was from creditor/saver
households to debtor households and the state itself. **The second reset — 1948.** Postwar West
Germany's currency reform converted Reichsmark cash and bank deposits into new Deutsche Marks at
**100:1 for cash/deposits down to effectively ~6.5:1 for practical purposes, and 10:1 for debts and
most wages/prices** — a second, administratively engineered wipeout of nominal claims, again borne
overwhelmingly by savers rather than holders of physical assets (goods, companies, real estate), who
gained relative to cash-holders. A **1952 Lastenausgleich** capital levy (one-half of assessed 1948
property value, payable over 30 years) then redistributed a further slice from the undamaged to the
damaged, cutting the top-1% wealth share by an estimated **~5.5 percentage points** — larger than the
effect of wartime physical destruction itself. **Lesson for L15.** Germany is the dossier's clearest
demonstration that the "hard reset" path is not one channel but typically **two or three stacked
together** (hyperinflation, then a currency-reform devaluation of nominal claims, then in some cases a
capital levy) — and that in every layer, the group that pays is the same: holders of nominal claims
(bonds, cash, bank deposits), never holders of real/physical assets. This is the strongest single
historical argument, outside DS4's own pooled equity result, for why a portfolio's debasement hedge
must be a **real** asset (gold, per L15's design) rather than an inflation-linked nominal claim, which
in a genuine hard-reset scenario is exactly the instrument most likely to be redefined out from under
the holder.

### 5. France, post-WWII — inflation as the quiet default

**Setup and mechanism.** France emerged from occupation with a heavy domestic debt stock and, per the
Banque de France's own historical review, its rapid postwar debt reduction "is regularly analysed as
being the result of inflationary policies initiated by governments," with a further slice of debt
"transferred to commercial banks after 1945 through financial repression." **The specific instruments.**
In **February 1945** the state administratively converted outstanding **4% (1917/1918 vintage) and
4.5% (1932 vintage)** annuities down to **3%** — a direct, legislated coupon cut on existing
bondholders, executed by decree rather than negotiated restructuring. Tight exchange controls, with
the external value of the franc "fixed in an authoritarian way according to bilateral agreements,"
persisted until **1947**, insulating the repression from an immediate capital-flight response.
**Growth cover.** The subsequent *Trente Glorieuses* (1947–1973) delivered **~5%/year average
growth**, providing the denominator-side cover that made the inflation-driven numerator erosion look,
in retrospect, like a growth story rather than a default. **Lesson for L15.** France is this Part's
cleanest illustration of Reinhart-Rogoff's "inflation is default without saying so" framing applied
outside the Anglophone cases: an explicit coupon cut (not merely negative real rates) combined with
capital controls and then thirty years of strong growth as cover — the same recipe as the US and UK
cases, but with the additional, more visible step of a legislated interest-rate haircut that a purely
inflation-only mechanism does not require.

### 6a. Japan, 1944–1950 — hyperinflation, the 1946 capital levy, and the Dodge Line

**Setup.** Wartime and immediate-postwar Japan financed itself through what the literature calls an
"internal financing mechanism" that produced outright hyperinflation once occupation-era expansionary
policy met an overheating economy: wholesale prices rose **more than 700% from September 1945 to
August 1948**, and **13-fold from April 1946 to March 1949**. **Who paid — the 1946–47 capital levy.**
Rather than relying purely on inflation, occupation-era Japan layered an explicit, steeply progressive
property tax (*zaisan zei*) on top of it, falling on the richest 2–3 centiles of society and **raising
over 10% of national income in the single year it was collected** — the levy channel (B1 §5) used at
genuine scale, not as a footnote. **The stabilization — the Dodge Line.** American banker Joseph
Dodge's March 1949 program forced a **balanced budget**, a fixed exchange rate, and an abrupt end to
inflationary financing — at the cost of a sharp "stabilization crisis" recession, with US aid running
at **~25% of GDP** through the transition and a balanced-budget norm that held **until the mid-1960s**.
**Lesson for L15.** Japan 1944–50 combines all three of the harshest channels — hyperinflationary
default, an explicit wealth levy, and then externally-imposed fiscal austerity — in under six years,
the fastest full-menu resolution in this dossier. It is the direct ancestor, forty years removed, of
the second Japan case below, and the contrast between the two is itself the lesson: postwar Japan
resolved debt in five brutal years; today's Japan has spent thirty-five years explicitly avoiding
that path.

### 6b. Japan, 1990–present — the 250%+ ongoing experiment

**Setup and current state.** Japan's post-1971 (fiat-era) debt/GDP peak, per our own DS1 computation,
is **254% in 2020** with **0pp of sustained decline** — the JST panel's most extreme "still ascending"
case. Independent 2024–26 data confirm the order of magnitude: general **gross** government debt was
**234.9% of GDP at end-March 2025** (¥1,324 trillion); a narrower Cabinet Office measure shows the
ratio **peaking at 202.8% in FY2022** and easing only slowly toward **~187.6% by FY2026** on a
different accounting basis — the exact level is measure-dependent, but every series agrees Japan runs
the highest sovereign debt ratio of any major economy, with **no** JST-verified completed deleveraging
episode. **How it has been carried.** Roughly **95% of JGBs are held by domestic residents**
`[VERIFY: exact current share; widely cited but not independently re-confirmed against a primary BOJ
table this pass]`, and JGB yields sat **near zero for decades** even as the debt ratio climbed —
"an unusual pattern," as the literature notes, in which elevated debt did *not* pressure yields upward,
attributed to durable home-country bias among domestic holders rather than any external discipline.
**The 2022–24 stress test of the arrangement.** The Bank of Japan's Yield Curve Control regime
(targeting the 10-year JGB near 0%, with a widening reference band through 2022–23) was formally
**ended on 19 March 2024**, alongside the exit from **negative policy rates** — the first BOJ rate
hike since 2007 and the end of the world's only negative-rate regime. This was the live test of
whether three-plus decades of repression-adjacent policy could be unwound without a disorderly JGB
sell-off; the exit proceeded in an orderly fashion, but yen weakness and a subsequent foreign bid for
JGBs became live watch-items in its aftermath. **Investor outcomes, in real terms.** The Nikkei 225's
**price** index only regained its December 1989 nominal peak (38,915.87) on **22 February 2024** —
**34 years** later, and on a pure price basis the **1989–2024 real return was negative** (**−1.1%/year
in yen, −8.6%/year in USD**, price-only). Including reinvested dividends, the picture flips: **total**
returns since end-1989 reached **+54% cumulative in yen / +42% in USD** (versus **+3,331%** for the
S&P 500 total return over the same window) — the dividend-inclusive index first exceeded its 1989
level in **February 2021**, 32 years in. **Lesson for L15.** Japan is the live proof that a fiat-era
debt ratio can sit above 250% of GDP for over three decades with **no** completed deleveraging and no
crisis, provided the debt is overwhelmingly domestically held, the currency floats, and the central
bank is willing to suppress the entire yield curve — but the price for bond and equity holders alike
is a multi-decade real-return drought (near-zero JGB real yields; a negative-to-flat real equity
index for a generation) that DS4's pooled "fiscal-dominance-state" mean real equity return of
**+4.5%/year** does not fully capture at this extreme end of duration. It is the strongest evidence in
this Part that L15's design choice — sizing gold as insurance against the *state*, not against any
specific *resolution date* — is correct: nobody who modeled a 5–10 year Japan reversion in 1995 would
have been right yet in 2025.

### 7. Italy and Belgium, 1990s — EMU-convergence consolidations, one that held and one that didn't

**Setup.** At the 1992 Maastricht Treaty, both countries sat far above the 60%-of-GDP reference value:
**Belgium at close to 140% of GDP**, **Italy above 100% and rising to 117% in 1994** (our own JST panel
records Belgium's post-1971 peak at **135% in 1993**). Both signed up to a treaty text that, because a
hard 60% ceiling was unreachable "anytime soon" for either, substituted a softer standard: debt must be
"sufficiently diminishing" toward the reference value "at a satisfactory pace." **Belgium — the channel
that held.** A sustained, multi-year primary-surplus effort — exactly the Eichengreen-Panizza rare
category from B1 §2 — brought Belgian debt down; DS1 records a **48-percentage-point decline from the
135% (1993) peak, with no subsequent re-ascent above it through 2020** — one of only **four** JST-panel
countries whose post-1971 peak arc is classified **YES, completed**. By euro launch in 1999, both
countries' debt had fallen to **roughly 110% of GDP**. **Italy — the channel that didn't hold.** Italy's
own consolidation continued after 1999, reaching **102% by 2002** and **below 100% by 2007** — a real,
documented ~17–21-point decline from the 1994 peak — but our own JST panel shows Italy's **post-1971
peak is actually 2020, at 156% of GDP, with 0pp of net decline**: the GFC and then COVID pushed debt
back up past the 1994 level and kept climbing, erasing the entire 1990s consolidation and then some.
**Which terms did the work, and why the difference.** Both consolidations ran on the same primary-
surplus-plus-EMU-discipline mechanism; the difference is durability, not mechanism — Belgium's
political system sustained the surplus effort through multiple subsequent governments and never let
debt reascend past its old peak, while Italy's did not survive two subsequent shocks. **Lesson for L15.**
This is the single cleanest matched pair in the entire case record for the point B1 already makes in
the abstract: a genuine Eichengreen-Panizza-style large sustained primary surplus **can** delever a
100%+-of-GDP economy — but "sustained" turns out to mean sustained across multiple electoral cycles and
multiple external shocks, not merely sustained long enough to hit a treaty benchmark. The consolidation
that looks identical in 2002 can look completely different by 2020.

### 8. The r>g-era successes — Canada 1995–2008, Sweden post-1993, Ireland post-2013

These three are the case record's answer to the hardest version of the question: can a country
delever **without** the repression tailwind, under the liberalized-rate, r>g regime DS3 shows governed
1981–2007 (mean r−g **+1.1pp**, r<g in only **37%** of years)? **Canada.** Federal net debt fell from
**74.4% of GDP in 1995 to 28.9% in 2008** — a **~46-point** decline — via Finance Minister Paul Martin's
1995–96 "Program Review": departmental budget cuts ranging **5–65%**, a **~20% cut to the core federal
public service**, and federal transfer reforms, turning a **~3%-of-GDP deficit (1995)** into **annual
surpluses from 1997 through the 2007–08 crisis**. This is expenditure-led consolidation with essentially
no repression assist — real rates were positive through most of the period. `[Note: this specific
"federal net debt" series differs from the general-government gross-debt concept our own JST panel
uses; DS1 records Canada's post-1971 peak on that broader basis as 117% in 2020 with 0pp decline —
i.e., a real and substantial 1990s federal consolidation did not, on the broader all-government gross
measure, prevent a new post-1971 high two decades later. Both facts are true simultaneously; they are
different debt concepts, not a contradiction.]` **Sweden.** Our own JST panel is the cleanest record
here: post-1971 peak **73% of GDP in 1996**, declining **38 points with no re-ascent through 2020** —
one of DS1's four "YES, completed" cases. The mechanism: a 1990–93 banking crisis (GDP fell three
straight years, unemployment rose from **2% to 11%**, the budget deficit peaked at **13% of GDP in
1993**) forced both bank recapitalization (at ultimately near-zero net fiscal cost — Sweden ranks
favorably among 39 systemic banking crises 1970–2007 on this metric) and a hard expenditure
consolidation that turned the deficit into **surplus by 1998**. **Ireland.** Post-bailout debt peaked
near **120% of GDP around 2012–13** (DS1: **120% in 2013**) and fell **63 points with no re-ascent
through 2020** — another of the four completed arcs. The consolidation combined VAT and carbon/motor-
vehicle tax increases, a supplementary personal income surtax, civil-service cuts, and capital-spending
savings, against a strong post-bailout growth recovery: one detailed accounting shows debt falling
from **118.9% (2012) to 65.3% (2017)** — a **53.6-point** move in five years, among the fastest
completed descents in the entire panel. **The shared political precondition.** All three required a
visible, acute crisis (a currency-and-banking crisis for Sweden, a sovereign bailout for Ireland, a
~30-year deficit habit reaching a breaking point for Canada) to generate the political mandate for
multi-year expenditure cuts; none of the three ran their consolidation as a discretionary choice absent
that precondition. **Lesson for L15.** The non-repression path exists and is well-documented — but in
every instance it required either a genuinely small, open economy with unusual political cohesion
(Sweden, Ireland) or a crisis-driven mandate (all three) that a larger, less externally-constrained
economy has historically found much harder to generate absent an acute shock.

### 9. Greece, 2010–2018 — default inside a monetary union

**Setup.** Greek debt reached **~186% of GDP by 2018** (some series: 181%) at the formal end of its
adjustment programs, after **six consecutive years of GDP contraction (2009–2015) totaling a
cumulative ~25% loss** — over a quarter of the economy gone by constant prices between 2008 and 2016.
Unemployment rose from **9.6% (2009)** to a peak of **27.5% (September 2013)**, with youth unemployment
above **60%**. **The PSI — default, precisely quantified.** The March 2012 Private Sector Involvement
restructuring is this Part's cleanest, most exactly documented default event: **~97% of privately held
Greek bonds** took a **53.5% nominal haircut** on face value, with the reduced coupons on the new bonds
pushing the **net-present-value loss to an estimated 75–80%** for private creditors — the largest
sovereign restructuring loss of the post-WWII era, executed entirely without a currency devaluation
(Greece had no exchange-rate channel available inside the euro, which is precisely why the fiscal and
internal-devaluation adjustment had to be so much larger than in any of the currency-flexible cases
above). **Investor outcomes, in real terms.** Beyond the PSI haircut itself: the Athens Stock Exchange
lost **more than 85% of its collective value from 2007 to August 2015**; after adjusting for inflation,
Greece's stock-return index was reported in 2025 as **lower than its 1999 level, and lower even than
its 1961 level** — a genuine, multi-generational real equity loss with no parallel elsewhere in this
dossier. The **4.4%-of-GDP primary surplus posted in 2018** — itself brushing the very upper edge of
the Eichengreen-Panizza rarity band from B1 §2 — illustrates how much fiscal effort a monetary-union
member without a repression or currency channel had to extract to even begin stabilizing the ratio.
**Lesson for L15.** Greece is the modern proof-case for what happens when a sovereign loses the
inflation/repression/devaluation channels simultaneously (currency union) and cannot generate a
sufficient primary surplus fast enough (political limits, per B1 §2): the residual adjustment lands on
bondholders (default, precisely as PSI shows) and on real output and equity values (a quarter-century,
not merely a cyclical, loss) rather than on savers-via-inflation. For an Indian book that is never
going to be inside a currency union, the direct transferability is limited — but Greece is the sharpest
available evidence for how much *worse* the outcome gets when the repression/currency channels are
foreclosed and only default and internal devaluation remain.

### 10. India — the repression apparatus, dormant but standing

**Pre-1991: financial repression in full.** On the eve of the 1991 crisis, India's Statutory Liquidity
Ratio stood at **38.5%** and the Cash Reserve Ratio at **15%** — together forcing banks to hold roughly
**half their deposit base** in government paper or with the RBI, at administered, below-market rates.
This was the repression apparatus at genuine, textbook scale: a captive domestic banking system
functioning as the fiscal system's primary financing arm, the exact mechanism Reinhart-Sbrancia
document for the 1945–80 advanced-economy era, running in India in essentially its purest form as late
as 1990–91. **The 1991 BoP crisis and the gold pledge.** Foreign reserves fell from **$1.2 billion in
January 1991 to about half that by June**, financing barely **three weeks of imports**; against a
Gulf-War oil shock and a collapse in remittances, the RBI **airlifted 47 tonnes of gold to the Bank of
England and 20 tonnes to the Union Bank of Switzerland** (67 tonnes total) as collateral for a
**$600 million** bridge loan, alongside a separate **$2.2 billion emergency IMF facility** — the
collateralized-pledge, not sale, structure meant the gold was recoverable, and the episode became the
proximate trigger for the 1991 liberalization program. **Post-2003: the growth-led decline.** The 2003
FRBM Act's deficit targets coincided with India's strongest sustained growth run: general-government
debt/GDP, at roughly **84–86% in 2003**, fell to approximately **75% by 2007** and toward a **~66–67%
trough around 2010–11** `[VERIFY: exact trough year and precise level — sources converge on
"mid-60s%, ~2010–12" but do not agree to the single point]` — a genuine growth-led descent of
15–20 points achieved with no repression assist and with India's own r−g arithmetic favorable through
most of the run. **The FRBM arc and its suspensions.** The Act was formally **suspended from FY2008–09
through FY2012–13** to fund the GFC-era stimulus (fiscal deficit widened to **6.2% of GDP** against a
budgeted 2.7%); a formal **escape clause** was written into the Act only via the **2018 Finance Act**
(N.K. Singh Committee recommendation), permitting up to a **0.5pp-of-GDP** deviation for national
security, calamity, or output-collapse triggers — first invoked in the **2020 COVID budget**.
**The COVID spike and today's level.** Combined general-government debt/GDP **peaked at
approximately 89% in FY2020–21** (IMF: "from 74% to almost 90%" across the pandemic year) before
easing with nominal-GDP recovery to roughly **82% in 2024** and a projected **~80% for 2025**.
**Today's ownership structure.** As of December 2025, commercial banks hold **34.31%** of outstanding
central-government securities, insurance companies **25.89%**, the RBI **14.52%**, and FPIs only
**2.96%** — domestic institutions (banks, insurers, RBI) together own **nearly three-quarters** of the
market, a captive-adjacent structure that, while today's SLR sits at only **18%** (unchanged through
the RBI's June 2025 policy review) versus the pre-1991 38.5%, still concentrates the debt stock inside
regulated, domestically-anchored balance sheets rather than distributing it to price-sensitive foreign
holders — the repression apparatus dormant, in the Contract's own phrase, but standing. **What the
RBI's own gold accumulation signals.** The RBI has bought gold "regularly from the market since
December 2017," lifting reserves from **557.8 tonnes (2015) to roughly 880 tonnes by Q1 2026** —
including **57.5 tonnes added in FY2024–25 alone**, its second-largest annual purchase in seven years
— and, in a detail with genuine narrative weight for this Part, **repatriated over 100 tonnes of gold
from the Bank of England back to its own vaults in 2024**, the **first such repatriation since 1991**
— the same Bank of England vault the 1991 pledge had used, now being emptied in the opposite direction.
**Lesson for L15.** India is the only case in this Part where the desk can watch the entire menu —
textbook repression, a genuine BoP/gold-collateral crisis, a growth-led descent, fiscal-rule
suspensions under stress, and a live, currently-accelerating central-bank gold-accumulation program —
inside one 35-year, domestically-documented arc, on the same currency and monetary order the book
actually trades. It is also the strongest domestic argument, independent of DS2/DS4's pooled global
priors, for why gold sits in the L15 design at all: the institution most directly responsible for
managing India's own debt stock is *itself*, right now, accumulating the asset the seat treats as the
debasement hedge.

### Six pooled conclusions, ranked by evidence strength, mapped to L15

1. **(Strongest — DS1's own 18-country, 150-year panel, corroborated in every pre-1971 case above.)**
   Successful, *lasting* fiat-era (post-1971) deleveraging is genuinely rare — 4 of 18 JST-panel
   countries — while the pre-1971 record (UK, US, France, Germany, Japan) is full of dramatic, largely
   repression-driven descents. The two facts are not in tension: they describe *different monetary
   regimes*. → **Design implication:** L15's Tier-C, reduce-only, state-classification-only mandate is
   the correct response to this exact asymmetry — the historical base rate for "this resolves the way
   the textbook 1940s–70s cases resolved" is lower under the current (post-1971, open-capital-account)
   order than the popular narrative assumes, which is precisely why the seat sizes insurance rather
   than timing a resolution.
2. **(Strong — Reinhart-Sbrancia's 12-country magnitude study, DS2's independent country-year
   replication, and the US/UK/Japan case decompositions above all agree on order of magnitude.)**
   Where a lasting descent *did* happen without default, repression (negative real rates operating on a
   captive or currency-flexible domestic debt stock) did more of the work than growth alone in every
   well-decomposed case (US, UK, France) — Acalin-Ball's reassessment is the sharpest single data point:
   only ~40–45% of the US's 1946–74 fall survives a counterfactual with undistorted rates. →
   **Design implication:** the gold-floor attribution band (0.40–0.50 of the gold sleeve's rationale)
   and the conditional +1–2pp lift when the fiscal-dominance state is ON are sized against a channel
   (repression) that is not a tail scenario in the historical record — it is the modal resolution
   mechanism whenever a resolution has actually occurred.
3. **(Strong — Eichengreen-Panizza's 54-country, 235-period study, directly corroborated by the
   Italy/Belgium matched pair and the Canada/Sweden/Ireland trio.)** Large, sustained primary surpluses
   (≥3% of GDP for ≥10 years) are achievable but require either an external convergence anchor (EMU,
   in Italy/Belgium's case) or an acute, mandate-generating crisis (Sweden, Ireland, and Canada's own
   near-crisis fiscal trajectory) — and even then durability is not guaranteed, as Italy's reversal
   shows. → **Design implication:** none of the ten cases supports treating "India will run a large
   sustained primary surplus" as a base-case input to any horizon of this design; the seat should never
   assume the surplus channel activates absent an equivalent acute domestic trigger, which is itself
   unforecastable and therefore correctly excluded from Stage 1.
4. **(Strong on direction, Tier C on India-specific magnitude — DS4's pooled cross-country result is
   Tier A/B; the India-specific instantiation is asserted by design, not yet measured.)** DS4 shows
   equities earn positive but roughly halved real returns in the fiscal-dominance state (+4.5%/yr vs
   +9.5%/yr normal) — this Part adds that the *tail* of that distribution (Japan's 35-year experiment,
   Greece's PSI-plus-internal-devaluation) can be far worse than the pooled mean, particularly when
   bond and property claims (not just equities) are counted, or when the repression/currency channels
   are foreclosed entirely (Greece). → **Design implication:** this is the direct justification for
   never treating DS4's pooled mean as a floor — the seat's tail budget (0.3–0.6%/yr of NAV) exists
   precisely because the mean understates the worst realized outcomes this Part documents (Japan,
   Greece), and the design must not fatigue even if the state persists for a decade, exactly as Part H
   already specifies.
5. **(Moderate — a single, well-documented but narrow domestic case: India's own 1991–2025 arc.)**
   India has run the entire five-channel menu (repression, BoP/gold-collateral crisis, growth-led
   decline, fiscal-rule suspension under stress) within living memory, on its own currency, and is
   *currently* running a live instance of the reserve-diversification/gold-accumulation leg (RBI
   holdings 557.8t→~880t since 2015, accelerating since 2017, including a symbolically pointed 2024
   repatriation from the same London vault used in the 1991 pledge). → **Design implication:** this is
   the strongest domestically-sourced evidence available for the L15 seat's gold-floor rationale — not
   a cross-country prior imported from the JST panel, but the balance sheet of the institution that
   actually manages India's own debt, observed making the same allocation choice the seat encodes.
   **What would falsify the seat:** a multi-year, sustained *reduction* in RBI gold holdings
   accompanied by a stable or improving domestic r−g regime (i.e., the repression gauge from Part E
   turning and staying OFF, with no CB gold-accumulation leg) — that combination, sustained, is the
   observable pattern this design would need to see before the gold-floor attribution band should be
   revisited downward.
6. **(Moderate — a definitional finding, not a market finding, but one this Part surfaces cleanly via
   the Canada/Italy contrasts.)** The choice of debt series (federal vs general government; net vs
   gross; held-by-public vs total) materially changes which decade reads as "the peak" and whether a
   consolidation reads as "completed" — Canada's celebrated 1995–2008 federal turnaround coexists,
   without contradiction, with a JST-panel finding that Canada's broader general-government gross-debt
   post-1971 peak is actually 2020. → **Design implication:** any India debt/GDP input the ladder
   consumes (per Part E's STEP 1) must be pinned to one explicit, stated series definition and
   vintage-tracked exactly as Part D's D07/DB3 failure-mode already specifies — a "peak" or "descent"
   claim is meaningless without naming which debt concept it refers to, and this Part shows how easily
   two contradictory-sounding claims about the *same country* can both be true under different
   definitions.
