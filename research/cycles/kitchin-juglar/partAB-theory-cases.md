# PART A + B + C — Kitchin strongest-form-first, the bullwhip mechanism, Juglar's priority claim,
# Schumpeter's schema and its collapse, the fold, and the verdict pair

This chapter does two things a single-cycle monograph does not have to do. It argues Kitchin's
1923 finding in its strongest form — a careful, six-series, two-country empirical result, not a
folk number — and then shows, using the desk's own pre-registered trial, exactly where a genuine
finding stopped being a clock and started being mistaken for one; and it argues Juglar's 1862
priority claim in its strongest form — the first person to date crises from banking data as a
recurring phenomenon rather than a list of accidents — and then shows that the modern content of
that claim already lives, fully seated and better evidenced, inside this program's credit and
capex seats. Read together, the two entries make the same point from opposite directions: 2.4 is
a real mechanism wearing a fake clock; 2.5 is a real object wearing an obsolete label. The design
response differs accordingly — CONTEXT for the mechanism, FOLD for the object — but the discipline
behind both verdicts is identical: never let a compelling 19th-century name buy a budget line that
the 21st-century evidence has not itself earned.

## A. Kitchin, strongest form first (Atlas entry 2.4)

### A.1 What Kitchin actually measured

**Joseph Kitchin (1923), "Cycles and Trends in Economic Factors,"** *The Review of Economics and
Statistics* 5(1): 10–16, Harvard University Press [Verified via search]. The paper is narrower and
more careful than its folk-memory version suggests. Kitchin drew on **six monthly time series
spanning 1890–1922 for the United States and the United Kingdom**: **bank clearings**, **wholesale
commodity prices**, and **short-term (commercial-paper) interest rates**, for each country
[Verified]. Working with this specific variable class — monetary and credit-market data, not a
physical stocks-on-hand series — Kitchin found that the price level and short-term interest rates
in both countries tended to move together through cycles averaging roughly **40 months (3.3
years)**, while noting explicitly that **individual cycles varied dramatically** around that
average [Verified]. Two features of the original paper are easy to lose in the label's later
career. First, Kitchin's own object was **never inventory** in the literal sense — no stock-on-hand
series appears anywhere in his six input series; the "inventory cycle" reading is a later
interpretation layered onto a monetary/credit-market finding, not Kitchin's own claim. Second,
Kitchin himself proposed a **nesting idea** — minor (his ~40-month) cycles bundling two or three to
a "major" cycle — a full sixteen years before Schumpeter's 1939 three-cycle schema formalized
exactly that nesting logic at three named frequencies (Part B.2 below); the seed of the schema's
later collapse is present in the very first paper that gave the shortest layer its name.

**Why the inventory interpretation came later, and why it is not re-derived here.** The formal
mechanism that gave Kitchin's short-frequency finding its "inventory cycle" content is **Metzler,
Lloyd A. (1941), "The Nature and Stability of Inventory Cycles,"** *Review of Economic Statistics*
23: 113–129 — already fully treated at `docs/cycles/18-business-cycle.md` §A.2(ii), which this
chapter cross-references rather than repeats. In one sentence for context: Metzler modeled firms
holding inventory toward a desired stock-to-expected-sales ratio, with expected sales themselves
adaptively updated from realized sales, producing a genuine feedback loop (rather than Samuelson's
fixed-coefficient multiplier-accelerator) with six distinct stability regimes — from strictly
damped, through sustained oscillation, to explosive — depending on how aggressively firms restock
relative to how fast they revise expectations. Metzler's model is the reason the business-cycle
band's shortest frequency carries Kitchin's name at all: it supplied a mechanism (adaptive
inventory-to-sales targeting) that could, in principle, generate a cycle at roughly Kitchin's own
empirical frequency, decades after Kitchin's own paper had already reported the frequency without
a mechanism attached. That gap — an empirical period discovered first, a candidate mechanism
supplied second, from an entirely different variable class (stock-adjustment behavior, not bank
clearings) — is itself the first sign that "Kitchin cycle" was always doing double duty as a label
for two related but distinct claims, exactly the same fault line that runs through the
Kondratieff/Perez pair (`docs/cycles/15-long-waves.md` §A.1) and, as Part B shows, through the
Juglar/Schumpeter pair as well.

### A.2 The bullwhip effect — the living mechanism

If Metzler supplied the inventory-cycle *mechanism* Kitchin's finding lacked, the modern
operations-research literature supplied its *microfoundation* — and, unlike Metzler's aggregate
stability analysis, gave it a name and an experimental demonstration that remains, eighty years
on, the most replicated result in supply-chain economics.

**Forrester, Jay W. (1961), *Industrial Dynamics*** (MIT Press) is the founding document, coining
what the field now calls **demand amplification** — later popularized as the **bullwhip effect** —
from the system-dynamics discipline Forrester built at MIT Sloan [Verified via search]. Forrester
and his students illustrated the phenomenon concretely with the **MIT Beer (Distribution) Game**,
first run around 1960 [Verified]: a four-echelon serial supply chain — retailer, wholesaler,
distributor, factory — in which each player's only decision each round is how much to order from
the echelon above, with a fixed order-and-shipping lag between echelons. The game's famous,
endlessly-reproduced result is that a small, temporary uptick in end-customer demand produces
progressively **larger, more volatile order swings at each successive echelon upstream**, with no
single player behaving irrationally, no communication failure beyond the ordinary lag structure,
and — critically for this desk's purposes — **no shared clock**: the amplitude and duration of the
resulting oscillation depend entirely on the size of the initiating demand shock and the length of
the order-and-ship lag, not on any fixed periodicity of the supply chain itself.

**Lee, Hau L.; Padmanabhan, V. & Whang, Seungjin (1997), "Information Distortion in a Supply
Chain: The Bullwhip Effect,"** *Management Science* 43(4): 546–558 [Verified via search] gave
Forrester's demonstration its modern formal treatment, identifying **four independent causes**
that together generate the effect even under fully rational, locally-optimal behavior at every
stage: **demand-signal processing** (extrapolating a short-lived uptick as if it were a trend
shift, then over-ordering against the extrapolation); the **rationing game** (when a supplier
imposes allocation rules during a shortage, downstream buyers rationally over-order to guarantee
their true needs, then cancel once supply normalizes — a phantom-demand spike with no real demand
behind it); **order batching** (periodic or economic-order-quantity ordering concentrates what
would otherwise be smooth demand into lumpy, correlated order pulses); and **price variations**
(forward-buying ahead of a known promotion or price change desynchronizes orders from underlying
consumption). Lee-Padmanabhan-Whang's central empirical claim — **the variance of orders exceeds
the variance of sales, and that distortion grows moving upstream** — is precisely the formal,
falsifiable statement of what Forrester's beer game dramatized and, on this reading, precisely the
mechanism Kitchin's 1890–1922 bank-clearings-and-commodity-price data was implicitly registering
two generations before either Forrester or Lee-Padmanabhan-Whang existed to name it: a real,
recurring order-flow oscillation that requires nothing more exotic than ordinary local inventory
policy and information lag, no coordination failure and no crisis, to reproduce itself whenever a
supply chain absorbs a sufficiently large demand shock.

**Verified modern instances — the mechanism is real and alive, on a scale no periodogram would
mistake for coincidence.** Three independent, contemporaneous episodes from 2021–24 show the exact
Lee-Padmanabhan-Whang taxonomy operating at global scale, not merely in a laboratory game:

- **The 2021–22 semiconductor shortage and its 2023 reversal.** The auto-chip shortage cost the
  global light-vehicle industry an estimated **more than 9.5 million units of lost production in
  2021 alone**, with Q3 2021 the single worst quarter at roughly **3.5 million units lost**, and a
  further **~3 million units** lost in 2022 [S&P Global Mobility, verified via search]. OEMs and
  Tier-1 suppliers responded with exactly the rationing-game and demand-signal-processing behavior
  the 1997 paper predicts — double- and triple-ordering chips to guarantee allocation under a
  supplier-imposed shortage, extrapolating pandemic-era demand strength as durable. By mid-2023
  "the semiconductor shortage is — mostly — over for the auto industry" [S&P Global Mobility
  headline, verified], and the bullwhip's second half arrived on schedule: semiconductor buyers,
  EMS providers and Tier-1s faced an **inventory correction** as the buffer stock built to
  safeguard 2021–22 production turned into surplus once true end demand reasserted itself, exactly
  as the semiconductor market itself entered "a major correction cycle" alongside falling PC,
  smartphone and consumer-electronics demand [Roland Berger / S&P Global Mobility, verified].
- **Target and Walmart's 2022 inventory glut.** Target's operating income fell **43% year-over-year
  in Q1 FY22** on excess inventory, with the retailer canceling orders and taking steep markdowns
  through the year; CEO Brian Cornell attributed the reversal directly to shoppers "becoming more
  focused on and responsive to promotions" after a period of strength [CNBC, verified]. Walmart
  cut its FY22 profit outlook on the same dynamic, its U.S. CEO acknowledging the company would
  "like to just wish away" a glut it expected would take "a couple of quarters" to clear [CNBC,
  verified]; Gap and Kohl's reported the identical pattern the same year [Modern Retail / Supply
  Chain Dive, verified]. This is textbook demand-signal processing: 2020–21 pandemic-stimulus-
  driven goods spending was read by major retailers as a durable shift, ordered against
  accordingly, and the resulting stock arrived just as consumer spending rotated back toward
  services — the same mechanism the beer game dramatizes with four players and a six-week horizon,
  now run at the scale of the largest retailers on earth.
- **The 2022–24 freight recession [VERIFY: precise inflection dates and normalization timing are
  secondary-sourced across trade press, not independently re-pulled from a primary index this
  session].** U.S. trucking shipment volumes began a steep decline in **April 2022** [verified via
  search], the start of what trade press describes as one of the longest, most disruptive
  downturns in modern trucking history — the capacity side of the same 2021–22 order-batching and
  demand-extrapolation dynamic: **for-hire carrier counts rose from roughly 241,000 (June 2020) to
  over 475,000 (July 2023)**, a **96% increase** chasing the pandemic goods boom [verified], only
  to collide with the 2022–23 retail destocking wave described above. Dry-van linehaul spot rates
  fell to roughly **$1.75/mile by end-2023**, with **van load-to-truck ratios down 44%
  year-over-year and reefer ratios down 54% year-over-year in December 2023** [verified]; retail
  inventory levels are reported to have normalized by roughly **Q3 2023** [Supply Chain Dive /
  trade-press reporting, verified directionally; exact normalization date not independently
  re-pulled — VERIFY]. Roughly **8,000 freight brokers and eleven times as many carriers** went
  bankrupt in 2023 as excess capacity met collapsing demand [verified] — the physical-capacity
  mirror of Target and Walmart's physical-inventory glut, the same shock propagating through the
  trucking sector that transports the goods those retailers over-ordered.

The framing this record earns is exactly the one the atlas states and this desk's own KJ1 trial
(§A.4 below) now confirms empirically: **the bullwhip mechanism is real and alive — it fires
whenever a large enough demand or supply shock synchronizes local reordering decisions across a
chain — but it does not run on Kitchin's or anyone else's fixed clock.** The 2021–24 episode took
roughly three years to complete its full cycle (shortage → panic-ordering → glut → destocking →
normalization); the beer game itself can complete a comparable arc in six rounds; nothing in either
case argues for a metronomic ~40-month period, only for a mechanism whose duration tracks the size
and persistence of whatever shock set it off.

### A.3 Why it weakened as a macro cycle

Three structural forces, verified independently of the bullwhip's own vitality, explain why an
inventory oscillation this real nonetheless stopped generating a *macro*-scale clock the way it may
plausibly have done in Kitchin's own 1890–1922 window.

**JIT/lean, diffused as the global manufacturing operating system.** Toyota's Just-in-Time system —
developed by Taiichi Ohno and Eiji Toyoda between roughly 1948 and 1975, with Ohno crediting a 1956
visit to an American supermarket for the "pull" replenishment idea later formalized as kanban
[Verified via search] — was not a single-firm curiosity. Its formal Western codification arrived
with the **MIT International Motor Vehicle Program's five-year study**, published as *The Machine
That Changed the World* (1990), which coined the term **"lean production"** after finding Toyota's
system "so much more effective and efficient than traditional mass production that it represented a
completely new paradigm" [Verified via search]. From the 1980s through the 2000s, lean/JIT
diffused as the default manufacturing and retail operating philosophy across most of the developed
world — mechanically damping the amplitude of precisely the stock-adjustment oscillation Metzler's
model and Kitchin's own data describe, by design rather than by accident.

**The evidence is in the aggregate inventory-to-sales ratio itself.** The U.S. aggregate
manufacturing-and-trade inventory-to-sales (I/S) ratio **trended upward at roughly 0.76%/year
before 1985 Q3 and downward at roughly 1.29%/year since**, with the downtrend beginning in
manufacturing around 1983, wholesalers around 1985, and retailers around 1990 [trend-break
literature, ScienceDirect, verified via search] — a dating that lines up closely with JIT/lean's
own diffusion window. In levels: the manufacturing I/S ratio ran roughly **1.8 through the 1950s,
1960s and 1970s**, falling to roughly **1.2 by the mid-2000s** [verified]; the **total** business
I/S ratio averaged **1.70 in 1992**, fell to **1.34 by 2012**, and reached a **record low of 1.23
in July 2020** [verified], attributed directly to "the combination of just-in-time manufacturing,
better supply chains, and better management of the supply chains" [verified]. Most recent prints —
**roughly 1.32–1.38 across 2025 and early 2026** on Census Bureau/FRED data [verified via search;
exact monthly print at time of writing subject to the usual one-to-two-month reporting lag —
VERIFY current figure at next refresh] — sit comfortably inside this post-2010 range, not durably
elevated above it (see the 2021–24 counter-lesson below). A macro cycle whose amplitude has been
mechanically compressed by forty years of deliberate operating-system change is not the same object
Kitchin measured in a pre-JIT, pre-lean industrial economy.

**Services-share growth compounds the same compression mechanically.** Both the U.S. and India have
shifted GDP composition toward services — which by construction carry near-zero physical inventory
— over the same window the I/S ratio has been falling. This shrinks the base over which any
inventory oscillation can register at the aggregate, economy-wide level, even in sectors (autos,
consumer electronics, apparel) where the bullwhip mechanism itself, per §A.2, remains fully intact.

**The honest, two-sided counter-lesson of 2021–24: did the cycle come back?** JIT's own fragility
under a genuinely extreme shock became a public argument for reversal during the pandemic. A **2022
SAP survey of 400 decision-makers found roughly two-thirds shifting explicitly from "just-in-time"
to "just-in-case" inventory strategy**, citing overly lean pre-pandemic stocks as the source of
supply-chain fragility [verified via search]. Taken alone, this would argue the cycle's amplitude
is structurally re-widening — a genuine reversal of forty years of JIT compression. But the honest,
symmetric half of the same record cuts the other way: **while roughly 60% of firms increased
inventory buffers by 15–40% at the pandemic's peak, only about 20% maintained those elevated levels
past 2022, and by 2023 aggregate stocks had reverted roughly halfway back to pre-pandemic baselines**
[CEPR VoxEU column, "From just-in-time to just-in-case," verified via search]; separately, an
estimated **97% of companies worldwide were still reconfiguring supply chains in some form by the
end of 2023**, but reconfiguring toward diversification and resilience is not the same claim as
durably carrying more stock [verified]. Reading the aggregate I/S ratio's own 2024–26 prints
(§ above, back in the 1.3–1.4 post-2010 range, not durably elevated) against this survey evidence,
the honest verdict is two-sided and stated as such: **2021–24 is one dramatic, real bullwhip
episode — a state, fully consistent with a live mechanism firing on an unusually large shock — not
evidence that a periodic macro clock has been restored.** "Just-in-case" rhetoric loomed larger in
survey answers about intent than it has, so far, in the aggregate data about behavior; a design
that treated the JIC narrative as proof of a returning Kitchin clock would be repeating exactly the
error this atlas exists to prevent — mistaking a live, real, one-off amplitude event for the
resumption of a periodicity Kitchin's own variable class never actually supported in the first
place, and which, per §A.4 below, this desk has now tested directly and found absent across 90
combined years of monthly data.

### A.4 The desk's own numbers, and India measurability, honestly

**KJ1 (`kitchin-RESULTS.md`), pre-registered, closes the question empirically.** Testing Kitchin's
own claimed ~40-month band directly on monthly commodity prices — the variable class closest to
Kitchin's own 1890–1922 series, extended across two independent cells (the gold floating era,
1968–2026, and the IMF all-commodity index, 1980–2017) — the desk's own trial finds **zero of 37
pooled trough-to-trough spacings landing inside the pre-registered [30,50]-month window**, on both
cells independently: gold's 28 spacings run 18–27 months (median 21m, 0% in-window); the IMF
index's 9 spacings run 20–29 months (median 23m, 0% in-window). The apparently "faster" ~21-month
median is explicitly flagged in the results file as a **resolution-floor artifact** rather than a
discovered faster clock — 8 of gold's 28 spacings pin exactly at the registered 18-month minimum
gap, meaning the machinery is finding *no* distinguished periodicity above its own floor, not a new
one at the floor. Combined with the sweep this closes — 40 months (KJ1), the 3–7-year growth-cycle
band (BC1, a real band, median 6y, not a point), 18 years (the property-cycle folk clock, RE1),
15–21 years (the commodity-supercycle count, CS1), and 45–60 years (Kondratieff, KW1) — this desk
has now pre-registered and tested the *entire* claimed-cycle frequency spectrum this program
touches, from 40 months to six decades, and found **zero surviving fixed clocks**, while finding
**real, persistent bands** at several of those same resolutions. Kitchin's own 40-month claim joins
Kondratieff's 45–60-year claim and the folk 18-year property clock on the same list, for the
identical reason: a genuine, real phenomenon (generational inflation waves; credit-property
co-movement; the bullwhip mechanism itself) was mistaken, at the moment of discovery, for a fixed
period it never actually was.

**India measurability, stated plainly rather than gestured at.** No monthly, economy-wide India
inventory aggregate exists — nothing analogous to the U.S. Census Bureau's Manufacturing and Trade
Inventories and Sales (MTIS) release, which is the primary source for every I/S-ratio figure cited
in §A.3. Four free proxies exist, each usable, none clearing a Tier-B bar on its own:

1. **IIP vs. sales-growth wedges.** MOSPI's monthly Index of Industrial Production (a production
   series) read against a sales-growth proxy (GST collections, FMCG volume data, or similar) can, in
   principle, imply an inventory build or drawdown as the residual wedge between production and
   sales growth — but MOSPI publishes no inventory line itself, so the wedge is inferred from two
   separately noisy, separately revised series, each with its own base-year splice history
   (cross-ref `docs/cycles/18-business-cycle.md` §G.3's IIP/GDP-GVA-gap discussion — not
   re-derived here). A residual-of-two-residuals construction is a genuinely weak instrument for a
   state the atlas already flags "weakly measurable."
2. **Corporate inventory-days from annual/quarterly filings.** Firm-level days-inventory-outstanding
   (DIO), aggregated sector by sector (autos, FMCG, capital goods) from listed-company reports, is
   computable — but it is the identical free-fundamentals domain Contract §7 Known Prior #7 already
   flags as biased upward 150–450bps/yr by restatement-with-no-knowledge-date and survivorship; any
   DIO-based inventory read inherits that same look-ahead problem and would need the point-in-time
   discipline the fundamentals book already owes itself before it could be trusted as more than a
   slow, lagged, backward-looking confirmation.
3. **PMI Stocks-of-Purchases sub-index.** The S&P Global/HSBC-sponsored India Manufacturing PMI is a
   weighted composite of five sub-indices — **New Orders (30%), Output (25%), Employment (20%),
   Suppliers' Delivery Times (15%) and Stocks of Purchases (10%)** [Verified via search] — and the
   Stocks-of-Purchases component is exactly an inventory-cycle read. But, consistent with the
   identical free/paid split `docs/cycles/18-business-cycle.md` §A.3(iii) already documents for
   PMI's headline number, **only the composite PMI figure and a qualitative press-release summary
   are free monthly**; the full sub-index time series, including Stocks of Purchases, is a paid
   subscription product — the component this entry most wants is precisely the one the free-data
   rule (Contract §7) rules out at full resolution.
4. **Auto channel inventories via FADA.** The Federation of Automobile Dealers Associations publishes
   monthly retail data (in collaboration with the Ministry of Road Transport & Highways/RTO
   registrations) that **does include inventory-days-of-stock by vehicle segment** — the single
   best genuinely inventory-native free India series this pass located. Recent example: passenger-
   vehicle channel stock, a sustained FY25 concern above 50 days, corrected to roughly **28 days by
   March 2026** on FADA's own reporting, with FADA continuing to advocate a **21-day stock norm**
   for the segment [Verified via search]. The caveats are real: single-sector (autos only, not
   economy-wide), industry-body self-reported rather than an independent statistical agency, and
   its cross-release definitional consistency would need direct checking before any design treated
   it as more than a sector-specific conditioner.

**Conclusion, matching the atlas exactly.** None of these four proxies — a noisy residual, a
look-ahead-biased filing aggregate, a paid sub-index, and a single-sector industry-body series —
clears a Tier-B bar as an economy-wide India inventory cycle. **CONTEXT, unbudgeted**, precisely as
Atlas row 2.4 already states. The bullwhip mechanism's real, alive, global manifestation (§A.2) is
read through **L9 (`global_financial_cycle`, `config/ladder.yaml`)** — the dollar/VIX/US-rate global
risk state that already pools PMI-adjacent global-cycle information — never seated as a separate
India state. A dedicated India inventory-wedge design is, at most, a future runsheet-gated research
candidate, exactly as `partDH-verdict-routing.md` already registers it: **KJ-D1** (an IIP-growth-
minus-sales-growth wedge, acceptance bar = sign-consistent lead on IIP-capital-goods drawdowns at a
pre-registered horizon, Tier-C reduce-only ceiling, registered with an explicit expectation that it
may fail) and **KJ-D2** (the PMI stocks-subindex wave, read through L9's own input-family tests,
never standalone) — both pre-registered, neither yet run, neither auto-promotable, consistent with
the atlas's own H53–H65 candidate discipline.

## B. Juglar, the fold (Atlas entry 2.5)

### B.1 Clément Juglar (1862) — the first empirical cycle dating, and why he earns the credit

**Juglar, Clément (1862), *Des Crises Commerciales et de leur Retour Périodique en France, en
Angleterre et aux États-Unis*** (On Commercial Crises and Their Periodic Return in France, England
and the United States) [Verified via search]. The book began as a prize-winning entry to an **1861
competition set by the Académie des Sciences Morales et Politiques**, asking researchers to
investigate the causes and effects of the commercial crises that had recurred across 19th-century
Europe and North America — Juglar's submission won [Verified]. What he actually did was examine
**bank balance sheets, credit aggregates and discount rates**, and use them to date recurring
commercial crises across France, England and the United States through the century — not a price
index in Kitchin's later style, but the banking system's own books.

**The historiographic case for Juglar's priority, stated precisely.** Juglar's central,
consequential claim was that **credit — not merely the quantity of money in circulation — played
the essential destabilizing role** in generating crises [Verified via search], and that crises are
the recurring, *endogenous* terminal phase of a prosperity–crisis–liquidation rhythm, not a
sequence of unrelated accidents each requiring its own separate story (a bad harvest here, a single
bank's fraud there, a war's finance somewhere else). This is not a minor conceptual advance. It
means Juglar dated multiple, co-moving national crisis chronologies — France, England, the United
States — from primary banking data **nearly a century before Burns and Mitchell (1946) formalized
the comovement-across-many-series dating methodology** this program's own business-cycle entry
still works from (`docs/cycles/18-business-cycle.md` §A.1(i) — cross-referenced, not re-derived),
and it anticipates, almost exactly, the structural claim this desk's own **L10 credit-cycle seat**
runs on today: Schularick-Taylor's (2012) and Baron-Xiong's (2017) finding that credit growth
predicts crisis probability — Atlas row 2.1's own citation, `docs/cycles/01-credit-cycle.md`,
ladder.yaml `L10_credit_block` — is, in its structural shape, the *same claim* Juglar made in 1862
with 19th-century bank-balance-sheet data and no econometrics whatsoever: a crisis is not a random
external shock arriving to interrupt an otherwise stable economy, it is what the credit expansion
phase itself produces once it has run long enough. Juglar continued refining the ten-year
prosperity-crisis-depression rhythm for the rest of his working life, publishing a substantially
revised second edition in **1889** [Verified via search]. The credit for treating financial crises
as endogenous rather than exogenous — a framing this desk's entire L10 seat still depends on —
belongs to Juglar first, on the documented historical record, regardless of what later commentators
chose to name the periodicity he only loosely specified.

### B.2 Schumpeter's three-cycle schema — its strongest form, then its collapse

**Schumpeter, Joseph A. (1939), *Business Cycles: A Theoretical, Historical, and Statistical
Analysis of the Capitalist Process*** presents the schema in its most ambitious and, on first
reading, most elegant form: **long waves of roughly fifty years (Kondratieffs) contain an integral
number of intermediate waves of roughly nine to ten years (Juglars), and each Juglar contains an
integral number of short waves of roughly forty months (Kitchins)** [Verified via search] — a
hierarchically nested clock system, built on Schumpeter's own classification of innovations by
their economic "thrust," in which every layer's phase implies something about every other layer's
phase. Steelmanned fully: if this were true, it would be close to the single most useful timing
instrument a cycle-based portfolio model could possess — three named, mechanically related
periodicities, each with its own class of driving innovation, nested by integer ratios rather than
merely correlated. That is the strongest form of the claim, and it deserves to be stated at full
strength before it is tested, exactly as this program's own house style requires for every
rejection or fold entry (`docs/cycles/15-long-waves.md` §A; `docs/cycles/10-war-cycles.md`).

**The collapse, using nothing but the desk's own already-completed trials, none re-derived here.**

- **KW1/KW2 (`research/cycles/longwaves/longwaves-RESULTS.md`, cross-ref `docs/cycles/
  15-long-waves.md` Part C) remove the top layer of the pyramid entirely.** KW1 finds **zero of
  five** trough-to-trough spacings landing inside the claimed [45,60]-year Kondratieff band on 165
  years of real commodity prices, at a resolution chosen specifically to give the band room to
  appear. KW2, testing Kondratieff's own object — synchronized generational inflation waves across
  the UK, USA and France — finds the waves real and synchronized, but spaced **25–41 years (median
  36y)**, with a different named mechanism each era, not a stable 50-year clock. If the schema's
  top layer has no period, "an integral number of Juglars per Kondratieff" is not a claim that can
  even be evaluated — there is no stable Kondratieff length for the ratio to be integral *with*.
- **CS1 (`research/cycles/commodity-deep`, cross-ref `docs/cycles/15-long-waves.md`) shows the
  nearest candidate band sits at the wrong resolution regardless.** Forcing a minimum 15-year gap
  between troughs, the commodity-supercycle count finds **8 troughs spaced 15–21 years** — a real,
  datable arc, but one that lands roughly *between* Juglar's 9–10 years and Kondratieff's claimed
  45–60, matching neither. Even the band nearest in length to Schumpeter's middle-to-long layers
  does not resolve where his own integer-nesting arithmetic requires it to.
- **BC1 (`research/cycles/buscycle-deep/buscycle-RESULTS.md`, cross-ref `docs/cycles/
  18-business-cycle.md` §A.3(ii)) shows the classical business-cycle band is real but does not
  resolve into discrete Kitchin multiples either.** On the JST R6 analogue panel, median
  peak-to-peak spacing across 301 spacings in 18 countries is **6 years (IQR 5–9y)**, with **65%**
  falling inside the pre-registered [3,7]-year window — a clean PASS for the *band's* existence,
  one level below Schumpeter's own Juglar (this is Atlas 2.3, the business cycle proper, not 2.5).
  Nothing in that spacing distribution clusters into discrete multiples of a ~40-month unit — a
  6-year median business cycle does not decompose cleanly into "roughly two Kitchins," it simply
  spans a range that happens to be commensurate with two-to-five 40-month intervals depending on
  which spacing in the distribution one selects, exactly the kind of after-the-fact curve-fitting
  the nesting claim requires and the data does not supply.
- **KJ1 (`kitchin-RESULTS.md`, this monograph's own Part A.4) removes the base layer of the pyramid
  too.** Zero of 37 pooled monthly-commodity-price spacings land inside the claimed [30,50]-month
  Kitchin band across two independent 37–58-year cells. The schema's *foundational* unit — the one
  every higher layer is supposed to be built from an integer count of — has no period either.

**Put together, the desk's own evidence sweeps the entire claimed spectrum of Schumpeter's schema —
40 months, 9–10 years, 45–60 years — and finds a period at none of the three claimed frequencies,
while finding real, persistent, datable bands at several *different* resolutions instead (a
3–7-year growth band, a 15–21-year commodity-arc band, a 25–41-year inflation-regime band, and,
via the credit/capex seats directly, a 3–6-year and 3–5-year band respectively).** The nested-clocks
*picture* dies for exactly the reason Kondratieff's own single clock died in the sibling monograph —
a genuinely well-evidenced partial observation (technology clusters into waves; credit cycles
recur; inventories oscillate) was pattern-matched into a period it never actually kept, and a
different named mechanism explains each supposed repetition once the dating is checked carefully.
But — and this is the reason 2.5's verdict differs from 2.4's — **the objects Schumpeter's middle
layer was pointing at survive**, under modern names, at whatever resolution the actual evidence
supports: Juglar's own 7–11-year credit-investment fluctuation is not a myth invented by an
overconfident 19th-century economist. It is exactly what this program's **L10 credit block** (tau_
half 36–72 months) and **L11 capex cycle** (tau_half 36–60 months) already measure, on a modern
Schularick-Taylor/Baron-Xiong/Drehmann-Juselius evidentiary base Juglar could never have had access
to, rather than on his own qualitative 19th-century crisis chronology.

### B.3 The fold, stated precisely

**7–11-year investment/credit fluctuations are already seated, and were seated before this pair of
atlas entries was ever written.** `config/ladder.yaml` carries **L10_credit_block** (tau_half
[36,72] months ≈ 3–6 years; Schularick-Taylor 2012, Baron-Xiong 2017, Drehmann-Juselius AUROC
0.83–0.85 → India prior 0.65–0.75; Tier B; `macro_credit_block`) and **L11_capex_cycle** (tau_half
[36,60] months ≈ 3–5 years; India chronology n≈2–3 — boom 2003–08, prolonged slump 2011–2020,
revival 2021+, per D08 I3/I4's OBICUS/IIP-capgoods/GFCF triad; Tier C, `reduce_only`,
`contribution_clamp: non_positive`; same shared `macro_credit_block`) — between them spanning
almost exactly the band Juglar dated qualitatively from bank balance sheets and Schumpeter later
labeled with Juglar's name. This is precisely the atlas's own one-line verdict for 2.5: **"the
19th-century name for 2.1+1.6's band ... historical label; no separate content."** Atlas entry
2.1 is the credit cycle (L10's own seat); atlas entry 1.6 is the India infrastructure/capex
supercycle (which itself folds into L11, per the atlas's Band-1 table). Juglar's object was never
missing from this design — it has been present, under its modern name and modern evidentiary
standard, since the ladder's first draft.

**What the fold means operationally, stated as a rule rather than a sentiment.** A Juglar-labeled
signal cannot enter the registry as new alpha, because its content — credit growth predicting
crisis risk, capex-cycle overbuild/underbuild states — already sits inside L10/L11's **shared**
`macro_credit_block` budget (≤20% of regime score, `docs/DESIGN.md` §4.2's de-duplication rule;
`config/ladder.yaml` line 12). If a future research pass ever produces something calling itself a
"Juglar-cycle timing model," the correct first step is not to evaluate whether it deserves a new
seat; it is to check whether it is simply L10 and/or L11 read through a different filter — and, per
the pattern this program has now confirmed at least twice (Kondratieff/inflation-regimes in
`docs/cycles/15-long-waves.md`; business-cycle/macro-block in `docs/cycles/18-business-cycle.md`
§A.1(iv)), it almost certainly is. Any genuinely new content such a pass surfaces gets folded into
L10 or L11's own construction notes; it does not get a budget line of its own under a borrowed
19th-century name.

**Why retiring names while keeping objects is register discipline, not dismissal — one paragraph,
stated once, because it is the entire reason this pairing exists at monograph length rather than as
a single dismissive line in the atlas.** A cycle-based portfolio program that has already reached
**64 registered hypotheses** (`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` §16) and counting survives only by refusing to
let a compelling, historically-freighted name buy a second budget line for a state the design
already prices. Every new candidate that walks into this research program wearing an old,
prestigious 19th-century label — Juglar, Kondratieff, Kitchin, and by direct extension any future
"Schumpeterian wave" or "long-cycle innovation clock" claim — must be checked against the register
*before* it is allowed anywhere near a budget line, precisely because "a Juglar signal" or "we're
in the Juglar upswing" are exactly the kind of confident, historically-freighted framings that let
a state variable this desk already carries inside its risk system quietly re-enter the
conversation as if it were independent, newly-discovered alpha — the identical "label-shopping"
failure mode the atlas's own rejected list (§7) and its Part-II de-duplication principle (§14:
"most 'new' cycle families are projections of states Part I already holds, onto a different
surface") exist specifically to prevent. Retiring the *name* costs this program nothing at all —
the *object*, and its budget line, stay exactly where the evidence already put them, inside L10 and
L11, unmoved. What is lost, and what should be lost, is only the ability to sell the same
credit-investment state twice, once under its modern citation and once under Juglar's name, as
though the second sale were a different discovery.

## C. The verdict pair

**2.4 — Kitchin inventory cycle: CONTEXT, unbudgeted.** The mechanism is real and, per §A.2, is
demonstrably alive today: Forrester's 1961 demand-amplification finding and Lee-Padmanabhan-Whang's
1997 four-cause formalization describe an order-flow oscillation that requires nothing more than
ordinary local inventory policy and lag structure to reproduce, and the 2021–24 semiconductor
shortage-then-glut, the Target/Walmart 2022 inventory crisis, and the 2022–24 freight recession are
three independent, verified, global-scale instances of exactly that mechanism firing within the
last five years. The clock is dead: KJ1 finds zero of 37 pooled spacings inside the claimed
[30,50]-month band across two independent 37–58-year cells of monthly commodity prices, closing —
together with BC1, RE1, CS1 and KW1 — this program's entire pre-registered clock-test frequency
sweep from 40 months to six decades with **zero surviving fixed periods**. India's own data are
honestly thin: no monthly inventory aggregate exists, and the four available proxies (an IIP-vs-
sales residual, a look-ahead-biased filing aggregate, a paid PMI sub-index, and a single-sector
FADA series) each fall short of a Tier-B bar on their own. The design consequence matches the atlas
exactly: **CONTEXT, unbudgeted** — global inventory/PMI waves read through **L9**
(`global_financial_cycle`), never a seated India state; a domestic inventory-wedge design (**KJ-D1**)
and a PMI-stocks-subindex read through L9's own input family (**KJ-D2**) remain pre-registered,
Tier-C, runsheet-gated candidates per `partDH-verdict-routing.md`, neither auto-promotable, both
carrying an explicit, honestly-stated expectation that they may fail — exactly the standard this
atlas holds every H-series candidate to.

**2.5 — Juglar investment cycle: FOLD into L10 (credit) + L11 (capex), never double-counted.**
Juglar's 1862 discovery — crises as the endogenous, recurring terminal phase of a credit expansion,
dated from primary banking data nearly a century before Burns-Mitchell formalized the method — is
historiographically real and deserves the credit the record gives it. Schumpeter's 1939 three-cycle
schema, which gave Juglar's object its modern periodic label and nested it inside Kondratieff and
Kitchin, does not survive: KW1/KW2 empty the top layer, CS1 shows the nearest middle-length band
sits at the wrong resolution, BC1 shows the business-cycle band below Juglar's own does not resolve
into discrete Kitchin multiples, and KJ1 (this monograph, §A.4) empties the base layer too — the
desk's own trials sweep all three claimed frequencies and find none of them a clock. But the object
Juglar actually identified — a 7–11-year credit-investment fluctuation — survives intact, because
it was never dependent on Schumpeter's nesting arithmetic in the first place: it is precisely what
**L10_credit_block** (tau_half 36–72 months, Schularick-Taylor/Baron-Xiong/Drehmann-Juselius) and
**L11_capex_cycle** (tau_half 36–60 months, India n≈2–3, OBICUS/IIP-capgoods/GFCF) already measure,
sharing one `macro_credit_block` budget. The Juglar label is retired, permanently, precisely so it
cannot re-enter this program's registry as new alpha under a different name; the object it named
stays exactly where L10 and L11 already carry it.
