## PART A — Theory and evidence: seven centuries to today

### A.1 Schmelzing's dataset and the suprasecular claim

**What it actually is.** Paul Schmelzing's "Eight Centuries of Global Real Interest Rates, R-G,
and the 'Suprasecular' Decline, 1311–2018" (Bank of England Staff Working Paper No. 845, January
2020; SSRN 3485734/3520654) reconstructs an annual global real long-rate series back to 1311 for
eight economies — Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, France, Germany, Japan, and
the United States — chosen because together they account for roughly **78% of advanced-economy
GDP** on average across the sample. It is not one instrument measured continuously for 700 years;
it is a splice. The series opens with personal loans to sovereigns and city-states in Renaissance
Italy (Venetian, Genoese and Florentine lending to princes and communes, with recorded nominal
rates as high as ~35% in the 14th century), runs through Habsburg Spain's forced and voluntary
loans, the Dutch Republic's *renten* once Amsterdam became Europe's deepest capital market, British
consols and gilts once London took that role, and modern marketable sovereign bonds (culminating in
US Treasuries) once New York did — each era's series segment is the long-term borrowing rate of
whichever polity then hosted the deepest, most liquid long-duration credit market, deflated by a
constructed inflation series for that era and place. By the mid-19th century, nominal rates on the
key reference loans had fallen from that 14th-century ~35% to roughly 6%, en route to the
20th–21st-century range this desk actually operates in.

**The headline.** Schmelzing's own framing: across successive monetary and fiscal regimes and a
variety of asset classes, a **trend decline of roughly 0.6–1.6 basis points per year** has
prevailed since the monetary upheavals of the late Middle Ages — a "suprasecular" trend, his own
coinage for a decline operating on a timescale longer than any single secular (multi-decade)
cycle and indifferent to the rise and fall of the gold standard, Bretton Woods, and fiat-floating
regimes in turn. Popular and secondary coverage frequently rounds this to "~1–2bp/yr" or, in a few
summaries, closer to "~2bp/yr" **[VERIFY: Schmelzing's own paper states the range as
0.6–1.6bp/yr in the abstract/summary language located this session; some secondary sources round
to ~1.8–2.0bp/yr — the discrepancy was not resolved by primary-source access, which this session's
network egress blocked for bankofengland.co.uk, ssrn.com, and nber.org directly]**. The atlas's own
line 44 already carries this forward as "~1–2bp/yr," and that is the figure this file treats as
load-bearing — the second decimal place is not.

**For the atlas.** This is the theoretical anchor for atlas entry 0.3 and the reason it exists at
all: an economic historian assembled the longest real-rate series that has ever been built, and it
shows a very slow negative drift. Nothing in what follows disputes that the drift is real, in the
narrow statistical sense of being detectable in a 700-year sample. What follows disputes — and our
own SR1–SR3 already discipline — whether that detectability licenses anyone to *use* it.

**Sources.** Schmelzing (2020), BoE SWP 845 **[Verified: existence, title, venue, headline range
via multiple independent secondary summaries this session; primary abstract text not directly

### A.2 The critiques — and what SR1 actually corroborates

**Data quality pre-1700.** Schmelzing himself, in public discussion of the work, has described the
period between antiquity and the 12th–13th century as "completely dark territory" archivally, and
medieval-source decipherment as a genuine methodological challenge — an admission from the author,
not an outside critic, that the earliest ~150–200 years of the 700-year series rest on thinner and
harder-to-verify archival ground than the modern tail. This matters proportionally: the 14th–16th
centuries contribute a large share of the total *level* decline (nominal nes fell from ~35% to
single digits) that anchors the visual "suprasecular" story, over exactly the centuries the
underlying loan records are hardest to authenticate and least standardized in form.

**Composition and splice effects — "does it measure one object?"** The deeper structural critique,
independent of any single data point's accuracy, is the one CONTRACT's own house standard already
applies to any long-run splice (cf. this program's reserve-currency-deep dossier on COFER's own
splice concerns): each multi-decade segment of Schmelzing's series is the borrowing rate of a
*different* borrower, in a *different* instrument, under a *different* monetary regime, with a
*different* risk/liquidity profile — a personal loan to a 14th-century prince with meaningful
default risk is not the same object as a 21st-century US Treasury bond with none; a forced loan
extracted under threat is not the same object as a voluntarily subscribed gilt. The series is best
read as "the going real cost of long-duration credit to the safest available borrower in the
deepest available capital market of each era" — a coherent and useful construct, but a *different*
one from "the real interest rate," a single stable economic quantity tracked continuously. Whether
a multi-century linear trend fitted across that chain measures a persistent economic force (falling
risk premia, deepening capital markets, as Schmelzing argues) or is partly an artifact of which
instrument happens to sit in the splice at which end is not resolvable from the series alone.

**The self-critique that matters most: Rogoff, Rossi & Schmelzing.** The most consequential
discussion of the original result comes from Schmelzing's own later collaboration with Kenneth
Rogoff and Barbara Rossi, "Long-Run Trends in Long-Maturity Real Rates, 1311–2021" (NBER WP 30475,
2022; published in the *American Economic Review* **[VERIFY: exact volume/issue/pages — located
via secondary aggregation as AER 114(8): 2271–2307, 2024, not confirmed against the primary AER
page]**). Re-examining the same long-maturity sovereign series with formal structural-break tests,
the paper finds three things directly relevant here: **(1)** the full 700-year sample is trend
stationary, and the persistent downward drift (~1.6bp/yr) is statistically real across the whole
window; **(2)** that same trend is **not reliably detectable in sub-samples of roughly 150 years**
— almost exactly our own SR1–SR3 window (1870–2020); **(3)** only two structural breaks are
consistently robust across specifications in seven centuries — the Black Death and Spain's 1557
"Trinity" default — with a 1914 break weaker but present in several series, and, notably, **the
1981 break most narratives assume matters (the Volcker disinflation, the start of the "modern"
decline) is NOT robust** in their re-estimation. A companion strand of work applying the same
700-year dataset to test whether the decline is a *monetary* phenomenon (nominal rates and
inflation moving together, leaving no residual real-rate signal) instead finds a one-to-one
Fisherian relationship between inflation and nominal-rate co-movement, i.e. evidence *against* the
decline being purely a monetary/inflation artifact **[VERIFY: exact authorship and journal — a
paper titled "Are Real Interest Rates a Monetary Phenomenon? Evidence from 700 Years of Data"
was located via ScienceDirect this session; the venue metadata returned by search was internally
inconsistent between "International Review of Financial Analysis" and "International Review of
Economics & Finance," and the author list was not confirmed]**, which sits in tension with Borio,
Disyatat, Juselius & Rungcharoenkitkul's "Why So Low For So Long? A Long-Term View of Real Interest
Rates" (BIS Working Paper 685, 2017 **[VERIFY: exact author order/year — recalled with high
confidence, cross-checked via one secondary source this session, not against the primary BIS
page, which the proxy blocked]**), which argues the received secular-stagnation/saving-glut
reading is incomplete because it treats the decline as purely structural when a meaningful share is
a *disequilibrium* effect of the monetary and financial cycle validating itself — low rates
lowering the estimated "natural" rate, rather than the reverse. The honest state of the literature,
in one line: Schmelzing's own most careful re-examination cannot find his trend inside a
150-year window, an independent BIS strand thinks part of the trend is monetary self-validation
rather than structural, and a third strand pushes back on that with the same data. None of this
overturns the 700-year headline; all of it should make anyone uncomfortable trusting the number at
a tradeable horizon.

**What SR1 actually is, honestly stated.** Our own SR1 computed a naive pooled linear trend of
**−1.27bp/yr on JST's clean, standardized 1870–2020 panel** — a dataset built for exactly this kind
of comparison (consistent instrument definitions, documented inflation series, no splice across
qualitatively different borrower types within the window). That −1.27bp/yr sits squarely inside
Schmelzing's 0.6–1.6bp/yr range and close to the ~1.6bp/yr Rogoff-Rossi-Schmelzing full-sample
estimate. That is a genuine, independently-computed corroboration — **of the order of magnitude
only**. It says nothing about whether the mechanism is structural or monetary, says nothing about
whether it is stable across regimes (B.3 below), and — per Rogoff-Rossi-Schmelzing's own point
(2) — is exactly the kind of 150-year sub-window in which their own re-estimation could not
reliably detect the trend they confirm exists over the full 700 years. SR1's number and
Schmelzing's number rhyming is evidence the magnitude is not a fluke of one dataset's construction.
It is not evidence the magnitude is a tradeable fact.

### A.3 The modern secular-decline literature

Five distinct mechanisms have been offered for *why* real rates fell across the 1980s–2010s, each
with a different implied policy lever and a different vulnerability to the 2022–24 reversal (A.4).

**Rachel & Summers — secular stagnation, the savings-investment channel.** Łukasz Rachel & Lawrence
H. Summers, "On Falling Neutral Real Rates, Fiscal Policy, and the Risk of Secular Stagnation"
(*Brookings Papers on Economic Activity*, Spring 2019) argue neutral real rates fell by **at least
300bp** over a generation, and would have fallen by considerably more — likely into deeply negative
territory — absent large offsetting fiscal deficits and debt issuance across advanced economies over
the same period; the private sector generated a savings glut that expansionary fiscal policy
partially absorbed, holding the *observed* neutral rate higher than the *underlying* private-sector
one. This is the modern academic heir to Bernanke's saving-glut framing (below), formalized with an
explicit fiscal offset.

**Laubach-Williams / Holston-Laubach-Williams — r-star, and the honesty of the error bars.** Thomas
Laubach & John Williams, "Measuring the Natural Rate of Interest" (*Review of Economics and
Statistics* 85(4), 2003), introduced the workhorse Kalman-filter approach to estimating an
unobservable "natural" real rate jointly with trend growth; Holston, Laubach & Williams (2017)
extended it to a three-country (US, euro area, Canada) semi-structural model that remains the
field's reference estimate, published and updated by the New York Fed. The honest finding is not
the point estimate — it is the confidence interval. The model's own published 95% band for the
smoothed two-sided r-star estimate has spanned roughly **+5.5% to −4.5%**, a ~10 percentage-point
width, around point estimates that have themselves ranged from roughly 1.3% (2021) down toward
~1% (2025) **[VERIFY: exact CI figures and vintage — sourced via Richmond Fed Economic Brief
24-36 and related secondary summaries this session, not the primary New York Fed r-star data page
directly]**. This is not a criticism of the modeling — Laubach and Williams are explicit that r*
is inherently unobservable and standard errors on model-estimated natural rates run to "several
percentage points" by construction — it is the single cleanest piece of evidence in this entire
literature for why no seat should ever consume a point r-star estimate as a number.

**Bernanke — the global saving glut.** Ben Bernanke's Sandridge Lecture, "The Global Saving Glut
and the U.S. Current Account Deficit" (Virginia Association of Economics, Richmond, 10 March 2005),
attributed falling US long rates in the mid-2000s to a surge of savings from export-surplus
emerging markets (chiefly China and oil exporters) recycled into US Treasuries and other safe
dollar assets — the mechanism behind Greenspan's 2005 "conundrum" (long rates not rising alongside
Fed hikes). The mechanism's empirical premise — persistent, large EM current-account surpluses
recycled disproportionately into advanced-economy safe assets — has structurally weakened since:
China's current-account surplus as a share of its own GDP is a fraction of its mid-2000s peak, and
the cohort of EM reserve accumulators has both diversified into other assets (gold; A.6 of the
reserve-currency-deep dossier) and, in some cases, become net reserve *drawers* rather than
accumulators. The mechanism is well-evidenced for its own decade; whether its premise still holds
today is a live and separate question, not answered by the fact that it was once right.

**Goodhart & Pradhan — the reversal thesis, the live counter-case.** Charles Goodhart & Manoj
Pradhan, *The Great Demographic Reversal: Ageing Societies, Waning Inequality, and an Inflation
Revival* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2020), argue almost the mechanism in reverse: the 1990–2020 decline
in rates and inflation was driven substantially by a one-off demographic dividend — China's and
Eastern Europe's entry into the global labor supply suppressing wage bargaining power worldwide —
and that dividend is now itself reversing as those same cohorts age out of the workforce, shrinking
the effective global labor supply, strengthening worker bargaining power, and structurally lifting
both inflation and real rates for decades to come. This is the one mechanism on this list that
explicitly predicted something like 2021–24 *before* it happened, which is exactly why it must be
treated as a live, unresolved counter-case rather than either confirmed or dismissed: the 2021–24
inflation and rate spike is consistent with their story, but is at least as well explained by
pandemic supply-chain disruption, unprecedented fiscal stimulus, and the 2022 European energy shock
— none of which are the slow demographic mechanism Goodhart-Pradhan describe. A single loud episode
cannot discriminate between "the demographic reversal has arrived" and "a demand-and-supply shock
hit, unrelated to demography, in a decade when demography also happened to be turning." Section G.1
returns to why this ambiguity is itself an operator-psychology hazard.

**Caballero, Farhi & Gourinchas — the safe-asset shortage.** Ricardo Caballero, Emmanuel Farhi &
Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, "The Safe Assets Shortage Conundrum" (*Journal of Economic Perspectives*
31(3), 2017) and related work model the secular decline as the price signature of a persistent
excess of global demand for genuinely safe, liquid stores of value over the supply the world's
sovereigns and financial systems have generated — the safe real rate falls (and can fall into a
"safety trap" recession) as the market clears the shortage through price rather than quantity. Of
the theories reviewed here, this is arguably the most portable to the 2022–24 reversal: it is a
supply-and-demand framework, not a one-directional secular claim, and a large expansion in
safe-asset *supply* (the post-pandemic surge in advanced-economy sovereign issuance) is exactly
what its own logic predicts should raise the market-clearing safe real rate — consistent with, not
contradicted by, what happened.

**Mian, Straub & Sufi — the saving glut of the rich.** Atif Mian, Ludwig Straub & Amir Sufi, "The
Saving Glut of the Rich" (NBER WP 26941; as of this session, under revision, "revise and resubmit"
at the *Review of Economic Studies* **[VERIFY: current publication status — a July 2025 working
draft was located; formal acceptance/publication not confirmed]**), trace US household saving by
income group through tax records back to 1963 and find top-1% saving has surged since the 1980s at
a scale comparable to the cross-border global saving glut and, they argue, equally responsible for
depressing the safe real rate and inflating asset prices — inequality as *cause* of a low r-star,
not merely correlate. Household wealth concentration has not meaningfully reversed since 2022 even
as real rates rose sharply, which is a genuine tension for the mechanism as a *cyclical* explainer,
though its authors would reasonably reply that it was always offered as a decades-scale channel, not
a two-year one.

### A.4 The 2022–2024 reversal, and the honest scorecard

**What happened to real rates.** The 10-year US TIPS yield (FRED DFII10) fell to a record low
around **−1.0% to −1.2%** in mid-to-late 2021 (a new-issue 10-year TIPS auction priced a record
**−1.016%** real yield in July 2021) and, over the following roughly two years, rose to about
**+2.4% to +2.5%** by late 2023 — a swing on the order of **350bp in under two years**, among the
sharpest real-rate reversals on record, and one this program's own JST-based SR3 finding
independently frames as "reversed roughly four decades of decline in two years" against the
longer-run trend **[VERIFY exact trough/peak dates and values — sourced via secondary reporting of
FRED DFII10 and TreasuryDirect auction results this session; the primary FRED series was not
pulled directly, egress to fred.stlouisfed.org was not attempted after repeated blocks on adjacent
domains]**. Term premium — the compensation investors demand for duration risk, separate from
expected short-rate path — moved with it: the ACM (Adrian-Crump-Moench) term-premium estimate
embedded in the 10-year Treasury rose by roughly **150bp in a single quarter (Q3 2023)** amid
concerns over widening US fiscal deficits and rising net issuance (the Federal Reserve's own
"Treasury Tantrum of 2023" note), and moved again with the September 2024–January 2025 repricing of
the expected Fed cutting path. A market that had priced essentially no compensation for
duration/fiscal risk through the 2010s began pricing some again.

**The honest scorecard.** No single theory in A.3 is cleanly falsified by 2022–24, because none of
them made a precise, testable prediction about a two-year window — but the episode stresses each
differently. Rachel-Summers's savings-investment channel is arguably *least* disturbed: fiscal
deficits exploded further post-pandemic, which their own framework predicts should push the
observed rate up relative to the underlying private-sector one, exactly what happened. HLW/r-star
is wounded not in its logic but in its usefulness — the model is a trend-extraction filter by
construction, and a filter cannot distinguish "regime change" from "noise" until years of data
accumulate on the other side, which is precisely B.2's point below. Bernanke's saving-glut
mechanism looks the most dated — its central premise, sustained EM current-account surplus
recycling, has weakened structurally since the 2000s, independent of what real rates did in 2022.
Goodhart-Pradhan's reversal thesis is the one genuinely *live* case: proponents can point to
2021–24 as first confirmation; skeptics can point to the confounding pandemic/war shocks and note
that a demographic mechanism operating on a decades-scale clock should not resolve itself in three
years either way. Caballero-Farhi-Gourinchas travels best, because its supply-demand logic
accommodates a reversal in either direction depending on quantity, not just price. Mian-Straub-Sufi
is the most strained by the episode as a short-run explainer, though its authors' own claim was
always about trend, not cycle.

**"Was 2010–2021 the anomaly?"** This is now an open, live disagreement between two of the field's
most prominent voices, not a settled question this program can adjudicate. In January 2023,
Lawrence Summers stated he did not expect a return "to the era of secular stagnation" once the
post-pandemic disinflation completed; days later Olivier Blanchard published a rebuttal titled
"Secular Stagnation Is Not Over" (Peterson Institute, 24 January 2023), arguing the deep structural
forces (aging, inequality, safe-asset demand) that produced the pre-2020 decline had not gone away
and would reassert themselves once the pandemic-and-fiscal-shock noise cleared. Both economists
helped define the secular-stagnation literature this file cites; both looked at the same 2022–23
data and drew opposite conclusions about which regime is the anomaly. That disagreement, at the top
of the field, with full access to every dataset discussed here, is itself the strongest possible
argument for Part B's verdict.

### A.5 India's real-rate history, briefly

**The administered-rate era.** For most of the pre-1991 period, Indian interest rates were not a
market outcome in any sense comparable to the JST panel or Schmelzing's splice: the SLR/CRR
apparatus (documented in `research/cycles/debt-deep/partA-theory-psychology.md` A.4/A.9 — SLR
falling from 38.5% toward 18% only across the liberalization era) created a captive market for
government paper, and lending and deposit rates were directly administered by the RBI rather than
cleared by supply and demand. A "real interest rate" computed on pre-1991 Indian data would be
measuring administrative fiat minus realized inflation, not a market's read of intertemporal
tradeoffs — it is not a comparable object to any series discussed in A.1–A.4, and this file does
not attempt to splice one in. Deregulation proceeded gradually through the 1990s (lending rates
substantively freed from 1994; deposit-rate deregulation phased through the decade and beyond).

**The only clean sample: post-2016 inflation targeting.** The RBI Act was amended in May 2016 to
mandate flexible inflation targeting, formalized by an August 2016 government notification setting
a **4% CPI target with a 2–6% tolerance band**, executed by a six-member Monetary Policy Committee.
This is the only window in which an Indian policy real rate reflects an actual, rules-anchored
reaction function rather than administered fiat or an unformalized discretionary regime — and it is
roughly a decade old as of 2026, well short of CONTRACT's own clock-test bar (≥4 observed complete
cycles) for treating it as anything but a single, ongoing regime observation. Tier C, by this
program's own standard.

**Current context.** As of the RBI's August 2026 policy review, the repo rate stood at **5.25%**,
against July 2026 headline CPI of **4.45%** — a current real repo rate of roughly **+0.8%** — with
the RBI's own FY27 CPI forecast near **5.0%**, implying a forward-looking real repo closer to
**+0.25%** if realized **[VERIFY: repo rate, CPI print, and forecast sourced via secondary
financial-press aggregation this session (finnovate.in, indiainfoline.com, tradingeconomics.com);
not cross-checked against RBI's own primary MPC resolution or DBIE release]**. This sits toward the
low end of the post-2016 regime's own experience without being unusual in any global-panel sense —
exactly the kind of single data point this file's Part B argues a desk should read as one more
observation in a ten-year, non-clock-tested regime, not as a signal.

## PART B — Why no seat: the case for CONTEXT-only, made affirmatively

### B.1 SR2's 14× ratio is the argument

The single fact that settles this file's verdict is one this program computed itself. SR2 measured
the median country's rolling 30-year mean real rate range across the JST panel at **5.4
percentage points**, against a secular trend contribution over the same 30 years of **0.38
percentage points** — a ratio of roughly **14×**. At the only horizon a multi-decade design like
this one could ever hold a position on the strength of a suprasecular thesis, the noise around the
trend is fourteen times larger than the trend itself. A seat sized off this series would be sized
off the smaller of two forces, betting that fourteen units of swing will not overwhelm one unit of
drift inside the holding period. That is not a risk a Tier-C, n≈1 narrative earns the right to take
(CONTRACT §4).

### B.2 The r-star literature's own confidence intervals are wider than the object

A.3's HLW discussion is not a digression — it is B.2's evidence. The most widely used, most
carefully engineered estimator of "the" natural real rate publishes a 95% confidence interval
roughly **10 percentage points wide** around point estimates that themselves range across barely
2–3pp of history. When the acknowledged measurement uncertainty on a quantity exceeds the entire
plausible range of the quantity's *true* values under debate, that quantity cannot be a seat input
— it can only be read, at most, as a directional, low-frequency backdrop. This is the estimation
version of B.1's noise-to-signal argument: SR2 shows the *realized* swings dwarf the trend; HLW
shows the *estimated* uncertainty band, even net of realized swings, dwarfs the trend too. Two
independent lines of evidence — one empirical, one econometric — converge on the same conclusion.

### B.3 Regime non-stationarity: this is not one object across 700 years

A.2 already raised the composition concern; Rogoff-Rossi-Schmelzing's own structural-break results
sharpen it into something closer to a disqualification for tactical use. In seven centuries of
data, spanning the transition from bilateral princely lending through bullion-anchored specie
regimes, the classical gold standard, Bretton Woods, and floating fiat, only **two** breaks are
robust across specifications — the Black Death and Spain's 1557 default — with the 1914 break
weaker and, most tellingly, **the 1981 break most narratives assume is real (the one usually
credited with "starting" the modern decline) not statistically robust at all** in the authors' own
re-estimation. If the paper's own co-authors cannot reliably locate the regime shift everyone
assumes matters, this desk should not assume it can locate the *next* one in real time, nor assume
the drift measured under a gold-anchored 19th century and the drift measured under a fiat-floating
21st century are governed by the same mechanism simply because a line fitted through both slopes
downward.

### B.4 What a desk could wrongly do with this series — three named failure modes

**(1) Terminal-multiple assumptions.** "Real rates will stay low (or negative) indefinitely,
therefore equities structurally deserve a permanently higher multiple" is exactly the mistake
priced into growth-equity valuations through 2020–21 and unwound violently when real rates snapped
back 350bp-plus in under two years (A.4). A terminal real rate baked into any DCF or Gordon-growth
terminal value is a single point drawn from a distribution B.1 shows is fourteen times narrower than
the swings around it — treating that point as fixed is a stationarity bet the data explicitly
reject.

**(2) Duration bets sized on "reversion."** "Real rates must mean-revert down over the long run, so
overweight long-duration bonds now" mistakes a 30-year-horizon statistical statement (SR1/SR2) for
a tradeable signal at position-holding horizons. A book sized to this thesis heading into 2022 sat
through one of the sharpest bond bear markets in decades; the suprasecular trend does not protect a
position held for quarters or even a few years against multi-hundred-basis-point cyclical swings
running in the opposite direction.

**(3) "Lower forever" leverage.** Using a persistently-low assumed real financing cost to justify
structurally higher standing leverage or duration — in the debt sleeve, or via the margin book — is
the exact mistake CONTRACT's own leverage-permission machinery already exists to prevent. `Known
Prior #4` shows 1.25x standing leverage would have produced roughly −58% in 2008 and −36% in
March 2020; CONTRACT §10 makes `funding_rate` a first-class, live config parameter precisely because
a real-rate regime shift is exactly the kind of shock that breaks a leverage assumption calibrated
to a 2010s "low forever" backdrop. This series must never be the reason that parameter is set once
and forgotten.

### B.5 The two legitimate uses

**Gold-floor debate input.** L15 (`docs/DESIGN.md` line 248) already lists "persistence of negative
real rates" among its own Tier-C, no-numeric-authority inputs, sourced from FRED DFII10 — this file
supplies the multi-century and cross-country intellectual backing for *why* that input belongs
there at all: the debt monograph's own DS2–DS4 findings (1945–80 repression era: 44% of
country-years with negative real rates, mean −0.8%; `research/cycles/debt-deep/jst-debt-RESULTS.md`)
show negative-real-rate persistence is the empirical signature of financial repression, and a
700-year backdrop in which real rates fall for a sovereign's political-economy reasons roughly as
often as (or more than) they fall for Rachel-Summers's structural reasons is directly relevant
context for the gold-floor case — without this file, or L15, ever converting that context into a
sizing number the repression gauge and the debt monograph's own frozen parameters do not already
supply.

**Terminal-multiple humility.** Not a number to plug in — a discipline to impose. Any construction
in this program that embeds an assumed terminal real rate (a Stage 3 optimizer input, a Stage 2
forward-looking valuation view) should be stress-tested against SR2's realized 5.4pp 30-year swing,
never against the −1.27bp/yr trend. "Humility" here means the suprasecular literature's only
legitimate output is a wider sensitivity band on any terminal-rate assumption, never a narrower or
more confident one.

## PART G — Operator psychology: two capture risks, one countermeasure

### G.1 "Rates always fall back" — 2010s anchoring

An operator who spent 2009–2021 inside a near-zero-to-negative real-rate world can over-learn that
world as normal, and read the 2022–24 reversal as a temporary aberration due to fade back toward
the 2010s regardless of the state of the underlying mechanisms. The failure mode is concrete:
under-pricing duration risk, or re-entering "buy the dip" long-duration positioning on the implicit
assumption that Rachel-Summers's or Bernanke's channels simply resume once inflation normalizes —
exactly the assumption Goodhart-Pradhan's live counter-case (A.3) exists to unsettle, and exactly
the assumption B.4(2) names as a rejected use.

### G.2 "Regime change" maximalism, the mirror error

The opposite failure treats the same 350bp, twenty-month reversal as proof the entire suprasecular
decline was a zero-interest-rate-policy artifact, now permanently over, and extrapolates two years
of data into conviction about a new multi-decade regime. Rogoff-Rossi-Schmelzing's own finding —
that their trend is not reliably detectable even in **150-year** sub-samples — is the direct
rebuttal: if the co-authors of the definitive re-estimation cannot find the signal in a window
nearly a hundred times longer than the 2022–24 episode, no desk-level read of two years is entitled
to more confidence than they have. Summers and Blanchard's own live, unresolved disagreement (A.4)
is the same point from the opposite direction — if the field's most credentialed participants,
with full data access, cannot agree which regime is the anomaly, a position-sized conviction call
is overconfident by construction.

### G.3 The countermeasure

Two structural features do the actual work, neither requiring the operator to out-reason the
literature in the moment either error feels most compelling. **The CONTEXT designation itself**
carries zero numeric authority: this series never writes a number into a config parameter, a
position size, or a `funding_rate` assumption (CONTRACT §10) — B.4's three failure modes are
foreclosed by construction, not by discipline that must be exercised anew each cycle. **The annual
review cadence**, paired with the fact that atlas entry 0.3 fails CONTRACT's own clock test
outright (this is not even a state variable ordered by `tau_half` in the risk system's own ladder —
it is Tier-C narrative, full stop) means no faster re-read is defensible and, per the 2022–24
lesson that multi-decade quiet can end in eighteen months, no slower one is safe. The same review
that re-reads L15's reserve-currency inputs annually re-reads this file's evidence annually,
checking only whether a genuinely new structural-break-quality event has occurred — never whether
the last four quarters "felt like" a regime shift.
