## PART A — The claimants, steelmanned

Every framework below is stated at its strongest, in its proponent's own terms, before Part B
touches it. A rejection that skips this step is not a rejection this desk can stand behind.

### A.1 Dalio's Big Cycle — the 8-metric empire arc

Ray Dalio's *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order: Why Nations Succeed and Fail*
(Avid Reader Press/Simon & Schuster, 2021) **[VERIFY: exact imprint/date]** builds a composite
**index of a nation's overall power** from eight measures — education, competitiveness, innovation
and technology, economic output, share of world trade, military strength, financial-center
strength, and reserve-currency status — layered onto a wider set of roughly eighteen tracked
determinants (debt, internal order, wealth gaps among them) **[Verified: the eight-measure
composite and its constituent categories via multiple independent summaries this session; the
"eighteen determinants" framing corroborated but not independently recounted against the primary
text — VERIFY]**. Dalio's own stated claim about duration is specific and worth stating exactly
because Part B will need it precisely: **"important empires typically lasted roughly 250 years,
give or take 150 years,"** with the internal economic-debt-political sub-cycles inside that arc
running **50–100 years each [VERIFY: exact wording and figures against the primary text; corroborated
via multiple independent secondary summaries this session, not the book itself]**. His own
comparative spine is three detailed case studies — the **Dutch, British, and American** empires,
each with its currency's reserve-status arc charted against the composite power index — with
shorter treatments of Germany, Japan, the USSR/Russia, India, and successive Chinese dynasties
layered around them. This is the atlas's own "n≈3 pattern-matched" characterization (line 47):
three fully worked comparators, not a large sample.

**What the framework actually predicts.** Dalio's live claim is that the United States sits in
**late-stage decline** (falling relative education and innovation share, rising internal
wealth-gap and polarization, large deficits monetized by the central bank) while **China is in a
rising, if imperfect, ascent** — the same structural position, on his own chart, Britain occupied
relative to a rising America a century ago, and the Dutch Republic occupied relative to a rising
Britain two centuries before that. **What it hedges:** Dalio is consistently on record that this
is a framework "to help you think," not a forecasting instrument — no declared year for any
transition, no committed probability, no falsification condition alongside the prediction. That
hedge is not incidental; Part B.5 shows it is structurally required for the framework to survive
contact with evidence.

### A.2 Modelski's long cycles of world leadership

George Modelski's *Long Cycles in World Politics* (University of Washington Press, 1987)
**[Verified: publisher and year via multiple independent bibliographic sources this session]**
proposes that the modern international system has moved through **five successive ~80–120-year
cycles of world leadership**, each launched by a "global war" that concentrates naval/maritime
power in a single "world power," followed by delegation and eventual deconcentration: **Portugal
(1494–1580), the Netherlands (1580–1688), Britain I (1688–1792), Britain II (1792–1914), and the
United States (1914–)** **[Verified: the five-cycle sequence and dates via multiple independent
secondary summaries this session]**. The mechanism Modelski proposes is specific, not vague: world
leadership rests on **command of the oceans** — a navy able to project and police global
sea-lanes — and each cycle's "global war" is the contest that decides who holds that command for
the following century. This is a genuinely falsifiable-sounding mechanism (count capital ships,
count contested sea-lanes) and a genuinely long, carefully dated historical reconstruction — the
strongest thing in Modelski's favor is that, unlike Dalio's composite index, it names one causal
variable and tracks it across five centuries with real archival specificity (his and William
Thompson's companion volume, *Seapower in Global Politics, 1494–1993*, is the data appendix).

### A.3 Organski's power-transition theory and Allison's "Thucydides Trap"

A.F.K. Organski's *World Politics* (Knopf, 1958) **[VERIFY: exact edition/page details]** inverted
the balance-of-power intuition: he argued that a roughly **even distribution** of power between a
ruling state and a rising challenger is the dangerous condition, not the safe one — parity plus a
dissatisfied challenger is when war is "most likely, of longest duration, and greatest magnitude,"
while a large power gap in either direction is comparatively stable **[Verified: core claim via
multiple independent summaries of the primary argument this session]**. Graham Allison's *Destined
for War: Can America and China Escape Thucydides's Trap?* (Houghton Mifflin Harcourt, 2017)
**[VERIFY: exact imprint]** operationalized this for the US-China relationship through the Harvard
Belfer Center's "Thucydides's Trap Case File": a systematically assembled set of historical
instances since roughly 1500 in which a rising power's growth put it on a collision course with a
ruling power. **The headline finding, stated at its final, most-cited count: 16 cases, 12 of which
ended in war [Verified: the 16-cases/12-wars figure via the Belfer Center's own case file and
multiple independent secondary sources this session]** — a 75% hit rate the book's marketing and
much subsequent commentary treats as close to a natural law. Steelmanned honestly: Organski's
underlying logic (rapid relative shifts, not absolute levels, are destabilizing) has genuine
theoretical pedigree independent of Allison's case count, and the case file itself is a serious,
named, sourced historical project — not an invented number.

### A.4 Turchin's structural-demographic theory and cliodynamics — the strongest-looking claim

Peter Turchin's structural-demographic theory (built on Jack Goldstone's earlier demographic-
structural work) identifies three reinforcing pressures that build toward internal — not
necessarily interstate — instability: **popular immiseration** (stagnant or falling real wages
relative to elite income), **elite overproduction** (more aspirants to elite status — degree
holders, wealth, political ambition — than the society's elite positions can absorb, intensifying
intra-elite competition), and **state fiscal distress** (declining state capacity to manage the
resulting conflict, often visible in rising public debt). These combine into a **Political Stress
Indicator (PSI)**, and the theory posits **roughly 50-year instability cycles** in complex agrarian
and industrial societies, driven by the multi-generational lag between a wage/elite-production
shock and its political expression. **The specific, dated, in-advance claim — the one entry in
this entire literature that meets a real forecasting bar — is Turchin's 2010 *Nature* comment,
"Political instability may be a contributor in the coming decade"** (*Nature* 463, p. 608, 4
February 2010) **[Verified: exact title, journal, volume/page, and date via Nature's own page and
PubMed this session]**, which explicitly forecast that measures of political instability in the US
and Western Europe, driven by rising inequality, elite overproduction, and growing public debt,
"look set to peak in the years around 2020" **[Verified: the "around 2020" framing via the primary
article and multiple independent secondary summaries this session]**. He elaborated the model at
book length in *Ages of Discord: A Structural-Demographic Analysis of American History* (Beresta
Books, 2016) **[Verified: title, publisher, 2016 date via multiple independent bibliographic
sources this session]** — worth noting plainly that Beresta Books is a small press, not a
university or major trade publisher, a fact Part B.4 returns to. This is, by a wide margin, the
best-performing advance claim in the entire hegemonic-conflict/instability literature this chapter
examines: a specific mechanism, a specific decade, published ten years before the fact. B.4 scores,
both ways, exactly what that confirmation is worth.

### A.5 Strauss-Howe generational theory (the *Fourth Turning*) — the pop end

William Strauss and Neil Howe's *Generations* (1991) and *The Fourth Turning: An American Prophecy*
(1997) **[Verified: titles and 1991/1997 dates via multiple independent bibliographic sources this
session]** propose a fixed, repeating sequence of four ~20–25-year "turnings," composing an
**~80–100-year "saeculum"** — the length of a long human life — with the fourth turning ("the
Crisis") a recurring period of institutional upheaval and, often, major war (the theory's own
examples: the American Revolution, the Civil War, the Great Depression/WWII) **[Verified: saeculum
length and turning structure via multiple independent secondary summaries this session]**. Its own
proponents' honest caveat, stated by the authors themselves, is that "the timing can wriggle around
a bit" — turnings must happen in *order*, within the ~80–100-year frame, but exact calendar dates
are explicitly not committed **[Verified: the authors' own hedging language via multiple independent
secondary summaries this session]**. Steelmanned at its best: the theory correctly identifies that
societies do appear to move through alternating high-cohesion and high-fragmentation moods across
multi-generational spans, and it has a real predecessor in serious historiography (Arthur
Schlesinger Sr.'s cycles-of-American-politics work). It earns its "pop end" placement not because
the underlying observation is silly, but because — as Part B will show — its forecasting apparatus
gives itself no way to be wrong.

## PART B — The dismantling, argument by argument

### B.1 n≈3–5 instances, each under a different regime — the draws-from-different-processes problem

`docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md`'s own Band-0 learning note (line 50) states this problem in general form,
for every entry in this band, before this chapter formalizes it for hegemonic conflict
specifically: *"every Band-0 entry looks like it has 4+ observations if you count sub-periods
mechanically — but each 'period' ran under a different monetary regime (gold standard vs fiat,
closed vs open capital account), so the realizations are not draws from one process."* Applied
here: Dalio's n≈3 (Dutch, British, American) spans a mercantile-chartered-company economy, a
first-wave-industrial gold-standard economy, and a fiat-reserve-currency nuclear-armed economy —
three monetary regimes, three technology bases, three institutional forms of "empire." Modelski's
n≈5 fares no better: a 15th-century caravel-and-spice-trade "world power," a 17th-century
joint-stock-company maritime empire, two centuries of one power (Britain) under two different
technology regimes (sail vs steam, then steam vs oil), and a 20th–21st-century nuclear superpower
whose command-of-the-sea-lanes mechanism looks nothing like a 16th-century Portuguese fleet's.
Contract §4's clock test requires **≥4 observed complete periods** before a phenomenon may be
called a cycle rather than a state variable; both frameworks fail this on raw count (n=3, n=5, and
n=5 is itself contested — B.3), and fail a second, deeper way even where the count nominally
clears the bar: **the observations are not draws from one stable process**, the entire statistical
justification for treating repeated instances as evidence of a recurring mechanism. A sample of
five events under five different generating processes is not a sample of five; it is five samples
of one each.

### B.2 Mechanism heterogeneity — one label, different causal stories

The point above generalizes: not only do the eras differ institutionally, the **proposed cause of
each transition differs case to case even within a single framework**. In Modelski's own account,
Portugal's rise is explained by navigation technology and spice-trade monopoly; the Dutch
transition by financial and shipbuilding innovation; Britain's rise by naval administration and,
later, industrial-coal advantage; America's by continental scale, industrial output, and eventually
nuclear/carrier airpower. These are not the same mechanism recurring — five different, individually
plausible, single-case narratives sharing only the *label* "command of the sea-lanes." The same
critique applies to Dalio's composite: the Dutch decline is a story about the English wars and
financial overextension; the British decline is two world wars' balance-sheet destruction and
imperial overstretch (`docs/cycles/06-reserve-currency.md`'s own sterling account); American
relative decline, on Dalio's own telling, is domestic wealth-gap polarization more than external
military defeat. Three different failure mechanisms, one word — "decline" — stretched across all
three. A framework that explains every instance with a different mechanism is not identifying a
recurring cause; it is retroactively fitting a common label onto whatever happened in each case,
precisely the pattern-matching-not-recurrence failure `docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` line 65 already names,
in almost identical language, for the Kondratieff wave.

### B.3 Selection and dating flexibility — degrees of freedom that fit any narrative

Every framework in Part A requires answering questions with no non-arbitrary answer, and the
answers move the result. **Who counts as "hegemon"?** Modelski's own count of five cycles requires
counting Britain *twice* (two separate "world power" tenures straddling one continuous nation)
while treating the Dutch Republic's shorter primacy as a single full cycle — defensible, but not
the only defensible coding; treating British primacy as one continuous ~226-year cycle instead
collapses n=5 to n=4 with no change in the underlying history, only the counting convention. **When
does a hegemonic "war" start, and who is a genuine "rising challenger"?** Allison's own case count
is the clearest demonstrated instance of this degree of freedom, because it moved *in print*: the
2015 *Atlantic* essay version cited **11 of 15 cases** leading to war **[Verified: the 11-of-15
figure and its 2012–2014 dating via multiple independent secondary summaries of the project's own
published history this session]**; by the book and the Belfer Center's finished case file, the
count had grown to **16 cases, 12 of which ended in war**. The ratio held roughly constant
(73% → 75%), but a rigorously "historical" case set expanding by more than a quarter between
publications, with the project's own stated criteria unchanged, is itself the demonstration:
"rising challenger" and "ruling power" are judgment calls about GDP share, military strength, and
historians' consensus, not measured facts with agreed thresholds — reasonable analysts applying the
same rule produced different case lists from the same 150-year window. Critics have pushed further,
noting a candidate list of 14 *additional* possible cases splits **7-to-7** between war and peace
**[Verified via multiple independent secondary summaries of Belfer Center supplementary material
this session]** — a coin flip's worth of selection sensitivity sitting immediately outside the
headline 16's boundary. The same flexibility infects "when does the war start": is the US-Japan
case's war Pearl Harbor, or the 1930s naval-treaty breakdown? Is the Cold War a "war" at all, given
no direct superpower combat? Every reasonable answer is available, and each produces a materially
different case count and hit rate — exactly the degree-of-freedom structure that lets a motivated
analyst land close to whatever conclusion they began with.

### B.4 The retrodiction-vs-prediction record, honestly — Turchin scored both ways

Retrodiction — fitting a story to events already known — is nearly free; genuine advance prediction,
with a date and a stated stake, is the only test that counts, and by that standard **every
framework in Part A except one has never made one**. Dalio's book, published 2021, describes a
US-China transition already visibly underway (the 2018 tariff war, COVID-era stimulus, 2020s
polarization all pre-date the book) — retrodiction wearing the grammar of forecast, not a
2005-vintage claim about 2020. Modelski's cycles are entirely retrospective; the theory names no
calendar date for the *next* transition with any precision (secondary-literature estimates for
"the next global war"/leadership transition run anywhere from the 2020s to the 2030s depending on
who is extrapolating, none of it a committed claim in Modelski's own primary text). Organski's 1958
theory is a mechanism, not a dated forecast; Allison's book is a 2017 warning about a relationship
already the dominant subject of contemporary commentary. Strauss-Howe's 1997 *Fourth Turning* did
name "around 2005–2008" as a plausible next Crisis onset — closer to a genuine advance claim — but
paired with the authors' own explicit "timing can wriggle" hedge that forecloses scoring it cleanly.

**Turchin's 2010 *Nature* piece is the one genuine entry: a specific mechanism, a specific decade
("around 2020"), published in a leading general-science journal a full ten years before the window
it named, with no retraction or quiet redating in the interim.** Scored honestly, both directions:
**for** — Turchin and Korotayev's own retrospective, "The 2010 Structural-Demographic Forecast for
the 2010–2020 Decade: A Retrospective Assessment" (*PLOS ONE* 15(8), 2020, e0237458) **[Verified:
title, journal, volume/issue/year/article number via PLOS ONE's own page, PubMed, and NCBI/PMC this
session]**, assembled real, external instability measures (protest and riot counts, not a
self-reported index) across the US and Western
Europe and found them rising sharply through the decade, broadly consistent with the PSI's
trajectory; the 2020–2021 US instability episode (culminating, on the paper's own framing, near the
January 2021 Capitol events) landed inside the named window. **Against** — three qualifications
this chapter will not paper over. **(1) Self-graded.** The retrospective is by the same author who
made the forecast, scoring his own model against instability measures he had latitude to select and
weight — not an independent replication; the *PLOS ONE* paper's own framing is explicitly a
self-assessment, not a blind test. **(2) Decade-scale, not event-scale.** "Instability rises through
the 2020s" is a coarser, more hedgeable claim than "X happens in year Y," and a decade is wide
enough that *some* qualifying instability episode is close to guaranteed to land inside it for any
complex society under any of several unrelated causal stories (a pandemic, a financial crisis, an
election dispute — none specifically predicted in kind, only in general temperature). **(3) The
construct itself is contested.** "Elite overproduction" has drawn serious academic criticism —
described by critics as "conceptually confused and empirically false" in its strongest form, its
necessity and sufficiency for the outcomes Turchin attributes to it separately disputed
**[Verified: the critical characterization and its substance via multiple independent academic and
long-form critical sources this session]**. The honest scorecard: a real, dated, directionally-
vindicated advance claim — genuinely the best in this literature — earned by a mechanism whose core
construct remains contested and whose confirmation was self-graded on a decade-wide, multi-causal
target. Meaningfully better than everything else in Part A, and still nowhere near a timing
instrument a desk could have sized a position on in, say, 2015.

### B.5 The unfalsifiability structure — hedged horizons, moving definitions

Put B.1–B.4 together and a common structure emerges: **the horizon is long enough, and the
definitions loose enough, that no plausible near-term outcome can refute the theory.** Dalio's
"250 years, give or take 150" per empire means a transition arriving in 2035 or 2075 is equally "on
schedule." Modelski's theory survives any decade's events because "world power" status is itself a
graded judgment, not a discrete event with a public trigger. Organski/Allison's case-selection
flexibility (B.3) means the *next* US-China outcome, however it resolves, can likely be added to
whichever side of the ledger the analyst already favored, using the same "leading historians'
judgment" standard the original 16 cases used. Strauss-Howe's authors say so explicitly: dates
wriggle, order does not. Even Turchin's comparatively precise claim was decade-wide, not year-wide,
and self-graded. None of this claims bad faith — Dalio's own hedges are stated in good faith as
the honest limits of the exercise — it is the structural reason Contract §4 assigns this entire
class Tier C (narrative, reduce-only) rather than treating "n≈3" or "n≈5" as a promotion-eligible
count the way it would for, say, four completed JST-panel deleveraging arcs
(`docs/cycles/05-debt-supercycle.md` DS1) measured against one stable fiat-era definition.

### B.6 What the frameworks get right — already held through measurable channels

None of this makes the underlying observation *wrong* — war and hegemonic rivalry plainly matter
to markets — it makes the **clock** the thing to reject, not the **channel**. And every channel
these frameworks gesture at, this program's design already carries, with data, a budget, and a
falsification condition, none of it timed off a turning-count:

- **War finance drives debt arcs.** `docs/cycles/05-debt-supercycle.md`'s own DS1–DS4 panel is
  substantially a wartime-debt panel by construction: several of its pre-1971 case studies *are*
  the two world wars' debt buildups and descents (the UK's ~260%-of-GDP Napoleonic-war peak, the
  US and UK's WWII peaks and the contested growth-vs-inflation-vs-capital-levy descent literature,
  France's WWII "quiet default" via inflation). The debt-supercycle seat (atlas 0.1, L15) already
  measures the *consequence* of major war finance — level and persistence-of-negative-real-rates —
  with no need to know a war is coming; it reads the balance sheet after the fact.
- **Wars end monetary orders.** `docs/cycles/06-reserve-currency.md`'s sterling case is explicit
  that the only *completed* modern reserve-currency transition "took four decades and two world
  wars" — WWI reversed Britain's net-creditor position and produced the sterling-balances overhang
  WWII then made unpayable, forcing the 1949 and 1967 devaluations. War finance is *inside* the
  reserve-transition seat (0.2, L15) as the documented cause of the one transition on record, not
  an external clock bolted onto it.
- **Wars start inflation regimes.** `docs/cycles/09-inflation-regimes.md`'s IR1 dates the fiat
  era's completed up-arc from **1930**, an acceleration inseparable from WWII-era deficit financing
  across the panel — the war's monetary consequence sits inside a measured, dated regime object,
  not a timing signal from counting hegemonic turnings.
- **Rivalry drives reserve diversification.** `docs/cycles/06-reserve-currency.md`'s sanctions
  leg — central-bank gold buying accelerating since 2022, tracked via WGC tonnage and the RC0–RC3
  COFER computation — is the live instance of the geopolitical-rivalry channel Dalio's book
  gestures at, measured monthly from a named source, with an honest null already recorded (RC3: no
  COFER-level regime break detected) rather than asserted from a narrative arc.

**The punchline, stated plainly:** this book already carries every measurable consequence of
conflict through seats with data, budgets, and falsification conditions attached — debt (0.1),
monetary order (0.2), inflation (0.5), reserve composition (0.2's sanctions leg). What it refuses
is not conflict as a causal force in markets; it is the **clock** — the claim that a hegemonic-cycle
turning-count can tell a desk *when* the next war-scale event arrives, at what stakes, with enough
lead time to be worth sizing a position on. Nothing in Part A clears that bar, and B.1–B.5 show
why nothing in the class plausibly could.

### B.7 The market-facing evidence on war and returns

Set the grand cycles aside entirely and ask the narrower, tradable question directly: **what does
war actually do to equity returns and volatility?** The record, verified, is genuinely
counter-intuitive and argues against a war-timing overlay on its own terms, independent of
everything in B.1–B.6.

**The long-run equity record through the two world wars is a "stocks still won, painfully" story,
not a "wars destroy returns" story.** Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton's *Triumph of the
Optimists: 101 Years of Global Investment Returns* (Princeton University Press, 2002) **[Verified:
title, publisher, year via multiple independent bibliographic sources this session]** documents
1900–2000 real returns across sixteen countries and is explicit that the first half-century — "two
world wars, the Great Depression, hyperinflations" — is where the real damage concentrates; the
book's central finding is that naively extrapolating the *second* half-century's calmer, higher
real returns overstates what a buy-and-hold investor should expect, precisely because the first
half's wartime episodes are the tail risk a longer sample reveals **[Verified: the book's central
survivorship/optimism thesis and its invocation of the world wars as the damaging episodes via
multiple independent summaries this session; country-specific real-return figures through each war
— VERIFY against the primary data appendix]**. The lesson is not "wars are good for stocks" —
several national markets (Germany, Japan, continental Europe) were effectively destroyed or closed
by the world wars — it is that the **US and UK aggregate record**, survivorship caveats aside,
shows equities recovering and compounding through and after both wars: a very different claim than
a "sell before the war starts" timing rule would need to be true.

**The "war puzzle" literature is the sharper, modern instrument, and it points the same direction.**
G. William Schwert's foundational observation (*Journal of Finance*, 1989) is that realized US
stock-return volatility is **not** elevated during major wars — running roughly a quarter *lower*
than the full-sample average **[Verified: Schwert's 1989 "Why Does Stock Market Volatility Change
Over Time?" (*Journal of Finance* 44(5)) and the ~25%-lower wartime volatility finding via the
paper's own results and multiple independent summaries this session]**, despite wars intuitively
raising uncertainty. Gustavo Cortes, Angela Vossmeyer and Marc Weidenmier's NBER working paper,
"Stock Volatility and the War Puzzle: The Military Demand Channel" (NBER WP 29837, 2022) **[Verified:
title, authors, WP number, 2022 date via NBER's own page this session]**, extends the puzzle across
1890–2017 US defense-spending data and finds volatility runs **about 33% lower** during wars,
proposing a mechanism: rising defense spending makes a wide swath of corporate cash flows *more*
predictable, because government procurement is steadier than the private demand it displaces —
visible in tighter analyst-forecast dispersion for procurement-intensive firms after 9/11 and the
Iraq invasion **[Verified: the ~33% figure and forecast-dispersion mechanism via the paper's own
abstract/results and the NBER Digest's summary this session]**. Amelie Brune, Thorsten Hens, Marc
Rieger and Mei Wang's own "war puzzle" paper (*International Review of Economics* 62(1), 2015,
pp. 1–21) **[Verified: authors, journal, volume/issue/pages, year via IDEAS/RePEc and the paper's
DOI record this session]** adds a related pattern: rising war *likelihood* depresses stock prices,
but the actual **outbreak** of an anticipated war tends to *raise* them (uncertainty resolving into
a known, often lesser-than-feared state) — except a genuine surprise outbreak, which drops prices,
the opposite case. Together: markets do not price "war" as one homogeneous shock a cycle-timing
overlay could front-run — they price the *change in uncertainty*, cutting differently by whether
the war was expected, whether it resolves ambiguity, and how spending shifts. **A defense-sector
effect is real and separable**: procurement-intensive cash flows become more forecastable and, per
Cortes et al., equity behaves accordingly — a sector-level, not a market-timing, implication (B.8
below).

**Geopolitical-risk indices are the honest, already-measurable object — watchable without any
cycle theory.** Dario Caldara and Matteo Iacoviello's Geopolitical Risk (GPR) Index (*American
Economic Review* 112(4), 2022, pp. 1194–1225) **[Verified: authors, journal, volume/issue/pages,
year via the AEA's own published record this session]** is built from automated text search across
ten major newspapers for eight event categories — war threats, peace threats, military buildups,
nuclear threats, terror threats, war onset, escalation, terror acts — updated **monthly, free,
since 1985 [Verified: methodology, category structure, monthly cadence, free availability via
Iacoviello's own hosted GPR page and the Fed's IFDP working-paper version this session]**,
normalized to average 100 across 2000–2009, extended back to 1900 in the authors' historical
companion series. GPR's **unconditional** power to predict *future* equity returns is genuinely
weak and contested — country-specific GPR betas show modest out-of-sample predictive power for a
minority of markets in recent cross-country work **[Verified: the mixed, partial-country
predictability finding via multiple independent recent academic summaries this session — VERIFY
the specific study and exact hit-rate against the primary paper]** — while its **contemporaneous
and forward-looking associations with investment, employment, and disaster/tail-risk probabilities
are considerably better established**: the authors' own paper finds elevated GPR foreshadows
*lower* investment and employment and *higher* downside-risk probability, a real-option/uncertainty
finding closer to this program's own vol-and-tail framing than to directional return timing. **The
design implication: GPR is a free, monthly, transparent index a desk can watch for exactly the
content it is good at — volatility and tail conditioning, not return-direction timing — without
adopting any hegemonic-cycle theory to justify watching it.** It sits inside this program's
existing tail-budget and regime-context machinery (B.6) rather than requiring a new seat.

### B.8 India positioning — two live borders, no clock needed

India has fought four wars with its immediate neighbors since independence with usable market
records for the later ones: **1962** (China), **1965** (Pakistan), **1971** (Pakistan/Bangladesh
independence), and **1999** (Kargil, Pakistan). The pre-Sensex era (Sensex launched only in 1986,
on a 1978–79 base) means the 1962/1965/1971 figures are retrospectively reconstructed estimates
from secondary financial-history sources, not primary exchange data: the BSE index fell roughly
**8% over the back half of 1962** (a steeper ~16% by mid-1963), roughly **15–18% over the 17-day
1965 war**, and a comparatively modest **8–10% over the 13-day 1971 war [VERIFY: all three

The honest reading across all four: India's war-market response has been **short-lived and
dominated by whatever broader cycle was already in motion** — consistent with B.7's finding that
war-outbreak effects are event-specific, not evidence of a tradeable hegemonic-conflict clock.

cycle-timing one.** Domestic defense production reached a reported **₹1.78 lakh crore in FY26,

a standing structural fact this desk holds as context, exactly as `docs/CYCLE_ATLAS.md` line 47
prescribes: informing how sector exposure and tail budget are read, never how they are timed.

## PART G — The psychology of grand-cycle narratives

### G.1 Why they sell

Hegemonic-cycle and war-cycle narratives share three psychological hooks that operate independently
of their evidentiary merit, and naming them is the first countermeasure. **Narrative coherence.**
A single organizing story — Dutch → British → American, or five centuries of naval succession, or
a 50-year elite-overproduction clock — converts an overwhelming, disorderly historical record into
a legible plot with a beginning, middle, and (implied) end, cognitively far more satisfying than
the true state of the evidence: a handful of genuinely different episodes that share a label.
**Mortality/civilizational salience.** These frameworks are, almost without exception, framed
around decline and succession — the reader's own nation's possible twilight, a hegemonic war
within the reader's own lifetime — which recruits the same machinery terror-management research
finds behind heightened receptivity to worldview-confirming, high-stakes narratives generally:
threat to a cherished in-group identity raises the emotional stakes of the story being *true*,
independent of its evidence. **The guru structure.** Several of Part A's proponents (most visibly
Dalio, whose book trades explicitly on his standing as one of the most successful macro investors
alive) carry personal authority that transfers, unearned, onto the specific historical claims — "a
person this successful at reading markets must also be right about 500 years of empire" does not
follow, but it is the implicit argument the packaging makes.

### G.2 The specific operator hazard — geopolitical scare plus Big Cycle book

Name the compound risk directly, because it is the reason this chapter exists at proportionate but
non-trivial length rather than as a one-line dismissal. A live geopolitical scare — a border
incident, a sanctions escalation, a war scare in the news cycle — arriving alongside a well-marketed
Big Cycle narrative is **the doom-cult vector squared**: it supplies both the emotional trigger
(G.1's mortality salience, now concrete and dated) and an intellectually respectable-looking
framework that converts "I am frightened by this headline" into "I have read the 500-year pattern
and I know where we are in it" — more dangerous for a disciplined desk than fear alone, because it
comes pre-equipped with the vocabulary of rigor (charts, composite indices, case counts) that fear
alone lacks. This is structurally the same failure mode `docs/cycles/05-debt-supercycle.md` G.2
names for debt ("doom-cult" capitulation into all-gold/all-hard-assets, DS4's own +4.5% real-equity
mean in the fiscal-dominance state the counter-evidence) and `docs/cycles/08-golden-constant.md`
G.1 names again for gold ("same evidence, doubled") — this chapter names the identical pattern
rather than assuming immunity because the trigger differs. The countermeasure those sibling
monographs already built — a hard-coded, registry-enforced **reduce-only ceiling** that
structurally cannot be overridden by a compelling narrative, however well-sourced — generalizes
here without modification: a genuinely well-evidenced partial truth (debt *does* matter; gold
*does* have a monetary role; war *does* drive debt, inflation, and reserve diversification, per
B.6) gets converted, under emotional load, into license for a conviction trade the evidence never
supported.

### G.3 The desk's rule

Conflict enters this program's design through exactly three channels, and never a fourth:

| Channel | What it is | What it is not |
|---|---|---|
| The GPR index (B.7) | A free, monthly, newspaper-text-based measure of geopolitical tension a desk can watch for its genuine content: investment/employment foreshadowing and tail/vol conditioning | A return-direction timing signal — its unconditional return-predictability record is weak and contested |
| The tail budget L15 already carries (0.1/0.2 seats) | War's measurable *consequences* — debt arcs, monetary-order transitions, reserve diversification — already flow through DS1–DS4, the reserve-transition seat, and IR1's own war-shaped up-arc dating | A justification for a fourth, separately-timed seat; L15 already prices in war's aftermath without needing to predict the war |
| The measurable seats (0.1 debt, 0.2 reserve/sanctions, 0.5 inflation) | Where every verified, falsifiable channel in B.6 already lives, with data and a stated tier | A place a hegemonic "turning-count" gets to add authority it has not earned |

**Never a turning-count.** No rule in this design may condition sizing, hedge ratio, or sector tilt
on "where we are" in a Dalio arc, a Modelski cycle, a Thucydides-Trap case tally, or a Turchin PSI
reading, for the same reason `docs/cycles/09-inflation-regimes.md` B.2(1) forecloses arc-timing for
its own, evidentially *stronger* n=2 completed arcs: if two mechanically-dated, single-mechanism,
single-country-panel arcs cannot clear Contract §4's clock test for timing authority, n≈3–5
narrative-heterogeneous, cross-mechanism, selection-flexible hegemonic "cycles" certainly cannot.
The rule is structural, not a reminder to stay disciplined — the same lesson every sibling
monograph's Part G has already converged on, because a memorized argument is exactly what a
compelling, well-marketed narrative is long enough and loud enough to erode in the moment it is
needed most.
