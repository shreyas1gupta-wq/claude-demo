## B1. What a commodity supercycle is, and how this record reads it

**The mechanism, stated once and then tested nine times — and stated honestly against the desk's own
panel result.** Atlas 1.3 names the supercycle's mechanism precisely: extraction capacity takes a
decade or more to build, so a genuine demand shock meets inelastic supply, prices run for years,
capital eventually floods in, and the resulting overbuild produces a decade-long glut on the way back
down. This is a **capacity-limit** mechanism in the Contract's own §5 taxonomy (survival argument ii) —
not a behavioral anomaly that decays as it becomes known, but a physical and financial lag that
persists because mines, wells, and refineries cannot be built faster no matter how well-understood the
cycle is. It is real at the level of individual, well-documented cases (cases 4, 5, and 8 below), and
CS2's cross-group breadth confirms the *state* it produces is genuine and broad, not one commodity
wearing several hats. But CS3/CS4's sign-consistency failure, stated above, means this Part treats
"mechanism" as case-level narrative throughout, never a sized or dated rule — a verdict below that says
the mechanism "appears" deliberately stops short of "confirmed as a tradeable regularity." The clearest
modern formalization
of the shape is David Jacks's "From Boom to Bust: A Typology of Real Commodity Prices in the Long Run"
(NBER WP 18874; *Cliometrica* 13(2), 2019; extended in later work to 42 commodities spanning **1850–
2024**), which decomposes real commodity prices into a long-run trend, medium-run cycles, and
short-run boom/bust episodes, and finds — as the atlas already states — real commodity prices modestly
rising since 1950, punctuated by large, long-lived cycle deviations from trend that are themselves
dotted with sharper boom/bust episodes. This Part supplies the case-by-case evidence *underneath* that
typology; the desk's own re-estimation of it (CS1–CS4, forthcoming) is a separate, later exercise this
Part does not anticipate.

**Nine global cases plus India at double length, in the order the task specifies, each checked
against the same question: does the extraction-capacity-lag mechanism actually appear, or is the
episode driven by something else (a demand spike with no supply-lag content, a war, a policy cartel, a
market-microstructure accident)?** The distinction matters directly for H53's own honesty: a
"supercycle" label applied to every large commodity move would smuggle war shocks, stockpiling panics,
and exchange-clearing failures into a mechanism that only some of them actually instantiate. Cases 3,
6, 7 and 9 below are included specifically because they are **not** clean instances of the mechanism —
a negative case teaches the boundary as much as a positive one does, and CYCLE_ATLAS.md's own
"REJECT" discipline (§7) exists for exactly this reason.

**A note on India's asymmetric role, which the record makes structurally obvious before any case is
read.** Every one of the nine global cases below is written from the perspective of a market that sets
or receives a world price. India, entering the record only in §B3, receives every one of these
episodes as an import bill — crude, gold, coal, edible oils, fertilizer feedstock — so a "supercycle
up" state that is unambiguously good news for Chile or Australia (case 8) is unambiguously a terms-of-
trade, current-account, and inflation headwind for India, while the same state is very good news for a
narrow slice of the Indian equity market (metals, energy upstream) at the same time. This double
sign — one macro state, two opposite portfolio consequences — is the reason atlas 1.3 tags H53 for
**both** a macro CAD/INR conditioner (via L9) **and** a sector-tilt conditioner, and it is the
organizing idea §B3 returns to at its close.

## B2. Nine global case studies

### 1. The 1890s–1910s boom — electrification, the 1907 copper panic, and WWI inflation

**Electrification as the demand shock.** The light bulb, the electric trolley car, and long-distance
telegraphy created what one industry history calls an "insatiable demand for copper" from the late
1800s onward — a single 1890 underground high-voltage mains project between Deptford and London
(Ferranti's) alone consumed **nearly 140 tons** of copper. World refined copper usage stood at **under
500,000 metric tons in 1900**, rising continuously thereafter as electrification spread from a handful
of cities to entire national grids. Compiled long-run price series put copper at roughly **$357/tonne
in 1900**, **$425/tonne in 1906**, and **$441/tonne in 1907** — on a cents-per-pound basis, a rise from
roughly **16.2¢/lb to 20.0¢/lb**, on the order of a **24% real climb over seven years**
`[VERIFY: figures are from a compiled secondary long-run series, not re-extracted from a primary LME
or USGS table]` — a genuine but unspectacular metal-price boom, well short of the speculative
multiples the era's *equity* history would suggest.

**The 1907 panic — a stock-market corner, not a metal-market one.** In autumn 1907, Augustus Heinze
and Charles Morse attempted to corner **United Copper Company** shares (not physical copper), betting
that a large short position existed against a stock they believed they effectively controlled. Heinze
bought aggressively from **October 14, 1907**, driving United Copper from **$39 to $52 a share** — the
corner failed when the presumed short interest did not materialize, and the stock collapsed instead.
Bank runs began at Heinze- and Morse-controlled institutions on **October 16–17**; when Knickerbocker
Trust Company — the country's third-largest trust — was revealed to have ties to Morse through its
president Charles Barney, a run forced its collapse within days, triggering the nationwide **Panic of
1907**, the **8th-largest single-episode decline in US stock-market history** at the time, contained
only by J.P. Morgan's privately organized rescue. This episode's historical lineage runs directly back
to the metal market itself: the **Secrétan Syndicate** of 1887–89 had already attempted, and failed
spectacularly at, a *physical* copper corner two decades earlier — buying LME Chile bars near **£40/
ton** and fixing 80% of world mine output at **£68/ton** for three years before the syndicate collapsed
and crashed the price. The 1907 episode is best read as the corner playbook migrating from the
commodity itself to a listed vehicle on it — a lesson in how a genuine demand-driven metal boom can
still detonate a purely financial panic riding on its back, with no supercycle mechanism required for
the panic itself.

**WWI commodity inflation — the boom's second, larger leg.** War finance, not extraction economics,
drove the era's largest price move. US wholesale prices roughly **doubled between November 1914 and
the November 1918 Armistice**, with **industrial wholesale prices up 130% from 1913 to 1920**; in
Britain, a 45-commodity wholesale index rose from **81.2 (June 1914) to 235.2 (December 1919)** — a
**202% increase**. Every belligerent left the gold standard within days of the war's outbreak and
financed the effort substantially through money creation rather than taxation, producing an inflation
that was monetary and logistical (shipping capacity, submarine warfare disrupting trade routes) as much
as it was a genuine capacity-limited commodity boom.

**Verdict.** This case is a layered one, and the layering is the lesson: an authentic, if modest,
extraction-capacity-lag boom in copper (electrification demand outrunning mine development, consistent
with H53's own mechanism) sits underneath a much larger, purely monetary WWI price surge, with a
financial-market corner-and-panic in between that had almost nothing to do with either. A design that
read "1907–1920 commodity prices rose sharply" as a single supercycle instance would be averaging three
different mechanisms into one number — exactly the Slutzky-style trap CYCLE_ATLAS.md's epistemics
section (§0) warns against.

### 2. The interwar collapse — 1920–21 deflation, the 1930s depression, and the first cartel-response era

**1920–21: the sharpest short deflation in the American record.** By the time the episode ran its
course in July 1921, **commodity prices had fallen roughly 40%**, **industrial production had fallen
31%**, and the **BLS wholesale price index had fallen 36.8–46%** depending on measure — "the most
severe drop since the American Revolutionary War." The Consumer Price Index fell **15.8% peak-to-
trough** over twelve months from its June 1920 high. This was the postwar unwind of the WWI inflation
documented in case 1 — prices fell faster and further than contemporaries expected, a violent
correction rather than a supercycle turn in the extraction-lag sense.

**The 1930s commodity depression.** The wholesale price index for all commodities fell a further
**37 percentage points by February 1933**, but — instructively — **not uniformly**: coal, metals, and
building materials (the inputs to capital formation) actually *rose* in real terms even as petroleum
and farm-product prices, which explain an estimated **89%** of the aggregate WPI's 1930s decline,
collapsed. Protectionist tariff responses (Smoot-Hawley and its foreign retaliations) worsened the
balance-of-payments consequences for primary-commodity-exporting economies across Africa, Asia, and
Latin America specifically.

**Rubber: the Stevenson Plan (1922–1928) — the first restriction cartel.** With British interests
controlling roughly **75% of world rubber production** and the US consuming roughly **75%** of it,
Britain's Federated Malay States and Ceylon enacted export-tonnage restrictions effective
**1 November 1922**, chaired by Sir James Stevenson. The result was immediate and large: rubber rose
from **17¢/lb when the scheme began** to **$1.21/lb within three years** — better than a **sevenfold**
increase. The scheme's flaw was structural: it bound only British-controlled production, and Dutch
East Indies and independent smallholder output expanded to capture the restricted price, eroding the
cartel's grip until it was abandoned in 1928.

**Coffee: Brazilian valorization and the "quota of sacrifice."** Brazil had run coffee-price-support
schemes since **1906**, buying at artificial prices in fat years for resale in lean ones; a second
program from **1924** collapsed after four consecutive bumper harvests, helping trigger the **1930
Revolution** and the creation of the Departamento Nacional do Café. Coffee fell from **22.5¢/lb (1929)
to 8¢/lb (1931)** as Depression-era demand collapsed onto 1920s overcapacity. From **1933**, the
government split the crop three ways: **40% burned** (the "quota of sacrifice"), **30% held off market**,
**30% freely traded** — a program that persisted through **1937**, with the outright burning of surplus
coffee continuing until **1944**. The government printed money to fund the purchase-and-burn program
directly, generating domestic income for growers while defending the international price.

**Verdict.** This is the record's first instance of **organized supply restriction as a policy response**
to a supercycle's downside — the direct historical ancestor of OPEC (case 4) and, in spirit if not
mechanism, of Chile's own rule-based smoothing (case 8). Both restriction schemes worked in the near
term (rubber's sevenfold rise, coffee's price defense) and both ultimately failed to hold indefinitely
against non-participating supply or fiscal exhaustion — the first documented instance of a pattern that
recurs through every cartel-era case in this record.

### 3. The 1950s boom — Korean War stockpiling and the birth of Prebisch–Singer

**Korean War stockpiling, 1950–51.** With the outbreak of war in June 1950, the US Congress
appropriated roughly **$2.9 billion over six months** for emergency stockpile purchases, and the
National Defense Stockpile's value rose from **$1.15 billion (1949) to $4.02 billion (1952)** — the
most rapid stockpile expansion in US history. The demand shock hit specific commodities with startling
speed: **wool prices tripled overnight**; **rubber surged more than 200% above pre-Korea levels by
early 1951**; copper, nickel, steel, aluminum, and tungsten shortages actually slowed weapons
production, forcing stockpile drawdowns even as new purchasing continued. Layered onto this was
broader postwar reconstruction demand across Western Europe and Japan.

**Prebisch–Singer, 1950 — the theoretical response to what came next.** Hans Singer's February 1949 UN
paper and Raúl Prebisch's May 1949 Havana address — both published in definitive form in **1950** —
argued for a **secular** (not cyclical) deterioration in primary-commodity terms of trade against
manufactured goods. Prebisch's own index, base **100 for 1876–1880**, had already fallen to **67.3
(1921–25)** and **62.0 (1931–35)** — a documented ~36.5% deterioration *before* either paper was
written — and the hypothesis's real test came in the two decades the task names: the **long 1950s–60s
real commodity decline**, as the Korean stockpiling spike unwound and reconstruction-era demand
matured into a slower-growing steady state, while manufactured-goods prices kept rising with
industrial-country wage growth.

**Verdict.** This is the record's cleanest example of a **pure demand-spike-without-supercycle-
mechanism**: the Korean stockpiling episode is a policy-panic buying wave (a close cousin of case 1's
1907 corner and case 7's 2022 shocks) rather than an extraction-capacity-limited boom, and its unwind
into the Prebisch–Singer decline was driven by the demand shock's own reversal, not by a mining-capex
overshoot of the kind case 5 documents in detail. Useful precisely as a negative case for H53: a large,
fast commodity price move need not imply the atlas's own multi-year capacity-lag mechanism.

### 4. The 1970s supercycle and the twenty-year glut

**The boom: OPEC twice, and a grain shock in between.** Following the Yom Kippur War, the Organization
of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries embargoed the US from **19 October 1973**; within months the
price of oil **nearly quadrupled**, from **$2.90/barrel pre-embargo to $11.65/barrel by January 1974**.
A second shock followed the **1979 Iranian Revolution**: despite a global supply loss of only around
4%, the market's reaction **more than doubled** the price over the next twelve months, to roughly
**$39.50/barrel**, and OPEC's own posted price rose from **$16/barrel (January 1980) to over
$36/barrel (September 1980)**. Between the two oil shocks, the **1972 "Great Grain Robbery"** saw
Soviet agents secretly contract roughly **440 million bushels (~$700 million)** of US wheat and other
grains through summer 1972, partly subsidized by US taxpayers who did not know the scale of the
purchases in advance; US wheat rose from **$1.68/bushel (July 1972) to $3/bushel (May 1973)**. To
prevent a repeat, Washington and Moscow signed a **five-year grain agreement in 1975** permitting up to
**8 million tonnes/year** of stabilized Soviet purchases. Running underneath all of this, **Nixon's
August 1971 suspension of dollar-gold convertibility** ended Bretton Woods and let gold float free — the
real-gold arc that followed (the 1973–82 stagflation-era real return, cited as roughly **+9.2%/yr** in
`docs/cycles/08-golden-constant.md`, itself flagged `[VERIFY]` there) is that monograph's subject
entirely and is not recomputed or re-argued here; it is cross-referenced only as the monetary backdrop
against which the 1970s' *real* commodity boom occurred.

**The bear: Volcker, new supply arriving on the extraction-lag clock, and two sovereign wrecks.**
Paul Volcker's Fed pushed the funds rate from roughly **10% to 20% between October 1979 and mid-1981**,
crushing global demand and driving a strong dollar. Simultaneously, the supply response the 1970s price
signal had commissioned finally arrived — North Sea and Alaskan (Prudhoe Bay/TAPS, first oil 1977)
production, alongside expanded non-OPEC output including Mexican copper capacity at Cananea
`[VERIFY: Cananea-specific production figures for this era not independently re-confirmed this
session]` — landing on the market roughly seven-to-ten years after the price signal that justified the
investment, precisely the atlas's own extraction-capacity-lag mechanism running in full. **The 1986
collapse** followed a deliberate Saudi shift from defending price to defending market share: spot
prices fell from an average **$28/barrel (1985) to $14/barrel (1986)**, with prices briefly under
**$10/barrel** by mid-1986 once Saudi Arabia adopted **"netback pricing"** in August of that year — a
**~60%+ collapse**. **Mexico's 1982 debt crisis** sits directly on this down-leg: a decline in oil
prices combined with rapidly rising US interest rates roughly doubled Mexico's external debt between
1979 and 1982, precipitating the August 1982 moratorium that opened the Latin American "lost decade."
**The USSR's terminal oil dependence** is the down-leg's other sovereign casualty: the 1986 price
collapse cost the Soviet Union billions in hard-currency export revenue at a stroke, forcing reserve
depletion amid deepening shortages across the union — a major, though not sole, contributor to the
structural exhaustion that culminated in **1991**. **The 1998 $10 oil episode** closed out the two-
decade glut: the Asian Financial Crisis devastated demand in a region that had supplied **80% of
global oil demand growth from 1990–97**, and OPEC compounded the error by raising quotas in November
1997 just as the crisis hit; WTI fell to roughly **$10/barrel by December 1998**, with some grades near
**$6**.

**Verdict.** This is the record's fullest single-episode narrative of atlas 1.3's mechanism: a
geopolitically triggered demand/price shock (OPEC) commissioned a decade-plus supply build (North Sea,
Alaska, non-OPEC expansion generally) that then overshot into a nearly **twenty-year glut**
(1980–1999), with two sovereign-collapse case studies riding the down-leg and a genuinely separate
monetary story (gold's decoupling, held entirely by the golden-constant monograph) running in parallel
rather than as part of the same mechanism. Read against CS1's own trough chronology (§B1) — troughs at
**1970 and 1986**, 16 years apart, sit inside the same 15–21-year band the panel finds everywhere else
in 165 years — this is not a special, unusually long "20th-century supercycle" so much as two
ordinary-length arcs of the atlas's own 15–20y prior, placed end to end by one unusually large shock.

### 5. The China supercycle, 2001–2011 — the canonical case, at length

**The demand shock and its intensity.** China's WTO accession in **2001** launched roughly a decade of
GDP growth averaging **~10.5%/year (2002–2011)**, with infrastructure spending rising from
**$200 billion/year (2000) to over $1 trillion/year (2010)**. Steel production rose from
**130 million tonnes (2000) to 820 million tonnes (2014)** — more than the rest of the world combined
by the end of that run — with the acceleration concentrated in the boom's core years and continuing to
climb even after the price peak the next paragraph documents, itself a small instance of the lag this
case is chosen to illustrate. Copper rose from the **low $1,000s/tonne (2001) to over $10,000/tonne
(February 2011)**; iron ore rose from roughly **$12/tonne to $187/tonne by early 2011**
`[VERIFY: exact benchmark-vs-spot reference points for these iron-ore figures]`.

**The benchmark system breaks — the mechanism moving prices in real time.** For roughly **forty
years**, iron ore had traded on an annual benchmark negotiated each November between major producers
and steelmakers. That system broke down across **2008–2010** as the GFC and China's own demand surge
pulled the two sides too far apart to negotiate a single annual number: spot sales rose from **~30% of
China's iron-ore trades in 2008 to ~60% in 2009**; in **July 2009**, BHP Billiton broke the 40-year
tradition by agreeing to sell **30%** of its ore via a mix of spot, quarterly, and indexed pricing; by
**2010**, Vale, Rio Tinto, and BHP jointly abolished annual benchmark pricing in favor of quarterly
contracts referencing the preceding quarter's spot prices — a structural regime change in how the
world's largest bulk commodity is priced, driven directly by the supercycle's own intensity.

**Freight: the Baltic Dry Index's 2008 collapse, and why it does not mark the supercycle's end.** The
Baltic Dry Index reached an all-time peak of **11,793 (20 May 2008)**, driven by the China-led
demand surge outrunning available dry-bulk shipping capacity, then collapsed to **663 (5 December
2008)** — a **~94% fall in seven months**, with a **50% one-week drop in mid-October 2008 alone** as
the credit crunch froze the letters of credit exporters needed to ship cargo. **This is the case's
central distinguishing lesson.** The 2008 crash was severe, fast, and genuinely a crash — but it was a
liquidity and demand air-pocket **inside** an ongoing supercycle, not its terminus: Brent hit its
all-time nominal high of **$147.30/barrel in July 2008**, the CRB Index hit its own all-time high the
same month, both cratered through the GFC — and then a broad set of *other* commodities went on to
their **own** cycle peaks fully three years later: gold at **$1,920/oz**, copper at **$4.63/lb**, and
silver at **$49.50/oz**, all in **2011**. The CRB Index composite was still down **32% from its July-
2008 high as of August 2011** even as several of its own components were making fresh records — proof
that a composite-index reading and an individual commodity's own cycle position can diverge sharply
inside the same supercycle, and that a single violent crash-and-rebound (2008) is not, by itself,
evidence that the underlying multi-year cycle has turned.

**The 2011 peak and the capex lag, observed directly.** The supercycle began unwinding through 2011 as
Chinese infrastructure intensity cooled. The mining industry's own capital response arrived with a
documented lag **after**, not before, that peak: BHP Billiton's capital-and-exploration spending rose
**76.5% from $12.9 billion (FY2011) to $22.7 billion (FY2012)** — a full year *after* several
commodities' own price peaks — before moderating only slightly to **$20.9 billion in FY2013** and a
planned **$16.2 billion for FY2014**. Rio Tinto's own multi-year capex trough after this cycle did not
arrive until roughly **2013**, with its next major spending round — **$11 billion in 2025** — explicitly
described by that company as its largest since **2013**
`[VERIFY: Vale's own comparable capex figures were not independently located this session]`. The
capex peak arriving in **2012–13**, a full **one-to-two years after** the 2011 price peak, is atlas
1.3's extraction-capacity-lag mechanism observed directly in the wild — miners committing the largest
capital of the cycle precisely as the price signal that justified it was already reversing.

**Verdict.** China 2001–2011 is the canonical modern *case* for H53's named mechanism: a demand shock
(China) drove a price boom that broke a forty-year pricing institution (iron ore) and a shipping
market (Baltic Dry) along the way, survived a violent but temporary crash (2008) without that crash
marking its end, peaked in 2011, and only then saw the capital response (BHP/Rio/Vale capex peaking
2012–13) the atlas's mechanism would predict arrives late — one clean instance of exactly the sequence
CS3/CS4 (§B1) tested across the *whole* metals panel and could not confirm as sign-consistent: a real
capex-lag episode, not proof the lag fires reliably enough, across enough metals and decades, to be
sized as a rule rather than read case by case. This is also the case that most directly informs atlas
row **2.11**'s H54 candidate (the China credit impulse) —
the same demand engine driving both a decade-scale supercycle here and a faster, ~3–4-year policy-pulse
cycle there, two different atlas entries reading two different frequencies off the same underlying
economy.

### 6. The 2014–16 collapse — the fastest supply response in oil history

**The price move.** Brent fell from **$112/barrel (June 2014) to $62 (December 2014)** — a **44% fall
in six months** — and on to a **$31 low in January 2016**, a cumulative decline exceeding **70% from
the June-2014 peak**. Roughly half of the initial decline is attributable to weakening global demand
by the literature's own accounting, not supply alone.

**Why shale broke the atlas's usual 7–10-year lag, specifically.** Shale's supply-price elasticity rose
from statistically **insignificant in its 2000–2008 pilot phase** to **1.1 (significant) in its 2009–
2016 expansion phase**, with roughly **85% of the total supply impact of a price change realized within
the first two years** — the fastest documented supply response in oil-market history. The mechanism is
structural, not merely a faster reaction: a shale well runs from spud to first production in **months**,
using modular, repeatable drilling technique, against North Sea or deepwater development timelines
measured in **years**. This is precisely why case 4's 1980s glut took a decade to arrive and this
episode's supply response arrived inside two years — the extraction-capacity-lag mechanism atlas 1.3
names is not a fixed constant across the oil market; it is technology-dependent, and shale specifically
broke it.

**OPEC's market-share war.** Meeting in Vienna on **27 November 2014**, Saudi oil minister Ali
al-Naimi confirmed OPEC would **not cut production** from its 30 million-barrel/day quota — a
deliberate strategy to defend market share against the shale producers threatening a repeat of the
1980s pattern, in which Saudi Arabia had cut output, lost share, and never fully recovered it. The
decision sent prices tumbling further through the rest of the down-leg.

**Negative WTI, April 2020 — a microstructure coda, not a repeat cycle.** On **20 April 2020**, the May
WTI futures contract fell **$55.90 intraday** to close at **–$37.62/barrel**, the first negative print
in the contract's history; Brent, trading on a different delivery mechanism, closed the same day near
**$26**. The proximate cause was purely mechanical: pandemic demand destruction had left US storage,
and specifically the Cushing, Oklahoma delivery hub for WTI, near physical capacity, so financial
traders who had not closed May contracts before expiry faced a genuine risk of being forced to take
delivery of oil with nowhere to put it. This episode belongs in this record as a coda precisely
**because** it was not a supercycle event at all — it is a single-contract delivery-logistics accident
layered on top of an already-depressed shale-era price regime, the oil-market analogue of case 7's
nickel squeeze below.

**Verdict.** 2014–16 is the direct sequel to case 4's bear market, but with the supply-side mechanism
itself transformed: conventional supply still runs a multi-year lag (as case 4 and, differently, case
5's mining capex both show), but shale's short-cycle economics collapsed oil's own lag to quarters,
meaning every subsequent oil price spike now meets a materially faster supply response than any prior
episode in this record — a structural regime change for atlas row **2.12**'s own oil-cycle input, not
merely a repeat of the 1980s pattern at a faster clock speed.

### 7. The 2020–22 spike and its aftermath — a war-and-logistics shock, not a supercycle start

**Pandemic logistics.** Broad global supply-chain disruption — container freight costs, semiconductor
shortages, factory shutdowns unwinding unevenly across regions — produced a demand-reopening commodity
spike through 2021 that predates, and is distinct in mechanism from, the 2022 war shock that followed.

**Wheat, gas, and nickel — three separate 2022 shocks, three separate mechanisms.** Wheat rose
**~58% year-on-year by March 2022**, some readings citing **80–90%** gains and 15-year highs, as
Russia's invasion (from 24 February 2022) blockaded Ukraine's Black Sea ports — Ukraine being the
world's **ninth-largest wheat producer and fifth-largest exporter** — cutting its exports by **over 90%
in March–May 2022**; Ukraine's own government simultaneously banned wheat, oat, and staple exports to
protect domestic food security. International coordination (the Black Sea Grain Initiative and
associated "Solidarity Lanes") is estimated to have shaved roughly **13 percentage points** off the
price spike, and **wheat prices had returned close to pre-war levels by the end of 2022** — a sharp,
substantially *reversed* shock inside a single calendar year, structurally unlike any supercycle case
in this record. **European gas** underwent a genuine **regime break** rather than a cyclical swing:
TTF hit an all-time high near **€350/MWh (~$100/MMBtu) in August 2022**, with individual spikes to
**€291.5/MWh** on Nord Stream 1 "maintenance" announcements and **€272/MWh** on its indefinite
shutdown; Russian piped gas exports to OECD Europe fell **~50% (~83 bcm) year-on-year in 2022**, their
lowest level since the mid-1980s — a permanent reorientation of European gas sourcing toward LNG, not
a price spike that reverts once the shock passes. **The LME nickel short squeeze, 8 March 2022** —
verified in detail — is the episode's clearest microstructure case study: Tsingshan Holding Group's
Xiang Guangda held roughly **30,000 tonnes short on the LME** plus another **120,000 tonnes short
over-the-counter** with banks including JPMorgan, BNP Paribas, Standard Chartered, and UOB; when nickel
prices **more than doubled**, breaching **$100,000/tonne** and briefly hitting a record
**$101,365/tonne**, the LME took the unprecedented step of **suspending trading** and **retroactively
cancelling roughly 9,000 trades worth ~$4 billion**; Tsingshan's paper losses stood at **~$8 billion**
at the moment of suspension (an estimated **~$15 billion** had the cancelled prices stood), resolved
via a JPMorgan-led rescue credit facility.

**Why the desk should treat 2022 as a supply/war shock, not a supercycle start.** None of the three
2022 episodes shows the atlas's own extraction-capacity-lag mechanism: wheat's shock was a blockade
that substantially reversed within the year; gas's shock was a geopolitical supply cutoff answered by a
demand-side and infrastructure response (European LNG import buildout, demand destruction) inside
12–18 months, not a decade-long underinvestment cycle; and nickel's headline move was a **failed short
squeeze under war-driven volatility on a single exchange**, not evidence of a multi-year nickel
supercycle — a single counterparty's positioning dominated the price for one trading session, the exact
kind of event a state-classification design must not mistake for a capacity-lag state.

**Where prices stand into 2025–26.** The World Bank's Commodity Markets Outlook forecast in October
2025 had prices falling a further **7% in both 2025 and 2026** on weak global growth and a widening
oil surplus; by mid-2026 that forecast reversed sharply — commodity prices are now projected to **rise
16% in 2026**, the first annual increase since 2022 and roughly **25% above** the January-2026
forecast, driven by Middle East supply disruptions in early 2026. Energy is projected **+24%**,
fertilizer **+31%**, metals and minerals **+17%**, precious metals **+42%** to record highs, while
agriculture is projected **–6%**; Brent is expected to average **~$86/barrel in 2026** and **~$70/
barrel in 2027** `[VERIFY: a live, moving forecast as of this writing — treat as a snapshot, not a
settled outcome, and re-check before any downstream use]`.

**Verdict.** 2020–22 is a compound demand-reopening-plus-war-shock episode whose individual legs mostly
reversed (wheat) or structurally rerouted rather than extended (gas) within 12–24 months, with one leg
(nickel) explained almost entirely by exchange microstructure rather than fundamentals — atlas 1.3's
own CONTEXT/sector-conditioner treatment, not a supercycle-length allocation signal, is the correct
read, and the 2025–26 reversal in the World Bank's own forecast direction within a single year is
itself evidence of how far this episode sits from the multi-year persistence the atlas's mechanism
requires.

### 8. Producer-economy mirror: Chile and Australia

**Chile — the structural balance rule as a designed answer to the supercycle's own volatility.**
Chile's **2006 Fiscal Responsibility Law** created two sovereign funds — the **Economic and Social
Stabilization Fund (ESSF)** and the **Pension Reserve Fund (PRF)** — governed by a **structural balance
rule**: government spending is keyed not to the spot copper price but to independent panels'
projections of **long-run copper prices and potential GDP growth**, explicitly excluding cyclical
swings. The two funds together held roughly **$30 billion** `[VERIFY: current combined AUM, a
figure that moves with copper prices and fund performance]`, built up during high-copper years and
drawn down when the cycle turns: in the **2009 financial crisis**, Chile withdrew **$9.28 billion
(6% of GDP)** from the ESSF to fund payroll tax cuts, housing subsidies, and public works — a rule-
based countercyclical fiscal response to a global demand shock, financed entirely by savings
accumulated during the prior boom rather than by new borrowing.

**Australia — the terms-of-trade boom, measured.** From 2003 to 2011, global US-dollar prices for
Australia's resource exports rose **over 300%**. Iron ore's export share rose from **3% (2003) to 17%
(2010)**; coal's from **7% to 15%** over the same window; China's share of global iron-ore imports rose
from **~15% (2000) to a peak of ~70% (2009)**, almost entirely a single-country demand story. By
**2011**, Australia's terms of trade stood roughly **75% above the average of the preceding century** —
the RBA's own mining-boom literature (Connolly & Orsmond 2011; Kearns & Lowe 2011) treats this as one of
the largest terms-of-trade shocks in the country's modern economic history.

**Verdict.** Chile and Australia are the same 2001–2011 China-driven supercycle documented in case 5,
read from the opposite side of the trade — a terms-of-trade **tailwind** rather than headwind, producing
a currency-appreciation and fiscal-windfall problem rather than a CAD/inflation problem. Chile's answer
(a rule-based structural balance, saving the windfall against the eventual downturn) is a genuine design
template — not one this desk's own India-facing, reduce-only mandate can adopt directly, since India sits
on the opposite side of the same trade, but one worth naming because §B3 below shows India experiencing,
almost commodity-for-commodity and year-for-year, the mirror-image stress this case's two producer
economies were spared.

### 9. The financialization episode, 2004–2012

**Tang & Xiong (2012) — the correlation finding.** Ke Tang and Wei Xiong's "Index Investment and the
Financialization of Commodities" (*Financial Analysts Journal* 68(6), 2012; NBER WP 16385) documents
that non-energy commodity futures grew **increasingly correlated with oil prices** as index investment
expanded rapidly from the early 2000s, with the effect **significantly more pronounced** for
commodities included in the two dominant investable indices (S&P GSCI, Dow Jones-UBS Commodity Index)
than for otherwise-similar commodities left off them — evidence the correlation rise was driven by
*index flows themselves*, not by a common macro or supply-side fundamental, and a documented
contributor to the synchronized boom-and-bust of a broad, seemingly unrelated set of commodities across
**2006–2008**.

**The Goldman roll and roll-yield decay.** The monthly **"Goldman roll"** — the predictable rolling of
GSCI futures positions from a near-dated to a further-dated contract — created a well-documented
front-running opportunity: one study measured the available arbitrage at a Sharpe ratio as high as
**4.4 over 2000–2010**, while estimating that price-impact costs from crowding into the same predictable
window cost investors roughly **3.6%/year** in forgone return relative to that theoretical arbitrage.
Roll yield itself is structurally two-sided — backwardated curves reward the roll (sell high, buy low
as the near contract expires), contangoed curves punish it (roll decay) — and the scale of index-driven
flow through this single mechanical window is itself a symptom of the financialization this case
documents.

**Partial post-2015 reversal.** Commodity assets under institutional management fell from roughly
**$150 billion pre-crisis** to **$67 billion by 2015**, as the 2014 price collapse (case 6) drove a
broad institutional retreat; average pairwise commodity correlations have since fallen back **below**
their financialization-era levels, and the literature describes a partial "de-financialization" —
pricing behavior reverting toward the pre-2004 "Keynesian" pattern of commodity-specific, fundamentals-
driven dynamics rather than the synchronized, equity-like co-movement of the 2004–2012 peak.

**Verdict.** The 2004–2012 financialization episode is a genuine but **temporary regime state**, not a
structural, permanent feature of commodity markets: the correlation-with-equities and cross-commodity
co-movement that peaked through 2008–2011 rose and substantially unwound with the institutional capital
that drove it. This is a direct caution against any design choice that treats commodities as a
permanent, equity-correlated asset class rather than a state-dependent one — the correlation itself is
a regime to be read, not a constant to be assumed.

## B3. India — the importer's full record (double length)

**Framing.** Every case in §B2 sets or receives a world price; India, entering the record only here,
receives every one of them as an import bill. This section documents India's own dependence figures,
walks the 2003–2013 boom-to-crisis arc and the 2014–16 windfall and 2021–22 stress-repeat that
followed it in full, surveys the administered-price machinery that has repeatedly stood between world
commodity prices and what an Indian household or firm actually pays, and closes with the equity-side
mirror of the same arcs — because for a net-importer economy, a commodity supercycle state is,
simultaneously, a **macro terms-of-trade** state and a **sector-dispersion** state, the double harvest
atlas row 1.3 itself already names for H53.

### B3.2 The 2003–2013 arc: boom, current-account deterioration, and the 2013 crisis

**The boom years, and their macro cost building underneath.** India's own strongest sustained growth
run coincided almost exactly with the China supercycle's boom leg (case 5) — good for growth, bad for
the trade account, since India was a *demand-side* participant in that boom (rising energy and
industrial-input needs) without a matching resource-export windfall of the kind Australia and Chile
enjoyed (case 8). The **current account deficit widened to a record 4.8% of GDP ($87.8 billion) in
FY2012–13**, up from **4.2% of GDP ($78.2 billion) the prior year**; within that year, the deficit had
briefly spiked as high as **6.7% of GDP** in one quarter before moderating to **3.6%** by the fiscal
year's end. The RBI's own contemporaneous assessment named the mechanism directly: "burgeoning trade
deficit along with significant decline in invisible earnings" — with **soaring crude prices, weak
export performance, and rising gold imports** the three named drivers. Gold's role in the debate had by
this point grown large enough that a former RBI governor, C. Rangarajan, was on record arguing publicly
that "**India can afford gold import of $30 billion a year**" — itself evidence of how central the gold
line item had become to the national CAD conversation by late 2013.

**The August 2013 crisis, precisely dated.** Fed Chair Ben Bernanke's **May 2013** remarks signaling an
eventual taper of quantitative easing triggered a broad emerging-market sell-off; India, running the
CAD documented above, was hit especially hard. The rupee — which had traded near **₹44–45/USD as
recently as 2011** before the CAD-driven depreciation set in — fell **over 15% between May and August
2013 alone**, reaching a then-record low of **₹68.85/USD on 28 August 2013**. This is the single
clearest "commodity-CAD-INR" event in India's post-liberalization history: a global monetary-policy
signal (taper) landing on a currency already structurally weakened by a commodity-driven current-
account gap, producing a currency crisis with no domestic policy trigger of its own.

**The RBI's gold-import curbs — the 80:20 rule.** In **August 2013**, the RBI and the outgoing UPA-II
government jointly introduced the **"80:20" rule**: nominated agencies importing gold were required to
export **20%** of every consignment, directly throttling the CAD's gold-import leg at its source, with
exemptions for SEZ units, EOUs, and Premier/Star Trading Houses holding pre-14-August-2013
authorizations. The rule was **tightened further in February 2014**, then **eased for star/premier
trading houses in May 2014**, before being **scrapped entirely around late November 2014** once the
CAD pressure that justified it had eased (a decision Indian financial press at the time characterized
as "when the govt prevailed over RBI"). **Raghuram Rajan** took charge of the RBI on **4 September
2013** and, on his first day, announced a **special FCNR(B) swap window** offering NRIs and banks a
fixed **3.5% rate over three years** to bring dollars into India — the scheme ultimately mobilized
**~$26 billion**, and the rupee stabilized and began strengthening almost immediately, moving India out
of the contemporaneous "Fragile Five" grouping of vulnerable emerging markets.

### B3.3 The 2014–16 windfall — and who actually captured it

**Oil's collapse (case 6) as a macro gift.** The June-2014-to-January-2016 oil collapse documented in
case 6 (Brent **$112 → $31**) landed on an Indian economy still carrying the CAD scars of 2013,
producing immediate and substantial relief: **WPI turned outright negative**, printing **–2.33%
year-on-year in March 2015** and **–2.36% in May 2015**, with fuel-specific WPI at **–10.41%
year-on-year in May 2015** as LPG, petrol, and diesel prices all fell together.

**The excise-duty capture — the windfall the government kept.** Rather than passing the full oil-price
collapse through to consumers, the central government raised excise duty on petrol and diesel **ten
separate times between November 2014 and January 2016**: petrol duty rose a cumulative **₹11.77/litre**
and diesel **₹13.47/litre** over those fifteen months, against pre-hike baseline duties of just
**₹9.48/litre (petrol)** and **₹3.56/litre (diesel)** in 2014 — the hikes several times over the
starting rate. Excise collections **more than doubled**, from **₹99,000 crore (FY15) to ₹2,42,000
crore (FY17)**. Retail pump prices, by design, saw **"no change in retail selling price"** through
several of these hikes, explicitly "set off against the reduction in retail prices that was warranted"
by the falling crude cost — the government, not the consumer, captured the bulk of the windfall.
**Diesel was fully deregulated in October 2014**, and OMC under-recoveries — described fully below —
were projected to fall from **₹1,39,869 crore (FY14)** toward **₹91,665 crore (FY15)** even before the
excise hikes' full effect, and continued falling as crude stayed low.

**The OMC under-recovery era, in full — the administered-price mechanism the windfall was escaping.**
Under the pre-2014 regime, public-sector oil marketing companies (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL) sold diesel, PDS
kerosene, and domestic LPG at government-controlled prices below cost, with the gap termed an
"under-recovery." This burden peaked at an **all-time high of ₹1,38,541 crore in FY2011–12**, with
OMCs losing roughly **₹399 crore/day** — about **₹7.16/litre on diesel alone**. The government split
this burden three ways with the **upstream** producers: **ONGC, Oil India, and GAIL bore roughly 40%
of total under-recoveries for over 13 years**, a subsidy-sharing arrangement that ended only in
**June 2015** once collapsed crude prices made it unnecessary. ONGC's own cumulative subsidy outgo from
**2003–04** through this period reached **₹216,336 crore**, cutting its cumulative net profit by an
estimated **₹125,477 crore** — a direct, multi-decade example of an upstream, ostensibly market-facing
oil company being used as a fiscal shock absorber for the downstream administered-price system.

**LPG/kerosene subsidy reform.** The **Direct Benefit Transfer for LPG (DBTL)** scheme launched as a
pilot on **1 June 2013**, was paused in early 2014 over implementation issues, relaunched nationally as
**"PAHAL" (Pratyaksh Hanstantrit Labh) from 15 November 2014**, and extended to every district in the
country from **1 January 2015** — consumers now pay the market price for a cylinder and receive the
subsidy directly into their bank account rather than through a suppressed retail price, reaching
roughly **177 million subscribers**. This reform, timed almost exactly with the oil-price collapse
that made it politically and fiscally easier to execute, is the direct institutional legacy of the
under-recovery era documented above.

### B3.4 The 2021–22 stress repeat

The 2021–22 episode reproduced the 2003–2013 arc's macro signature in compressed form. **WPI ran in
double digits for twelve consecutive months from April 2021**, reaching a **record 16.63%
year-on-year in May 2022** (later revised); April 2022 printed **15.08%**, June 2022 **15.18%**, and
even July's **13.93%** and September's **10.7%** remained elevated by any historical standard — the
task's "WPI 15%+ prints" verified precisely across the 2022 peak months. India's crude import bill
**more than doubled**, from **$63.38 billion to $130.22 billion** across the first ten months of
FY2022 alone, with roughly **85%** of oil demand met through imports across this window; the current
account deficit widened toward an estimated **~3% of GDP (~$105 billion) in FY2022** before narrowing
to roughly **2% of GDP in FY2023** `[VERIFY: precise final-print FY2022/FY2023 CAD figures — sources
cite mid-year forecasts and one full-year print; not fully reconciled to a single authoritative
series this session]` as global commodity prices eased from their 2022 peak.

### B3.5 Administered-price mechanisms — the recurring machinery

Three separate administered-price systems have repeatedly stood between world commodity prices and
what Indian consumers actually pay, each documented above or in this closing summary:

1. **OMC under-recoveries (pre-2014)** — described in full at §B3.3: controlled retail prices, a
   government-and-upstream-shared subsidy burden, peaking at **₹1,38,541 crore** in FY2012, ended by
   diesel deregulation (October 2014) and the LPG/kerosene DBT reforms (PAHAL, 2014–15).
2. **Coal India's hybrid notified-price/e-auction system** — the majority of Coal India's output moves
   at **administered "notified" prices**, with a smaller e-auction channel selling at a market-set
   premium over the notified price (recent readings show e-auction premiums running **~40–60% over
   notified price** in individual months `[VERIFY: premium figures are recent-period spot readings, not
   a long-run average]`); the **National Coal Index** blends notified, auction, and import prices into
   a single reference series — a live, ongoing example of a state producer operating a two-tier
   administered/market pricing system rather than a single market-clearing price.
3. **Electricity's fuel-cost passthrough** — power tariffs carry a **Fuel and Power Purchase Cost
   Adjustment (FPPCA)** mechanism, formalized under amended electricity rules from **December 2022**,
   allowing distribution companies to pass coal and other fuel-cost changes to consumers on a monthly
   basis rather than absorbing them — the most recently formalized of the three mechanisms, and, unlike
   the pre-2014 OMC system, explicitly designed as an automatic passthrough rather than a suppressed,
   subsidized price.

**WPI-vs-CPI composition — why the supercycle shows up in one index before the other.** WPI is
commodity-heavy — crude, metals, and fuel carry substantial direct weight — while CPI is food- and
services-heavy with a much smaller direct energy/metals weight and a large weight on items whose
prices adjust with a lag (rents, services, administered items). The result, visible across every arc
this section documents: a global commodity supercycle state shows up in **WPI first and more sharply**
(the 2021–22 double-digit WPI prints against comparatively more moderate CPI prints over the same
window is the clearest recent instance), with the CPI/retail-consumer effect arriving later, more
diluted, and further filtered by exactly the administered-price mechanisms enumerated just above.

### B3.6 The equity side — sector dispersion as the second harvest

**Nifty Metal as the sector-level mirror of every arc above.** The Nifty Metal Index (launched
**12 July 2011**, base-dated to **1 January 2004 = 1,000**) is the cleanest single instrument for
reading India's own metals-sector procyclicality against the global supercycle arcs documented above.
Directionally, its behavior across three of this record's own episodes is unambiguous: it fell
**–73.3% in 2008** — the same GFC air-pocket case 5 documents globally — before rebounding **+232.5%
in 2009**; it gained **+48.4% in 2016** and then fell **–16.8% in 2018** as the mid-cycle metals rally
that followed the 2014–16 collapse's eventual base-metals recovery ran its course; and across the
2020–22 spike (case 7) it returned **+18.0% (2020)**, **+73.4% (2021)**, and **+25.3% (2022)** —
materially outperforming the Nifty 50 in every one of those three years (**16.1%, 25.6%, 5.7%**
respectively) `[VERIFY: 2003–08-era Nifty Metal returns specifically — the index's live base predates
its 2011 launch, so pre-2011 figures are back-computed from the 2004 base rather than live-traded
history; directionally consistent with the China-supercycle boom case 5 documents, but the precise
annual print for those specific years was not independently re-confirmed this session]`.

**OMCs as an inverse-crude trade.** Because retail fuel prices in India have, across every era this
section documents, been either administratively fixed (pre-2014), incompletely passed through (the
2014–16 excise-capture episode), or otherwise sticky relative to the world price, IOCL/BPCL/HPCL stock
performance runs **structurally inverse to crude oil**: a falling crude price improves OMC refining and
marketing margins directly, while a rising crude price — especially one not matched by a retail price
increase — compresses those same margins. This is the equity-market mirror of the CAD/WPI macro
channel documented above: the same oil-price move that stresses the current account simultaneously
squeezes (rising oil) or fattens (falling oil) a specific, identifiable sector's equity margins.

**Tata Steel–Corus and Hindalco–Novelis — producer procyclicality at the top of a cycle.** Both of
India's largest metals M&A deals of the era were struck at, or near, the top of the very cycle case 5
documents. **Tata Steel acquired Corus Group** for **$12.11 billion (£6.2 billion / €8.5 billion)** —
**608 pence per share** — completed in **early 2007**, after a nine-round bidding contest against
Brazil's CSN, becoming the largest overseas acquisition by an Indian company and vaulting Tata Steel to
the world's fifth-largest steelmaker; the price was subsequently and widely criticized as too high, and
the deal's high leverage weighed on Tata Steel's balance sheet through the GFC and beyond. **Hindalco
acquired Novelis** in a deal valuing the target at approximately **$6.0 billion including debt**,
signed **10 February 2007** and completed **15 May 2007** — the same narrow window, the same cycle top.
Both deals illustrate, at the level of individual corporate decisions, exactly the mechanism case 5
documents at the industry level: capital committed at scale precisely as, or just before, the price
cycle that justified the valuation began to turn — India's own metals-sector instance of the
BHP/Rio/Vale capex-lag pattern, expressed through M&A rather than mine-development capex.

**Coal India and ONGC as administered-price/market hybrids.** Both companies sit structurally between
world commodity pricing and Indian administered pricing, and both are documented in detail above:
**Coal India's** notified-price/e-auction split (§B3.5) and **ONGC's** multi-decade role bearing ~40%
of downstream under-recoveries (§B3.3) mean neither stock trades as a clean proxy for its underlying
world commodity price — an investor reading either as a pure coal or crude-oil play, rather than as a
hybrid administered-price instrument whose earnings are partly a function of government policy rather
than the world price alone, would be missing the mechanism that has repeatedly determined each
company's actual reported profitability across every arc this section documents.

### B3.7 The honest India summary

For a net-importer economy, the commodity-supercycle state named in atlas row 1.3 is never a single
signal — it is **two simultaneous states pointing through two different channels**. Through the
**macro channel**, a rising supercycle state is unambiguously a **terms-of-trade headwind**: a wider
CAD, a weaker rupee, a WPI print running ahead of CPI, and — as the 2013 taper tantrum shows most
starkly — a state that can convert a global monetary-policy event into a domestic currency crisis
purely because the CAD was already stretched by the same commodity move. Through the **sector
channel**, the identical supercycle state is a **dispersion driver**: metals and upstream-energy names
(and, in the opposite direction, OMCs and other net importers of the same commodities) diverge sharply
from the broad market, sometimes for years at a stretch, in ways that have nothing to do with the
broad index's own valuation or earnings cycle. Neither channel substitutes for the other, and a design
that reads only one — treating the supercycle purely as a CAD/INR macro conditioner, or purely as a
metals-sector momentum tilt — would be discarding exactly half of what atlas 1.3 names H53 to capture:
the same state, read simultaneously as **macro headwind and sector-dispersion driver**, is the honest,
double harvest this record leaves India with.

## B4. Synthesis

**Read against the desk's own measured chronology, not the literature's label.** A fixed 30–40y framing
is not what 165 years of data actually contain (CS1/CS1b, §B1): the pre-registered, one-sided trough
search finds **eight** troughs since 1870 at **15–21y (median 18y)** spacing — twice the literature's
"3–4 supercycles" count, an artifact of that literature's own two-sided bandpass method. The table below
therefore tests each episode against two claims: the brief's own 30–40y framing (almost always wrong),
and the atlas's/CS1's own 15–20y prior (a closer fit for the episodes that are genuine instances at all).

|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1890s–1910s boom | Demand (electrification) + policy/monetary (WWI finance) | ~20–25y across both legs | A supercycle "due" around 1900–1910 on any fixed clock roughly matches — coincidentally | Two different mechanisms (capacity-lag copper boom, monetary war inflation) layered into one apparent episode; a single-cause reading would be wrong |
| Interwar collapse | Demand collapse (1920–21, 1930s) + first cartel policy responses | ~1919–1933, ~14y | A "40-year-cycle" clock would place the *next* boom near 1940s–50s — roughly right by luck, not mechanism | Restriction cartels (rubber, coffee) are the era's real innovation; both worked short-term, both eventually failed to non-participating supply |

**The pattern across every row.** A 30–40y framing is wrong for essentially every episode here,
including the two (1970s, China) usually cited as its best examples — CS1's own chronology fits both
far better at 15–20y, once the 1970s' "twenty-year glut" is read as two ordinary arcs, not one long one.
The *mechanism* question is separate from the *length* question, and the panel is honest about both at
once: CS2 confirms the *state* — genuine, broad, cross-group co-movement (89% of pairs positive) —
while CS3/CS4, the two mechanism legs (price boom → next-decade capacity; capacity surge → next-decade
price), **fail** their sign-consistency bars across the full metals panel. That is not a contradiction
of cases 4, 5, and 8 above; it is their correct scope — each a real, individually verified instance of
the capacity-lag story, while CS3/CS4 tested whether the story holds as a *systematic, tradeable
regularity* across every metal and decade in the panel, and found it does not. This is the same
conclusion CYCLE_ATLAS.md's own epistemics (§0) reaches for every long cycle in the atlas, now with
this Part's own case record and the desk's own panel test agreeing from two different directions: the
mechanism is a **persistence**, documented case by case, never a **periodicity** or a sized, dated rule —
and treating it as either would have mis-read most of the nine global cases in this record and offered
India, which experiences every episode as a received shock rather than an internally generated one, no
usable timing information at all.
