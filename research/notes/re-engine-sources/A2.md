## 1. Two anchors, and why they disagree

The literature has two tools for "are house prices too high relative to what housing delivers": the **rent-price ratio** and the **user cost of housing**. User cost is, in equilibrium, what the ratio *should* equal, but it adds real rates, property tax, depreciation, a risk premium, and — the term doing most of the work — **expected appreciation**, which is unobservable and has to be proxied. That single assumption can make almost any price level look "justified," which is the core critique below.

## 2. Himmelberg, Mayer & Sinai (2005): the framework, and its own miss

**Himmelberg, C., Mayer, C., Sinai, T. (2005), "Assessing High House Prices: Bubbles, Fundamentals and Misperceptions," *JEP* 19(4):67-92** [1-SOURCE, consistent across NBER/AEA/SSRN listings]. Their user-cost identity: **UC = (1−τ)(i+p) + d + risk premium − expected appreciation** (i = rate, p = property tax, τ = deductibility, d = depreciation) [1-SOURCE]. Applied to 46 US metros, 1980-2004: cost-of-owning rose relative to renting 1995-2004, but in most metros not to overvalued levels [1-SOURCE].

**This is the arc's central disconfirming episode.** Fed staff cited this style of user-cost work as evidence *against* overvaluation in the mid-2000s; prices then fell 30%+ into 2008-2012 [1-SOURCE — a Liberty Street Economics retrospective explicitly frames HMS-style analysis as part of "the failure to forecast the Great Recession"]. Mechanism: the model's appreciation term was fed by the bubble itself, so rising prices mechanically lowered measured user cost — a self-referential loop Verbrugge later quantifies (§6).

## 3. The Gallin arc: no cointegration on income, but a real 3-year rent-price signal

**Gallin (2006), *Real Estate Economics* 34(3):417-438** (FEDS 2003-17): panel-cointegration tests (bootstrap-corrected for cross-city shocks) on 95 metros/23 years **fail to reject no-cointegration between house prices and income** [2-SOURCE, matched across SSRN and Gale summaries] — directly against the folk belief that prices track income long-run.

**Gallin (2008), *Real Estate Economics* 36(4):635-658** (FEDS 2004-50): quarterly error-correction is inconclusive, but a **long-horizon regression shows the rent-price ratio predicts real rent AND real price changes over 3-year (12-quarter) horizons** [1-SOURCE]. Gallin flags that such regressions are **biased when price has a unit root but isn't an exact random walk**, and uses bootstrap distributions instead of standard errors [1-SOURCE] — the housing analogue of the Stambaugh/overlapping-window bias this desk already treats as load-bearing [2-SOURCE, general mechanics corroborated independently via a Fed IFDP note and a ScienceDirect methods paper]. **India read: a 3-year-horizon regression on India's short post-2007 HPI has very few non-overlapping points and needs the same bootstrap treatment, not OLS t-stats.**

On direction of adjustment: Gallin's own 2008 result finds *both* real rents move more and real prices move less than usual when the ratio is low (some rent-side adjustment) [1-SOURCE], while Amsterdam/CDGM evidence below finds price-side adjustment dominates. **This is a genuine unresolved tension, not settled** — price does most of the work in most studies, rent contributes, and the split varies by sample.

## 4. Campbell-Davis-Gallin-Martin (2009): what actually moves the ratio

**CDGM, *Journal of Urban Economics* 66(2):90-102** (FEDS 2006-29 precursor) [2-SOURCE, corroborated across ScienceDirect and the Fed abstract]. Dynamic Gordon-growth decomposition of the log rent-price ratio into expected real rent growth, expected real rates, and a time-varying "housing premium," 23 metros + regions + nation, 1975-2007. **Headline: expected rent growth explains only a small share of ratio variance; real rates and the premium explain most of it** [2-SOURCE]. In the 1997-2007 boom, **65% of the aggregate ratio's decline is attributed to a falling housing premium**, not rate declines or rent optimism [1-SOURCE]. A regime break near 1997: pre-1997, expected rates and the premium were strongly negatively correlated (canceling out); post-1997 they turned positively correlated, so declines compounded rather than offset [1-SOURCE]. Their own caution: **models focused only on rates and ignoring these covariances mislead** [1-SOURCE].

## 5. Half-life of deviations — an honest gap, not a clean number

The brief asks for a documented half-life. **No single reliable cross-study number survived search — report the gap rather than invent a figure.** Capozza, Hendershott, Mack & Mayer (NBER WP 9262 / *REE* 2004) estimate mean-reversion on 62 US metros, 1979-1995, and find reversion speed **varies with city size, income/population growth, and construction costs** — faster in large, fast-growing, low-cost metros; worst overshooting in high-construction-cost coastal metros (Boston, NY, SF, LA, San Diego named) [1-SOURCE]. Search could not retrieve a specific numeric half-life with confidence. [RECALL — unverified: this desk's general memory is a roughly 3-6 year half-life for the median US metro from CHM-style estimates — NOT confirmed, do not cite as fact.]

The one concrete duration claim that did survive: **Ambrose, Eichholtz & Lindenthal (2013), "House Prices and Fundamentals: 355 Years of Evidence," *JMCB* 45(2-3):477-491** (Amsterdam 1650-2005; note the brief's "350 years" — the actual title says **355**) [2-SOURCE, corroborated across Wiley/SSRN/Penn State]. Correction occurs mainly through prices, not rents, and **"can take decades"** [2-SOURCE]. Consistent with Shanghai below (ratio stuck at extreme levels for years) and with this desk's own CN Programme conclusion the same day: structure is knowable, timing is not.

## 6. Verbrugge: why user cost and rent diverge — and how fragile that divergence is

**Verbrugge (2008), "The Puzzling Divergence of Rents and User Costs, 1980-2004," *Review of Income and Wealth*** [1-SOURCE]. In levels, for the median structure in the five largest US cities, **rents and ex-ante user costs diverge markedly and for years, sometimes with user cost well above rent** [1-SOURCE]. Critically: **divergence size is extremely sensitive to the appreciation proxy** — a forecast of actual future appreciation produces large divergence; substituting expected CPI inflation (zero real gains) or a long-horizon annualized forecast **collapses most of it** [1-SOURCE]. The user-cost verdict is largely a statement about which appreciation assumption was chosen — exactly the vulnerability behind HMS's benign 2004 read.

**Sommer, Sullivan, Verbrugge (2013), *Journal of Monetary Economics* 60(7):854-870** [1-SOURCE]: a full tenure-choice general-equilibrium model with both prices and rents endogenous. Calibration: **lower rates, relaxed lending standards, and higher incomes explain roughly half the 1995-2006 US price-rent-ratio rise** [1-SOURCE] — implying the other half is an unexplained premium, consistent with CDGM.

## 7. Commercial real estate: cap rates work, except for offices

**Plazzi, Torous, Valkanov (2010), *Review of Financial Studies* 23(9):3469-3519** [2-SOURCE, corroborated across SSRN and a second search]. Cross-sectional US metro transactions: cap rates forecast **up to ~30% of realized-return variance for apartments, retail, and industrial** — but **NOT for offices in-sample**, even though office expected returns are shown time-varying by other means [1-SOURCE]. Decomposing the cap rate: **~30% from local fundamentals + rent growth; a larger, significant "orthogonal" share unrelated to fundamentals** — a genuine unexplained risk-premium/sentiment component [1-SOURCE]. Cap rates marginally forecast **office** rent growth, but not apartment/retail/industrial rent growth [1-SOURCE]. **The office asymmetry is itself the finding** — do not assume "the cap rate works" uniformly across property types; relevant directly to India's office-heavy REIT sector.

## 8. Other evidence: London, Ireland 2006, Shanghai's unresolved extreme

**Bracke (2015), *Real Estate Economics* 43(2):403-431** (matched sale/rental data, Central London): rent-price ratios differ systematically by unit size and neighbourhood — **not one number even within one city** [1-SOURCE]. A Bank Underground note: Prime London prices **+180% since 2005 vs rents +40%**, yields compressed to 3-4% [1-SOURCE]. A separate LSE blog post (not Bracke) attributes **53% of London's 1997-2018 153% ratio rise to persistent demand meeting constrained supply** [1-SOURCE, distinct source].

**Ireland, 2006**: one synthesis (drawing on Morgan Kelly's work) reports **actual yield ~4% vs an ~8% equilibrium estimate** [1-SOURCE, unverified against Kelly's primary text], with the Irish central bank on record (Nov 2005) estimating **20-60% overvaluation** [1-SOURCE]. Consistent directionally with this desk's own CN-programme JST work: Ireland 1999 is the fastest post-1970 real boom (+17.25%/yr), Ireland 2006 the worst modern bust (-55.7%).

**Shanghai**: community-level price-rent ratio **78-79 raw in 2016Q4/2017Q1** (67-70 hedonic-adjusted) [1-SOURCE], with **>90% of communities at ratio 50-80 in 2018** — implying gross yields of roughly **1.3-2%**, high even against expensive global comparators cited at 30-50 (2-3.3%) [1-SOURCE]. A live emerging-market analogue: extreme, sustained compression with no forced resolution date.

## 9. Imputed rent: measurement, and what breaks where the rental market is thin

Two methods: **comparison/rental-equivalence** (match rents of comparable tenant units) and **user-cost-of-capital** (impute a notional return) [1-SOURCE]. US BLS uses rental equivalence for OER, surveying a rotating panel every six months [1-SOURCE]. **The method assumes a thick, liquid, comparable rental market — true-ish in the US (~35% rental share), badly strained in India.**

What breaks for India specifically:
- **Homeownership is very high, rental stock thin:** Census 2011: **~87% own nationally, ~95% rural, ~69% urban** [2-SOURCE, corroborated by a separate 2015-2023 series showing ~86.6% stable]. The rented urban minority supplying "comparable rent" is small and likely non-representative (migrants, younger households, specific unit types) — a textbook selection-bias problem.
- **The rental market itself is mostly informal:** NSSO 2012 found **71% of rentals have no written contract**; NSSO 2008-09 found only **~5% used formal agreements**, of which only ~a quarter were registered [2-SOURCE, two independent NSSO rounds converging]. Some states run >90% informal (Andhra Pradesh, Telangana ~94%; Bihar ~92%) [1-SOURCE].
- **The 11-month lease is a deliberate registration/rent-control workaround**, not a market preference — avoids mandatory registration (1.5-5% of annual rent) and older Rent Control Act triggers [2-SOURCE] — meaning recorded rent data understates typical tenancy and carries a registration-avoidance selection effect with no US analogue.
- **Rent control produced a documented "vacancy paradox":** ~11.1 million vacant urban units in 2011, **~12.4% of urban stock** (up from 1.83m in 1971), reaching **~19% in Gujarat, ~16% in Maharashtra** — landlords under weak enforcement prefer empty units to formal renting [2-SOURCE, CSEP/USC plus independent ORF/Scroll coverage of the same Census figures]. A rental-equivalence index built on *occupied* rental stock misses this supply distortion entirely.
- **The Model Tenancy Act (2021)** targets exactly this (2-month deposit cap, mandatory digital registration, annual rent-revision limits, a 3-tier dispute system) [1-SOURCE] but **adoption is state-by-state and partial** (Maharashtra, Karnataka cited as early adopters as of 2025) [1-SOURCE].
- **India's own CPI housing treatment is mid-reform:** intended as rental-equivalence [1-SOURCE], but MOSPI's 2025-26 reform moves from bi-annual/urban-only to monthly/rural-and-urban collection, drawing for the first time on HCES 2023-24 rural rent and imputed-rent data — implying materially thinner prior rural coverage [1-SOURCE]. Housing carries **21.67% weight in urban CPI, 10.07% all-India combined** [1-SOURCE].

## 10. India's valuation-anchor data landscape today

**Price side:** RBI's HPI (quarterly, registration-based, rebased to 18 cities/2022-23) and NHB RESIDEX (since 2007; now spans HPI/Land/Building-Materials/a Housing Rental Index, though no granular HRI print surfaced) [1-SOURCE each]. Classified by carpet-area band (≤60/60-110/>110 sqm) [1-SOURCE].

**Yield side, portal-based not survey-based:** aggregator "gross yield" figures cluster **~4.4-5.2%** recently (varying by source/city: Bengaluru ~4.45%, Chennai ~4.16%, Ahmedabad ~3.9%) [1-SOURCE, internally inconsistent across portals]. **This conflicts with older, still-repeated claims of "2-3% net, lowest in the world"** [1-SOURCE, unverified superlative] — a claim also contradicted by global data showing **Taiwan ~1.9-2.3% and Singapore ~3.1%** lower than most cited India figures [1-SOURCE]. Treat as unresolved: India is low by global standards, but not verifiably lowest, and portal numbers (asking-price, pre-cost) likely run above true net yields.

**Property-tax input:** three municipal systems — ARV (UP cities, Punjab, Chennai), Unit Area (Delhi, Bengaluru, Kolkata), Capital Value (Mumbai/Pune) [1-SOURCE]. A widely-repeated **"5-20% of property value"** figure is a conflation — that rate applies to *Annual Rental Value*, not capital value. A Pune CVS worked example implies an **effective ~0.2% of market value** [1-SOURCE, single illustrative example, not a national average]. **Best read: effective India property tax as % of capital value is likely sub-1% in most cities**, closer to the US ~0.9-1.1% norm [1-SOURCE] than the 5-20% figure suggests — exactly the kind of error to catch before it enters a model.

**Transaction integrity:** circle-rate-vs-market-value gaps have narrowed sharply in the **primary/developer** segment (from >100% in 2015 to as low as ~6%), but the **resale segment remains where under-reported/black-money transactions concentrate** [1-SOURCE] — primary and resale series should not be pooled without a stated correction.

**Area basis:** carpet-to-super-built-up loading is **~25-35% typically, 40-50% in Mumbai** [1-SOURCE] — squarely inside, and at the top exceeding, the brief's flagged 20-35% range.

**Commercial/REIT:** India REITs (Embassy, Mindspace, Brookfield India, Nexus) cited at **~7.5% distribution yield** [1-SOURCE, soft-sourced, and distribution yield ≠ cap rate]; **no residential REIT exists in India**, so Plazzi-Torous-Valkanov-style testing has no residential listed proxy here.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Never build one national India price-rent ratio.** Bracke shows the ratio varies by unit size and locality even within one city; India's heterogeneity (plus carpet/built-up inconsistency) is at least as large. Build city-and-segment level, with area basis stated every time.
2. **Prefer the rent-price ratio over a full user-cost model as the primary India signal.** User cost's weak point (HMS's 2004 miss; Verbrugge's demonstration that the appreciation assumption alone can erase the "puzzle") is worse in India, where reliable real-rate, depreciation, and appreciation-expectation inputs barely exist.
3. **Treat every India rent series (RESIDEX HRI, CPI housing) as thin and self-selected**, not US-grade OER — ~69% urban homeownership and ~70%+ informal tenancy mean observed rents are not a random draw from the stock being valued.
4. **Do not build entry/exit timing on an implied half-life.** Amsterdam shows price-driven correction "can take decades"; Shanghai has sat at extreme compression for years unresolved. A valuation signal here is a state, not a clock — consistent with this desk's existing Tier-C discipline.
5. **Register every India rent/tax data break before using it**, same discipline as BR7-9: Model Tenancy Act adoption is state-by-state and partial; MOSPI's CPI-housing methodology is itself mid-overhaul (effective ~Feb 2026). No blended pre/post series without a marker.
6. **Correct the "5-20% of property value" property-tax figure before it enters any formula** — it is an ARV-based rate, not a capital-value rate; effective India property tax is more plausibly sub-1% of market value in most cities.
7. **Do not adopt "India has the world's lowest rental yields" without a footnote** — contradicted by Taiwan/Hong Kong/Singapore data, and India's own portal-based (~4.4-5.2%) vs older net-yield (~2-3%) claims disagree by over a full point.
8. **If the engine ever extends to commercial/REIT pricing, do not port the residential price-rent playbook** — offices are the one documented property type where cap-rate return-predictability fails (Plazzi-Torous-Valkanov).
