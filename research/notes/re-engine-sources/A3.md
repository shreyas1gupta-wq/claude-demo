# A3 — Credit, mortgage supply and the leverage channel

*As of 2026-09-11. Compiled via WebSearch only (WebFetch is egress-blocked in this environment); every claim is tagged [2-SOURCE], [1-SOURCE], or [RECALL — unverified].*

## 1. The Mian-Sufi program — elastic mortgage supply as the US mechanism

**Mian & Sufi, "The Consequences of Mortgage Credit Expansion," *QJE* 124(4), 2009.** 2002–2005 saw a securitization-driven shift in mortgage supply toward zip codes with high subprime (sub-660 credit score) share; credit flowed fastest to zip codes with *slower* income growth — the signature of supply-driven, not demand-driven, expansion. [2-SOURCE mechanism; exact coefficients not retrieved — RECALL for magnitudes]

**Mian & Sufi, "House Prices, Home Equity-Based Borrowing, and the U.S. Household Leverage Crisis," *AER* 101(5), 2011** (NBER WP 15283). Using Saiz land-topology supply-elasticity as an instrument for house-price growth: homeowners extracted **25–30 cents per dollar** of home-equity gain via cash-out refi/HELOCs; home-equity borrowing added **$1.25 trillion** to household debt 2002–08 and explains **≥39% of new defaults 2006–08**. [2-SOURCE]

**Mian, Sufi & Matvos, "Credit Supply and Housing Speculation," *Review of Financial Studies* 35(2), 2022** (NBER WP 24823, 2018). The 2003 private-label-securitization (PLS) surge, funded by non-core-deposit lenders, drove a speculative-volume channel distinct from owner-occupier demand: PLS-exposed zip codes saw transaction-volume surges driven almost entirely by "flippers" — **<1% of the adult population** in 2005–06 — with amplified boom-and-bust. The credit-supply shift toward high-subprime zip codes appears even in the most **elastic**-supply MSAs (no price growth), but is *stronger* where supply is inelastic — elasticity moderates, not triggers. [2-SOURCE]

**Mian, Rao & Sufi, "Household Balance Sheets, Consumption, and the Economic Slump," *QJE* 128(4), 2013.** The net-worth companion result: consumption elasticity to housing net worth **0.6–0.8**; marginal propensity to consume out of housing wealth **5–7 cents/$**, higher in poorer/more-levered zip codes, which also saw larger credit-limit and refinancing cuts — leverage amplifies transmission on the way up *and* down. [2-SOURCE]

**Mian & Sufi, *House of Debt*, University of Chicago Press, 2014, 192pp.** Book synthesis: household (not bank) balance-sheet leverage, an inelastic mortgage-supply response, and asymmetric debt contracts (borrower bears full downside) are argued as the central 2007–09 mechanism; policy prescription is equity-like "shared responsibility" mortgages. [2-SOURCE]

## 2. Jordà-Schularick-Taylor — credit is what makes a bubble dangerous

**"Betting the House," *Journal of International Economics* 96(S1), 2015** (NBER WP 20771). 140 years, advanced economies; uses the trilemma (fixed-exchange-rate countries inherit foreign rate shocks) as an instrument. Exogenously loose rates produce real-estate-lending and house-price booms, both independently raising crisis risk, with the effect **stronger postwar** than pre-WWII. [2-SOURCE mechanism; exact point-elasticities/IRF horizon not retrieved — RECALL]

**"Leveraged Bubbles," *Journal of Monetary Economics* 76(S), 2015** (NBER WP 21486). 17 countries, 140 years. Central finding: equity bubbles *without* a credit boom have **"virtually no effect"** on recession depth/recovery speed. An equity bubble *with* a credit boom adds **one extra year** of recession and **~3 percentage points** of cumulative GDP-per-capita drag after five years. Adding credit growth to a baseline crisis-classification model raises **AUC from 0.61 to 0.71** [1-SOURCE, provisional]. Housing bubbles rank more dangerous than equity bubbles, and the gap widens further when credit-fueled — but **no exact "X% deeper recession" figure specific to credit-fueled housing bubbles** (vs. the general equity+credit figure above) was retrieved — flag as **RECALL** and treat as the single highest-priority primary-source chase from this dossier.

**Schularick & Taylor, "Credit Booms Gone Bust," *AER* 102(2), 2012** (NBER WP 15512; replicated by Summers, *J. Applied Econometrics*, 2017). 14 countries, 1870–2008: a credit boom over the prior five years robustly predicts crisis risk; credit/GDP decoupling itself signals rising risk. A downstream citation reports baseline **AUROC 0.717** [1-SOURCE]. [2-SOURCE mechanism]

## 3. Identified instruments: does credit cause prices?

**Favara & Imbs, "Credit Supply and the Price of Housing," *AER* 105(3), 2015.** US intrastate branching deregulation (1994–2005) instruments local credit supply; deregulated banks expand credit (verified against a placebo, ruling out demand stories), raising prices **less** in elastic-supply metros (stock absorbs it) and more where supply is topographically inelastic. Reported: house-price-to-loan-volume elasticity **0.134** over one year; the channel **"explains up to one half"** of 1994–2005 price growth [1-SOURCE, both figures via secondary citation]. [2-SOURCE mechanism]

**Di Maggio & Kermani, "Credit-Induced Boom and Bust," *Review of Financial Studies* 30(11), 2017.** 2004 federal preemption of national banks from state anti-predatory-lending law as a credit-supply shock: **11% rise** in annual lending 2004–06 → **3.3%** rise in annual house-price growth and **2.2%** nontradable-employment expansion in exposed areas, reversing afterward; the same expansion **cut delinquencies in the boom, raised them in the bust**. [2-SOURCE]

**Adelino, Schoar & Severino, "House Prices, Collateral, and Self-Employment," *JFE* 117(2), 2015** (NBER WP 18868). Conforming-loan-limit/80%-LTV discontinuity as instrument: newly 80%-LTV-eligible houses gain **~$1.17/sq ft** versus otherwise-similar ineligible ones; the resulting collateral-lending channel (home equity → small-business borrowing → hiring) explains **15–25%** of local small-business employment variation in the boom. [2-SOURCE] Companion identification paper (NBER WP 17832, ~10 metro areas straddling the CLL) implies a **house-price/interest-rate elasticity below 6** — smaller than aggregate time-series estimates. [1-SOURCE]

## 4. Mortgage-market structure: which constraint binds, does credit reach price?

**Greenwald, "The Mortgage Credit Channel of Macroeconomic Transmission"** (MIT job-market paper, widely mirrored/updated). Distinguishes an **LTV** (collateral) from a **PTI/DTI** (cash-flow) constraint that move in *opposite* directions on a rate shock (lower payments loosen PTI; higher resulting prices tighten LTV). PTI elasticity to rates **~8**; LTV largely insensitive except via prepayment. PTI liberalization alone explains **42%** of the price-rent-ratio rise and **51%** of the debt/income rise in the US boom. [2-SOURCE]

**Greenwald & Guren, "Do Credit Conditions Move House Prices?", *AER*, Oct 2025** (NBER WP 29391, 2021). Resolves the "credit has no effect" vs. "credit drives the cycle" split via rental-market **segmentation**: US rental markets are **highly frictional, near fully segmented** (price-rent ratio responds ≥4× more than homeownership to an identified credit shock), implying credit-standard changes explain **34–55%** of the price-rent-ratio rise over the 2000s boom. [2-SOURCE]

## 5. Cross-country macroprudential surveys

**Kuttner & Shim, BIS WP 433 (2013) / NBER WP 19723 / *Journal of Financial Stability*, 2016.** 57 economies, 30+ years, nine policy tools. Housing-*credit* growth responds to DSTI, LTV, exposure limits and housing taxes in conventional panels, but under stricter mean-group/event-study methods **only DSTI limits remain significant**; on house *prices*, **only transaction-tax changes** show a robust effect. DSTI tightening: **4–7 percentage-point** deceleration in credit growth over the following year. [2-SOURCE mechanism / 1-SOURCE exact range]

**Cerutti, Claessens & Laeven, IMF WP/15/61 (2015) / *Journal of Financial Stability* 28, 2017.** 119 countries, 2000–13. Emerging economies use macroprudential tools (esp. FX-related) most; advanced economies favor borrower-based tools. Borrower-based caps (LTV/DTI) associated with **~1.5pp** lower annual household credit growth and **~1.2pp** lower real house-price growth; weaker where markets are more open/developed, partly offset by cross-border-borrowing leakage. [2-SOURCE usage / 1-SOURCE magnitudes]

**Duca, Muellbauer & Murphy** — "House Prices and Credit Constraints," *Economic Journal* 121(552), 2011 (Dallas Fed WP 1103): standard US house-price models **break down mid-2000s** absent an explicit credit-supply proxy; adding a cyclically-adjusted first-time-buyer LTV ratio restores stable long-run fit. Mature statement: **"What Drives House Price Cycles?", *Journal of Economic Literature* 59(3), 2021, pp. 773–864** — credit-availability measures are a necessary state variable in any cross-country house-price model, not optional. (Note: no 2016 JEL version of this survey exists — a 2016 *AER P&P* piece by the same authors, "How Mortgage Finance Reform Could Affect Housing," is a distinct, shorter piece.) [2-SOURCE]

## 6. Country natural experiments — prices and volumes

**Korea** (LTV/DTI tightenings since 2002): DTI limits more effective than LTV at stabilizing price growth; loosening either boosts growth, only DTI tightening reliably restrains it (BOK WP 1610; *J. Financial Stability* 31, 2017). [1-SOURCE, single research thread]

**Hong Kong** (HKMA LTV caps since 1991, intensified post-2009): caps are countercyclical/forward-looking; a cap cut is followed by declining high-end transaction-volume growth after **~1 year** and total volume growth after **~1.5–2 years** — volume moves before price is separable. LTV policy also cuts default-risk sensitivity to price shocks; mortgage insurance doesn't undermine this. Cross-country regressions confirm LTV caps generally decelerate price growth; both LTV/DTI slow lending growth. [2-SOURCE mechanism; no single HK price-percent figure retrieved — RECALL for magnitude]

**New Zealand** (RBNZ LVR restrictions, Oct 2013): RBNZ's own stated expectation was **1–2pp** lower house-price inflation. Reintroduction in 2021 had **"little impact"** — an explicit disconfirming result on repeat application. Main achieved benefit framed as financial-system resilience, not price control; 2026 saw easing given contained risk. [2-SOURCE, primary RBNZ sources]

**Israel** (Bank of Israel LTV-differentiated caps, Oct 2012: investors ≤50%, upgraders ≤70%, first-timers ≤75%; earlier 2010 risk-weight measure). The risk-weight measure's identified effect: prices on units likely financed with newly-higher-risk-weighted mortgages fell **~2.5pp**, larger in expensive areas and lower-quality neighborhoods within them; post-policy, borrowers with LTV just above the 60% threshold paid **0.31–0.36pp** more interest than otherwise-identical borrowers just below it. [2-SOURCE]

**Sweden** (FI: 2010 LTV cap; 2016 1%/yr amortization above 50% LTV; 2018 tightened for DTI>4.5×income): the **2010 LTV cap alone had no measurable price effect**; the **2016 amortization rule** is associated with **~−7%** on prices, 2018 tightening adding a smaller effect — a payment/cash-flow tool working where a pure collateral tool did not. [1-SOURCE, magnitude not independently cross-checked]

**Canada** (OSFI B-20 stress test: 2016 insured, 2018 extended to uninsured): Bank of Canada (2024) finds stress tests **improve credit quality, reduce credit and house-price growth**, and improved 2022–23 rate-shock resilience; mechanically **cuts maximum qualifying mortgage ~20–25%** vs. qualifying at the contract rate. [2-SOURCE]

## 7. Lead-lag and the false-alarm problem — vs. the desk's 87.2% JST print

The desk's own JST-panel finding (CN programme): mortgage-credit growth lifts 3-year crash odds **1.72×** with an **87.2% false-alarm rate**. The general EWI literature uses **noise-to-signal ratios / AUC**, not a single "false-alarm %," so a literal comparison needs care.

**Borio-Drehmann convention, carried through Drehmann & Juselius, *Int'l J. Forecasting* 30(3), 2014 (BIS WP 421), and Aldasoro, Borio & Drehmann, BIS Quarterly Review, March 2018**: thresholds are chosen to **minimize noise-to-signal subject to catching ≥two-thirds of crises** — i.e., the convention *builds in* heavy false-alarm tolerance as the price of not missing crises, directionally consistent with (not contradicting) the desk's 87.2%. Thresholds: credit-to-GDP gap **2–6pp**, real property-price gap **15–25%**. Credit-gap and debt-service-ratio (DSR) "consistently outperform" other indicators; **DSR dominates short horizons, credit gap dominates long horizons**. [2-SOURCE mechanism; the exact noise-to-signal % — the number directly comparable to 87.2% — was **not retrievable** from snippets: RECALL, chase the primary BIS tables before any numeric comparison is published.]

JST's own discrimination numbers (Section 2: AUC 0.61→0.71; AUROC 0.717) sit in the **modest-to-fair** classifier range — again directionally consistent with a high tolerated false-alarm rate at any crisis-catching threshold, though not the same statistic as 87.2% and not to be equated numerically.

**Timing**: credit expansion consistently **precedes** the sharpest price acceleration (2003 US PLS surge before 2004–06 price acceleration; Di Maggio-Kermani's 2004 shock followed by acceleration in the same 2004–06 window, reversing after). No source gave a universal "N quarters" lead — **RECALL** for any generic lag figure absent a specific paper's own reported structure.

## 8. India-specific evidence

**RBI LTV/risk-weight schedule**: priority-sector housing loans ≤₹20 lakh may carry LTV up to **90%**; for ≤₹30 lakh loans, risk weight **35%** at LTV≤80%, **50%** at 80–90%; for ₹30–75 lakh, **35%** at LTV≤75% (50% at 75–80%). [2-SOURCE] Priority-sector eligibility (post April-2025 broadening) is population-tiered: **₹50 lakh** (cost cap ₹63 lakh) in cities >50 lakh pop.; **₹45 lakh** (cap ₹57 lakh) in 10–50 lakh; **₹35 lakh** (cap ₹44 lakh) below. [1-SOURCE]

**Bhupal Singh, "House Prices and Macroprudential Policies: Evidence from City-Level Data in India," IMF WP/20/291, 2020** (+ companion, *Economic Systems*, 2023). Using **NHB RESIDEX** city data: LTV, risk weights and provisioning all detectably dampen Indian city prices atop strong fundamentals. Crucially, the **LTV effect is size-differentiated**: elasticity **0.40–0.59 for small-ticket** mortgages vs. **1.10–1.14 for large-ticket** — India's LTV lever is disproportionately a large-mortgage/premium-segment tool, not broad-based. Risk-weight tightening shows a significant downward price effect. [2-SOURCE, same paper via two independent hosting mirrors]

**NHB RESIDEX methodology** (NHB white paper): Laspeyres index, four-quarter weighted moving average, dynamic category weights; classified by **carpet-area bands** (≤60 sq.m., 60–110, >110); source data is **bank/HFC loan-valuation data** (Assessment Price) and **primary/secondary transaction data** (Market Price, under-construction). NHB's own cleaning process explicitly **converts super-built-up/built-up area to carpet area** — confirming the area-basis problem is a live, first-order measurement issue NHB itself manages, not a hypothetical caveat. Coverage: **50 cities, 21 states**. [2-SOURCE]

**India mortgage-to-GDP**: risen from **~3.4% (2001)/6.6% (FY12) to ~11–12.5% (FY24/25)** [2-SOURCE, ±1pp across sources] vs. cited comparators US ~51–76%, UK ~64–82%, Denmark ~74%, China ~28% [1-SOURCE, comparator figures not cross-checked]. India's mortgage market is structurally far less penetrated than any major comparator.

**RBI FSR household leverage**: household debt/GDP **26% (2015) → ~42% (end-2024) → 41.3% (Mar 2025, 5yr avg 38.3%) → 45.5% (Sep 2025)**, one source citing **47.8% (Dec 2025)** [2-SOURCE trend / 1-SOURCE latest point]. Composition (Sep 2025): **non-housing retail 55.3%, housing 28.6%, agri/business 16.1%** — mortgage debt is a *minority* of India's small household-leverage stock, unlike the US where it dominates. [1-SOURCE] Average debt/person: **₹3.9 lakh (2023) → ₹4.8 lakh (Mar 2025)**, +23% in two years, ~2× income growth. [1-SOURCE]

**The 2018–19 NBFC/HFC shock — India's own credit-supply-contraction natural experiment.** NBFC/HFC share of developer credit rose **36% (FY12) → 58% (FY18)**, ~₹4 trillion [1-SOURCE]. The **September 2018 IL&FS default** (with ~$34bn of related MF debt maturing Oct 2018–Mar 2019) triggered a squeeze: NBFC/HFC lending to developers fell **~48% to ~₹27,000 crore in FY19**. [2-SOURCE]. This mirrors Favara-Imbs/Di Maggio-Kermani mechanics in reverse, but hits **developer/construction finance**, not household mortgages — a structurally different channel from the demand-side US/European canon.

**RERA's 70% escrow rule** (2016 Act, state rollout from 2017): mandates 70% of buyer collections into project-specific escrow, bans pre-launch sales; reported to cut developers' financeable share from buyer advances from **~50% to 20–30%**, forcing more external borrowing per project. [1-SOURCE] Post-2017, developer/construction-finance conditions are a more binding India supply-side credit constraint than in the pre-RERA era.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Do not import US/European credit-elasticity magnitudes wholesale.** Favara-Imbs (0.134), Greenwald-Guren (34–55%) and JST are calibrated to mature, 51–76%-mortgage-to-GDP markets; India sits at 11–12.5%. Treat India as structurally under-leveraged with rising, not saturated, credit elasticity — use Bhupal Singh's India-specific LTV elasticities (0.40–0.59 / 1.10–1.14) as the only directly-applicable numbers found here.

2. **Segment any India credit-channel model by loan ticket size, not just city.** Bhupal Singh shows LTV changes hit large-ticket mortgages ~2–3× harder than small-ticket — the India credit channel today is disproportionately a premium-segment phenomenon, the segment-identity mirror of Mian-Sufi's subprime-zip-code US story.

3. **Model developer/construction-finance credit (NBFC/HFC) as a distinct, currently more India-relevant channel than household mortgage credit.** The 2018–19 IL&FS shock (48% collapse in developer lending) and RERA's escrow-driven rise in external-financing dependence argue for an NBFC/HFC disbursement-growth indicator as a **leading indicator for new supply/launch volumes**, separate from any mortgage-rate variable.

4. **Every India house-price series needs an explicit area-basis flag.** NHB RESIDEX itself converts super-built-up/built-up to carpet area during cleaning. Use only carpet-normalized series, or build an explicit basis-conversion layer before ingesting developer/broker data (typically quoted super-built-up, inflating "price" 20–35%).

5. **Prefer DSTI/PTI (cash-flow) framing over LTV (collateral) alone.** Greenwald (PTI explains the bulk of the US boom), Sweden (amortization moved price ~7%, the earlier LTV cap alone did not) and Kuttner-Shim (DSTI dominates LTV/DTI cross-country) all point the same way — India's household DSR/EMI-to-income (from RBI FSR data, split by loan type given the 55%-non-housing composition) is likely a better leading indicator than the LTV level alone.

6. **Treat "credit growth predicts a crash" as high-recall/low-precision, per the desk's own JST print.** The EWI literature's own two-thirds-hit-rate design choice tolerates a high noise-to-signal ratio by construction — consistent with, not contradictory to, 87.2%. Follow the DSR/credit-gap combined-indicator approach; report as a state/probability shift, never a point call.

7. **Watch RBI risk-weight/provisioning changes, not just LTV or repo-rate moves, as regime-shift dates**, and expect transaction-volume data to turn before price data confirms it (Hong Kong: ~1yr high-end volume, ~1.5–2yr total volume) — payment/risk-weight tools showed more consistent effects than pure LTV caps across Korea, Hong Kong, Sweden.

8. **Open primary-source task**: pull the exact NBER WP 21486 ("Leveraged Bubbles") table for the credit-fueled-*housing*-bubble-specific recession-depth number (distinct from the general equity+credit 3pp/5yr figure sourced here) before sizing India-engine downside risk on it.
