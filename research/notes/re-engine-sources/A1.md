# A1 — House-price predictability: the core empirical record

*As of 2026-09-11. Compiled via WebSearch only (WebFetch is egress-blocked in this environment); every claim below is tagged [2-SOURCE], [1-SOURCE], or [RECALL — unverified].*

## 1. The starting fact: momentum at 1–4 quarters, reversion only at long horizons

**Case & Shiller (1989), "The Efficiency of the Market for Single-Family Homes," *American Economic Review* 79(1), pp. 125–137** (also NBER WP 2506, 1988). Repeat-sales prices on 39,210 individual homes in Atlanta, Chicago, Dallas, and San Francisco/Oakland, 1970–1986. Finding: year-to-year changes in city-level real house-price indexes are positively autocorrelated — an annual change is followed the next year by a change in the same direction, roughly one-quarter to one-half as large — and real interest rates are not incorporated into prices. The paper's headline conclusion is that the market is **not weak-form efficient**. [2-SOURCE — corroborated across NBER, AEA/EconPapers, and multiple secondary reviews]

Critically: **this is an in-sample regression test of serial correlation / weak-form efficiency, not a genuine out-of-sample forecast.** At the individual-home level the R² is reported as **never more than 0.04** — lagged price changes explain at most 4% of individual-home price variance, even though the same serial correlation is highly significant at the city-index level. [1-SOURCE — HUD "Recent House Price Trends" review]. This gap (significant at the index level, weak at the unit level, never a real forecast test) recurs through the literature below and is the single most important caveat for the India engine.

**Case & Shiller (1990), "Forecasting Prices and Excess Returns in the Housing Market," *AREUEA Journal* (now *Real Estate Economics*) 18(3), pp. 253–273** (NBER WP 3368). Same four-city repeat-sales data, 1970Q1–1987Q3. Extends the 1989 paper: construction-cost-to-price ratio, change in real per-capita income, and change in adult population are all shown to have (in-sample) predictive power for next-year price changes and for excess returns of housing over debt instruments. Confirms the one-year-ahead continuation finding. [2-SOURCE]

**Cutler, Poterba & Summers (1991), "Speculative Dynamics," *Review of Economic Studies* 58(3), pp. 529–546.** This is the cross-asset paper the brief asks to chase; it studies stocks, bonds, FX, real estate, collectibles and precious metals together and documents three stylized facts common to all of them: (i) returns are **positively serially correlated at high frequency** (short horizon momentum), (ii) returns are **negatively serially correlated over long horizons** (long-horizon mean reversion), and (iii) deviations from fundamental-value proxies have predictive power for subsequent returns. [2-SOURCE — NBER/JSTOR abstract plus the HUD review]. Later literature links the paper's real-estate leg to Case-Shiller's serial-correlation finding, and the *general* Poterba-Summers convention is that mean reversion appears in variance-ratio terms at roughly 3–8-year horizons for other assets (equities, FX). **No real-estate-specific variance-ratio/reversal-horizon number from the 1991 paper itself was retrievable** — flag any specific housing reversal horizon attributed to CPS(1991) as **[RECALL — unverified]** pending the original tables.

## 2. Why momentum exists: three competing/complementary mechanisms

**Glaeser & Nathanson (2017), "An Extrapolative Model of House Price Dynamics," *Journal of Financial Economics* 126, pp. 147–170** (NBER WP 21037, 2015). Buyers make a "modest approximation": rather than fully back out the beliefs of past buyers when inferring demand from past prices, they treat past prices as if they only reflected contemporaneous demand (the way an economist naively reads a price trend as a demand signal). Calibrated to this approximation, the model *jointly* generates three facts rational models struggle to produce together: **momentum at ~1-year horizons, mean reversion at ~5-year horizons, and excess long-run volatility relative to fundamentals.** [2-SOURCE — NBER abstract + ScienceDirect/SSRN, consistent]. This is the cleanest citable statement of the **momentum→reversal crossover**: ~1 year vs. ~5 years.

**Guren (2018), "House Price Momentum and Strategic Complementarity," *Journal of Political Economy* 126(3)** (job market paper: "The Causes and Consequences of House Price Momentum"). Empirical starting point: house prices show **substantially more momentum than existing search/matching theories can generate** — positive autocorrelation in price changes persisting **2–3 years** after a shock before reverting. Mechanism: sellers face a **concave demand curve** (raising an above-average list price sharply cuts sale probability; cutting a below-average price barely helps), creating **strategic complementarity** that amplifies search-friction momentum by a factor of **roughly 2–3×**. Empirically, Guren reports the **median US city has an annual AR(1) coefficient of about 0.60** for house-price growth. [1-SOURCE — BU/Harvard working-paper pages and JPE abstract, consistent but not independently cross-checked against a second unrelated source].

**Head, Lloyd-Ellis & Sun (2014), "Search, Liquidity, and the Dynamics of House Prices and Construction," *American Economic Review* 104(4), pp. 1172–1210** (corrigendum AER 106(4), 2016). Uses city-specific income shocks across **106 US cities** and a search-theoretic model in which variation in **time-to-sell** (a liquidity/search-friction channel, distinct from Guren's price-setting channel) induces serially correlated transaction-price growth, giving a "substantial quantitative improvement" over a no-search benchmark. Qualitative claim [2-SOURCE, across AEA/IDEAS/Semantic Scholar]; the specific R²/error-reduction magnitude of "substantial" is **[RECALL — unverified]** — not retrievable from snippets.

**Ma (2020), "Momentum and Reversion to Fundamentals: Are They Captured by Subjective Expectations of House Prices?", *Journal of Housing Economics*.** Direct behavioral cross-check: household subjective house-price expectations **capture the momentum component but not the reversion-to-fundamentals component** — naive extrapolation shows up in real expectations data, but the eventual mean-reversion is not anticipated, exactly the belief structure Glaeser-Nathanson assume. [1-SOURCE — SSRN + ScienceDirect abstract, single thread, not independently cross-checked].

## 3. Cross-sectional evidence: serial correlation, mean reversion, and metro heterogeneity

**Capozza, Hendershott, Mack & Mayer, "Determinants of Real House Price Dynamics" (NBER WP 9262, 2002) and "An Anatomy of Price Dynamics in Illiquid Markets: Analysis and Evidence from Local Housing Markets," *Real Estate Economics* 32(1), 2004, pp. 1–32.** Panel of **62 US metro areas, 1979–1995**. **Serial correlation is higher** in metros with higher real income growth, population growth, and real construction costs; **mean reversion is faster** in large metros and faster-growing, lower-construction-cost cities. Framed as a damped-vs-oscillatory dynamical-systems problem: high serial correlation + low mean reversion produce **overshooting ("cycles")**, with the classic **high-construction-cost coastal metros — Boston, New York, San Francisco, Los Angeles, San Diego** — named as sitting in that overshoot-prone region. [2-SOURCE — NBER, SSRN, Wiley abstract, and University of Michigan working paper all agree]. This is the paper that operationalizes "supply elasticity conditions the crossover horizon": inelastic supply (proxied by construction cost) → longer momentum, slower reversion.

**Glaeser, Gyourko & Saiz, "Housing Supply and Housing Bubbles" (NBER WP 14193, 2008), *Journal of Urban Economics* 64(2), pp. 198–217.** Complementary, elasticity-first framing: places with more **elastic** housing supply have **fewer and shorter** price booms with smaller amplitude; in the 1980s boom, mean price growth was **29% in the most inelastic metros vs. 3.4% in the most elastic metros**. Even where elastic markets do see a boom, it tends to be **short-lived** because supply responds. [2-SOURCE — NBER abstract, Wharton working paper, and ScienceDirect record all corroborate the specific 29%/3.4% figures and the "shorter booms in elastic markets" conclusion].

Read together, Capozza et al. and Glaeser-Gyourko-Saiz give the clearest statement available on how the **momentum/reversal crossover differs by supply elasticity**: in supply-constrained (inelastic) metros, momentum both starts higher and decays more slowly, pushing the crossover to reversal further out; in elastic metros, new construction closes the demand-supply gap faster, momentum is weaker, and reversion (or simply boom termination) arrives sooner.

## 4. Genuine out-of-sample forecasting: the harder test

**Rapach & Strauss (2007), "Forecasting Real Housing Price Growth in Eight District States," Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis *Regional Economic Development* 3(2), pp. 33–42.** 25-variable ARDL framework for the Eighth Federal Reserve District states; **forecast combinations across ARDL specifications outperform a simple AR benchmark** out-of-sample. [1-SOURCE — ResearchGate/SLU description; exact % MSFE reduction not retrievable — **[RECALL — unverified]** for magnitude, [1-SOURCE] for direction].

**Rapach & Strauss (2009), "Differences in Housing Price Forecastability Across US States," *International Journal of Forecasting* 25(2), pp. 351–372.** Genuinely out-of-sample forecast evaluation, **1995Q1–2006Q4**, for the 20 largest US states, using mean square forecast error (MSFE) as the metric. Headline: **coastal states are systematically harder to forecast out-of-sample than interior states** — consistent with Capozza et al.'s inelastic/coastal-overshoot story. Forecast-combination methods help, but "it is difficult to choose *a priori* a consistently effective predictor set" because predictive power is time-varying. [2-SOURCE — IJF/ScienceDirect abstract plus an independent secondary citation confirming the asymmetry and window].

**Gupta & Das (2010), "Predicting Downturns in the US Housing Market: A Bayesian Approach," *Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics* 41(3), pp. 294–319.** Bayesian VARs (spatial and non-spatial) for the **20 largest US states**, estimated 1976Q1–1994Q4, forecast **1–4 quarters ahead** out-of-sample over **1995Q1–2006Q4**. BVARs were the **best-performing models in 19 of 20 states** and correctly signaled the coming downturn in **18 of 19** states tested for that. [2-SOURCE — SSRN, IDEAS/RePEc and Springer abstract all agree on sample, method and the 19/20 and 18/19 figures].

**Gupta & Das, "Forecasting Housing Prices: Dynamic Factor Model versus LBVAR Model" (2011 AAEA/NAREA conference paper, 42 US metro areas)** and **Das, Gupta & Kabundi (2011), "Forecasting Regional House Price Inflation...," *Journal of Forecasting* 30(2), pp. 288–302** (empirical application: **five South African metros**, 1980Q1–2006Q4, 282 macro series via principal components; 1–4-quarter-ahead RMSE OOS, 2001Q1–2006Q4). Result: **DFM statistically outperforms VAR alternatives in the majority of cases**. [2-SOURCE for the South African result — IDEAS/RePEc + ResearchGate agree; the 42-metro US conference paper's specific numbers are **[RECALL — unverified]**, existence/framing [1-SOURCE]].

**Bork & Møller (2015), "Forecasting House Prices in the 50 States Using Dynamic Model Averaging and Dynamic Model Selection," *International Journal of Forecasting* 31(1), pp. 63–78.** First application of DMA/DMS (Bayesian methods letting both predictor set *and* coefficients change over time/states) to house-price forecasting. **Allowing the model itself to change over time substantially improves accuracy**, beating an equal-weighted combination scheme; the **most volatile states needed the most model/parameter change** — predictability is itself regime-dependent. [2-SOURCE — ScienceDirect, SSRN, Aalborg research-portal agree]; specific R²/MSFE numbers **[RECALL — unverified]**.

**Ghysels, Plazzi, Torous & Valkanov, "Forecasting Real Estate Prices," *Handbook of Economic Forecasting* Vol. 2 (2013)**. The field's standard survey: frames the literature around **short-run persistence and long-run reversal** in real-estate log-price changes and the ability of local vs. aggregate variables to forecast returns, flagging high transaction costs (≈6%+ of property value) as a friction distinct from equities/FX forecasting. [1-SOURCE — consistent across SSRN, ScienceDirect, CiteSeerX; numeric bottom-line verdict not retrieved from snippets].

**A directly relevant negative/decaying result — Milunovich (2020), "Forecasting Australia's real house price index: A comparison of time series and machine learning methods," *Journal of Forecasting* 39(7), pp. 1098–1118.** A large set of ML and time-series models **outperform a random-walk-with-drift benchmark at 1- and 2-quarter horizons**, but at **4- and 8-quarter horizons the outperformance is no longer statistically significant.** [1-SOURCE — Wiley/IDEAS abstract]. This is the cleanest concrete illustration retrieved in this search run of the horizon-decay of house-price predictability: real, measurable OOS gains close to the random walk at short horizons, and **statistically indistinguishable from the random walk once you get to 1–2 years out.**

**A companion negative finding on nonlinearity — Balcilar et al.** (cited via secondary description): nonlinear regime-switching models can be **detected in-sample** for regional house-price growth, but "the evidence in favor of nonlinear models was virtually non-existent" **out-of-sample**. [1-SOURCE — description via a secondary review only; original Balcilar paper not independently pulled, so treat with appropriate caution].

## 5. Is there any credible study beating a random walk at 3, 5, or 10 years?

Honest answer: **essentially no, and the literature does not really try.** Every genuinely out-of-sample forecast evaluation found above (Rapach-Strauss, Gupta-Das, Das-Gupta-Kabundi, Bork-Møller, Milunovich) is conducted at a **1-to-4-quarter horizon**; none report a validated, real-time, expanding-window forecast race against a random walk at **3, 5, or 10 years**. Where a horizon effect is explicitly tested (Milunovich, Australia), the OOS advantage **shrinks to statistical insignificance by 1–2 years** — the shortest end of the range this question asks about. The theory papers (Glaeser-Nathanson, Guren, Capozza et al.) *imply* mean reversion by year 5, but that is a **calibration statement**, not an empirical forecast-beat. **No source retrieved here claims a genuine long-horizon (3y/5y/10y) OOS beat of the random walk for house prices** — report this absence as the finding, not as "yes, at long horizons too." The evidence is **strongest at 1–4 quarters**; directionally suggestive but essentially untested beyond that.

## 6. The momentum→reversal crossover, and how supply elasticity moves it

Putting theory and cross-section together: the **canonical crossover is roughly 1 year (momentum) to roughly 5 years (mean reversion)** per Glaeser-Nathanson, with Guren's continued momentum for **2–3 years post-shock** sitting between those anchors — different mechanisms (extrapolative beliefs, concave-demand complementarity, search/liquidity frictions) converge on a similar order of magnitude without one agreed-upon number. Capozza et al. show the crossover is **not a fixed constant** but a function of local supply: **high real construction costs / inelastic supply** → higher serial correlation, lower mean reversion, a later crossover, genuine overshoot (Boston, NY, SF, LA, San Diego); lower construction costs / faster population growth → faster reversion, earlier crossover. Glaeser-Gyourko-Saiz's elasticity results (29% vs. 3.4% 1980s boom amplitude) are the amplitude-side complement: elastic supply compresses both the size and the duration of the momentum phase.

## 7. India-side literature and data landscape (context, not this dossier's core focus)

A full India data-source inventory belongs in a companion dossier; three points surfaced directly relevant here. (a) **NHB RESIDEX**, India's official house price index, **50 cities, quarterly, base FY2012-13**, National Housing Bank, with separate "HPI@Assessment Prices" and "HPI@Market Prices" (under-construction) series, sourced from bank/HFC valuation data plus primary-market data [2-SOURCE — NHB white paper + secondary description]. (b) **Singh & Shanmugam (2026), *PLOS ONE*** — a U-MIDAS model using digital-payment value, CPI and a financial-stress index against quarterly RBI HPI (Oct 2019–Sep 2024) beats ARIMAX for **short-term** forecasting; the only India-specific evaluated forecasting paper found, and explicitly short-horizon. [1-SOURCE — PLOS ONE/PubMed abstract]. (c) India house prices do **not** converge nationally — recent work finds **three convergence "clubs"** across 50 cities (higher initial price/loans/rent/density/literacy → higher-price club; higher local inflation → lower-price club) [1-SOURCE — Asia-Pacific J. Regional Science/Springer]; an earlier panel unit-root study (Aye et al., ~2012) likewise found no convergence across 15 cities, 2007–2011 [1-SOURCE, cited only secondarily]. This reinforces Capozza et al.: a single national India index likely masks the metro-level heterogeneity that drives momentum/reversion.

No India-specific paper estimating a momentum AR(1), a crossover horizon, or a supply-elasticity-conditioned cycle length was found. India's FSI/land-use-regulation literature confirms FSI is a binding, city-varying supply constraint (one Israeli paper estimates a ~48% regulatory tax via an FSI-adjacent method, as a foreign analogue only) [1-SOURCE for the general India FSI-constrains-supply claim; the 48% figure is Israel-specific, **not India**]. The mechanism plausibly applies to Indian metros; **no one has yet measured it**.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Calibrate ambition to 1–4 quarters.** Every credible out-of-sample beat-the-random-walk result in the international literature lives at a 1-to-4-quarter horizon; nothing here supports building a 3/5/10-year point-forecast engine. This mirrors the desk's own ER-arc doctrine (state-conditional distributions, never point forecasts) — apply it to real estate too.
2. **Build the core signal as short-horizon momentum continuation, sized down with horizon.** An AR(1)-type or search/momentum feature at 1–3-year horizon (Guren: 0.60 median annual AR1, 2–3-year persistence; Glaeser-Nathanson: 1-year momentum peak) is the best-evidenced building block; do not extrapolate it past ~5 years without an explicit reversal overlay.
3. **Estimate India's own AR(1)/momentum-decay parameters — do not import US/Israel/Australia magnitudes.** The mechanisms (extrapolative beliefs, concave demand, search frictions, supply elasticity) are plausibly portable; the specific numbers (0.60 AR1, 2–3-year decay, 29%/3.4% boom amplitude by elasticity) are not shown to transfer and must be re-estimated on NHB RESIDEX / IIMA / RBI HPI series.
4. **Build the engine city/cluster-level, not national.** India's own club-convergence evidence (three price clubs, no single steady state) plus Capozza et al.'s "aggregation masks the metro heterogeneity that drives the whole predictability story" argue against a single national India house-price model.
5. **Use FSI/land-regulation intensity as a candidate supply-elasticity moderator, but register it as an untested design, not a fact.** The Capozza / Glaeser-Gyourko-Saiz mechanism (inelastic supply → longer momentum, slower reversion, bigger booms) is plausible for supply-constrained Indian metro cores (e.g., island-city Mumbai, central Bengaluru) vs. elastic peripheries (NCR extensions, outer Hyderabad/Pune) — but no India-specific paper measures this; pre-register it as a design (per this desk's own working discipline) before treating it as a result.
6. **Treat any in-sample "efficiency test" result (Case-Shiller-style) as insufficient validation on its own.** The R² ≤ 0.04 individual-home caveat from Case & Shiller (1989) itself is a warning: strong, statistically significant serial correlation at the index level can coexist with almost no explanatory power at the unit/transaction level relevant to a pricing engine. Require genuine expanding-window OOS backtests against a random-walk/AR benchmark, exactly as this desk already requires elsewhere (trial-ledger pre-registration discipline applies here too).
7. **Pin the exact NHB RESIDEX series definition before importing any momentum parameter.** "Assessment prices" vs. "market prices for under-construction properties" are economically different series (one lender-valuation-based, one primary-market-based) and will not have identical momentum/reversion dynamics; conflating them will corrupt any AR(1)/crossover estimate — and this compounds the standard area-basis (carpet/built-up/super-built-up) ambiguity that separately moves Indian per-sqft figures 20–35%.
8. **The strongest, best-evidenced "edge" here is a horizon-decay risk-management rule, not an alpha signal**: Milunovich's Australia finding (OOS beats the RW at 1–2Q, statistically indistinguishable by 4–8Q) suggests the single most defensible near-term India-engine design is a **short-horizon (1–2 quarter) tactical continuation signal with an explicit, pre-committed decay/stop rule beyond ~1 year** — not a long-horizon valuation-timing model, which this dossier finds essentially no genuine OOS support for anywhere in the international literature.
