## 1. Search-volume nowcasting: Wu & Brynjolfsson and what followed

**Wu & Brynjolfsson, "The Future of Prediction: How Google Searches Foreshadow Housing Prices
and Sales"** [RECALL — unverified]. Lynn Wu and Erik Brynjolfsson (MIT Sloan), circulated as a
working paper roughly 2009-2013, to the best of my recall eventually appearing as a chapter in
the edited volume *Economic Analysis of the Digital Economy* (Goldfarb, Greenstein & Tucker,
eds., University of Chicago Press, c. 2015) rather than a standard journal — treat the venue as
unconfirmed. Design: a Google "housing search index" from real-estate-related query volume,
tested against a baseline autoregressive model of housing starts/sales/prices. Claimed result:
the index **Granger-causes future home sales more strongly than future prices** — search intent
shows up in transaction *volume* before it shows up in *price* — reducing out-of-sample forecast
error by a double-digit percentage (I recall **~20-25% for sales**, materially smaller for
prices, trusted to no more than one significant figure). This volume-not-price asymmetry is the
paper's single most citable point, and it is consistent with the theoretical volume-leads-price
literature already in A5 (Stein 1995), which raises my confidence I am recalling the actual
finding — but it remains [RECALL — unverified].

**Replications and near-replications** [RECALL — unverified throughout]. I recall **Beracha &
Wintoki, "Forecasting Residential Real Estate Price Changes from Online Search Activity,"
*Journal of Real Estate Research*, c. 2013** — MSA-level Google search intensity forecasting
price changes, effect concentrated **1-2 quarters ahead** and, notably, **stronger around
turning points than in trending markets** (a regime-change detector more than a continuous
forecaster — needs confirmation). I also recall German/European work, roughly **Dietzel, Braun
& Schäfers, "Sentiment-based real estate forecasting with Google search volume data," c. 2014**
— extending the Wu-Brynjolfsson logic outside the US and finding a **weaker, less stable**
relationship, consistent with a "works best in the market it was discovered in" pattern common
to this literature.

**The standing failure case that frames all of this**: **Lazer, Kennedy, King & Vespignani
(2014), "The Parable of Google Flu: Traps in Big Data Analysis," *Science* 343(6176), 1203-1205**
[RECALL — unverified, high confidence this exists roughly as stated]. Google Flu Trends
over-predicted flu prevalence by a wide margin after ~2011-2012, traced to (a) silent algorithm
changes on Google's own search product altering the input series, (b) media-driven search spikes
decoupled from actual prevalence, and (c) no error-correction because the model was never
re-estimated against ground truth in real time. **This is the standard cautionary citation every
"alternative data nowcasts X" paper — housing included — has to address**: a search-volume index
measures *collective attention*, not the underlying transaction, and its mapping to the target
can drift when the platform, query mix, or narrative changes — the exact risk in any India
Google-Trends housing signal built from, e.g., "flat for sale Mumbai" query volumes.

## 2. Listings, months-of-supply, days-on-market, price cuts as leading indicators

**Anenberg & Laufer** [RECALL — unverified, moderate-high confidence on substance, low
confidence on exact venue/year]. I recall a Federal Reserve Board FEDS working paper, later
published (possibly *Review of Economics and Statistics*, c. 2017), titled close to **"A More
Timely House Price Index Using Zillow Data."** Design: rather than waiting for closed
transactions (a repeat-sales index like Case-Shiller needs these, with a resulting **~2-month
publication lag**), use *for-sale listing* data — asking prices, price cuts, time-on-market — to
nowcast the transaction-based index weeks to months earlier. Claimed payoff: this nowcast
**detected the 2006-2007 turning point materially earlier** (recalled as "about a year," not
trusted at that precision) **than Case-Shiller/FHFA showed in real time**. This is the closest
thing I recall to a rigorous academic validation of "price cuts and days-on-market lead price,"
versus industry commentary merely asserting it.

**Industry-side evidence (not academic, tag accordingly)**: Zillow Research and Redfin's Data
Center have both published (in blog/report form, not peer-reviewed) time series of **median
days on market**, **share of listings with a price cut**, **months of supply**, and
**sale-to-list ratio**, updated at **weekly (Redfin) to monthly (Zillow)** frequency — far
higher frequency than any government series. [RECALL — unverified] I recall Zillow economists
(Skylar Olsen, Aaron Terrazas were names associated with this research stream in the mid-to-late
2010s) writing specifically that a **rising share of price cuts** was an early tell of the
2018-2019 US market cooling, months before the repeat-sales indices showed deceleration. I also
recall these being widely cited during the 2020 pandemic boom and the 2022 rate-hike bust as the
fastest-available read on turning points — but I cannot cite a specific peer-reviewed paper
validating the causal lead-lag magnitude with a number attached; treat the *mechanism* as
plausible and well-attested in practitioner use, and the *quantified* lead time as unconfirmed.
The general real-estate-industry heuristic that **months-of-supply above ~6 signals a
buyer's/softening market and below ~4-5 a seller's/tightening market** is standard NAR-adjacent
folklore [RECALL — unverified] rather than a number I can attribute to a specific study.

## 3. The AVM error record: Zestimate, Zillow Prize, and the ML-vs-hedonic literature

**Zestimate published error.** [RECALL — unverified, given as ranges because I do not trust
point figures] Zillow has historically self-reported a **median absolute percentage error
(MAPE)** for the Zestimate, split by on-market vs. off-market homes (off-market is harder, and
more relevant to an AVM used without a current list price to anchor on). Recalled trajectory:
**launch-era (2006) double digits (~13-15%)**, narrowing over the 2010s to roughly **~7-8%
off-market / ~5% or under on-market** by the mid-2010s, with a further claimed step down after a
**neural-network production model around 2019** (press coverage recalled citing **~1.9%
on-market**, mid-single-digits off-market — the number pairing I trust least here and would
re-check first). The shape — **large early gains, diminishing later gains, off-market
persistently ~2-3x harder than on-market** — I trust more than any single figure in it.

**The Zillow Prize** [RECALL — unverified on specifics]. A public Kaggle competition, roughly
**2017-2019**, task: predict the **log-error of the existing Zestimate** against actual sale
price — a residual-improvement competition layered on Zillow's production model. Recalled:
**total pool ~$1.2 million, $1 million grand prize**, and a **two-stage structure** — a public
leaderboard round on one time window, then a **private, later time window** for final judging,
specifically *because* the public leaderboard was known to be gameable/overfit-prone. This
structure is itself the cleanest illustration of the **random-split-vs-time-split trap** (§4):
Zillow engineered the competition to defeat overfitting to a fixed historical sample by
re-scoring finalists on **data from after the model was frozen** — a temporal holdout, not a
random one. I have no reliable recall of the winning team or the exact production improvement;
naming either here would be a fabrication risk, so both are omitted.

**Kok, Koponen & Martinez-Barbosa, "Big Data in Real Estate? From Manual Appraisal to Automated
Valuation," *Journal of Portfolio Management*, c. 2017 (real-estate special issue)** [RECALL —
unverified]. As I recall it, the paper compares traditional hedonic/appraisal-based valuation
against ML approaches (I believe including random forests) on a large commercial/apartment
dataset, arguing ML can materially cut prediction error versus a hedonic baseline **when
properly validated**, while warning naive validation overstates the gain — consistent with §4.
No specific MAPE/R² figures recalled with confidence; do not attach a number without
re-verification.

**Pace, Bourassa, Cannaday and the spatial-AVM literature** [RECALL — unverified, moderate
confidence on the general body of work, low confidence on any single paper's exact title/year].
R. Kelley Pace's body of work (often with Gilley, Barry, or others) on **spatial autoregressive
(SAR) and geostatistical models for house-price prediction** is a foundational strand: ignoring
spatial autocorrelation in a plain hedonic OLS understates precision and can bias coefficients,
and adding a spatial-lag/error term or moving to **geographically weighted regression (GWR)**
improves out-of-sample accuracy, particularly where submarket structure is strong. Bourassa and
coauthors (recalled with Hoesli, possibly Peng or Cantoni) compared **submarket-dummy vs.
spatial-statistical approaches**, finding the two are often substitutes — a spatial model can
proxy unobserved submarket boundaries without the analyst hand-defining them. I cannot confirm a
specific "Pace, Bourassa & Cannaday" joint paper by that exact author triplet; treat the names as
**a literature body to search for**, not one confirmed citation.

## 4. The random-split-vs-time-split trap (the central methodological point)

This is the one point in this dossier I am most confident is correct as a *methodological
fact*, independent of any single paper, because it follows from how these models are built and
is exactly what the two-round Zillow Prize structure (§3) was designed to defeat:

A **random train/test split** on a housing panel puts transactions from the *same market period*
(same macro rate environment, same local price level, same lending conditions) into both the
training and test sets. A gradient-boosted tree or random forest can then exploit **fine-grained
neighborhood/time-adjacent patterns** — essentially interpolating within a price regime it has
already seen examples from — and will show large error reductions versus a linear hedonic OLS
baseline on this kind of split. **[RECALL — unverified, but this is the consensus finding across
essentially every AVM-methods paper I can recall, e.g. Antipov & Pokryshevskaya-style random-
forest-for-apartments studies]** typically report gains of this kind on the order of
**20-40% error reduction vs. OLS** under random splits.

A **temporal (chronological) split** — train on transactions before date T, test only on
transactions after date T — removes that leakage. The model must now extrapolate to a *price
level and macro regime it never saw*, which is exactly what a tree-based model (which predicts
by averaging/voting among training leaves) is structurally bad at, while a hedonic OLS with a
time trend or index term can at least extrapolate linearly. The empirical pattern I recall
across this literature: **the ML-over-hedonic advantage shrinks sharply and can invert** under a
true temporal holdout, especially across a **regime break** (a rate-hiking cycle, a bust, a
policy change) — the model has, in effect, memorized the training period's price level rather
than learned a transferable pricing function. This is the mechanism behind the Zillow Offers
failure (§5): a valuation model can have an excellent *reported* median error on its normal
(quasi-random, same-regime) validation set and still be badly wrong on the **specific, narrow,
temporally-forward slice** that actually matters for a trading or buying decision — the next
few months, in a market that has just changed regime. **Any AVM-style or ML-hedonic component
built for the India engine must be validated on a strict chronological (and, ideally,
purged/embargoed) split, never a random k-fold**, exactly per this desk's own CONTRACT process
note on purged CV — this section is simply the AVM-literature version of a rule the desk already
enforces elsewhere.

## 5. Zillow Offers: the real-world out-of-sample failure

[RECALL — unverified on exact figures; high confidence on the broad narrative, widely covered in
business press at the time — the claim most worth a priority re-check] Zillow operated **Zillow
Offers**, an "iBuying" business: the Zestimate-derived pricing engine made cash offers directly
to sellers, Zillow bought the home, renovated lightly, and relisted it. In **Q3/Q4 2021**, amid
an unusually fast-moving market (sharp appreciation then rate-driven deceleration), Zillow's
algorithm was — by the company's own admission — **buying homes at prices it could not
profitably resell**, compounded by labor/contractor shortages slowing the flip cycle and
swelling unsold inventory. Zillow paused new purchases, then in **November 2021** announced it
would **wind the business down entirely**, cut roughly **25% of its workforce (~2,000 jobs,
recalled)**, and took a large inventory write-down — recalled at **~$300 million for Q3 2021
("$304 million" specifically recalled but flagged for re-verification)**, with cumulative
losses/impairments on the ~7,000-9,000 unsold homes reported at the time running toward, by some
tallies past, **$500-900 million** by the 2022 wind-down completion. CEO Rich Barton's
explanation, as recalled: the algorithm's error bands were **too wide for the flipping
business's thin margins** — a model good *on average* but not reliable enough for a
**balance-sheet-committing, single-name, short-horizon** decision, precisely the §4 failure mode
realized as a real loss rather than a backtest statistic. **This is the dossier's single most
important negative finding**: a widely-trusted, publicly-benchmarked AVM (the same Zestimate
whose *aggregate* error looked good, §3) failed at exactly the task the India engine cares about
— committing capital to a narrow, forward-looking, single-asset bet in a regime-shifting market
— not at the task it was validated for (broad, backward-looking, cross-sectional accuracy).

## 6. Remote sensing: nightlights and built-up-area time series

**Henderson, Storeygard & Weil (2012), "Measuring Economic Growth from Outer Space," *American
Economic Review* 102(2), 994-1028** [RECALL — unverified, but this is a very well-known,
heavily-cited paper and I have high confidence in the core facts]. Uses DMSP-OLS satellite
nighttime-lights intensity as a proxy for local economic activity, primarily to **improve or
substitute for GDP growth estimates in countries with weak statistical capacity**. Core finding,
as I recall it: lights-based growth estimates and official GDP growth are correlated with an
**elasticity on the order of ~0.3** (i.e., a 1% rise in measured light intensity associates with
roughly a 0.3% rise in true output growth in their preferred specification — I recall this
figure being in the 0.2-0.3 range across specifications, not exact), and an **optimal composite
estimator blending lights-based and official GDP data** outperforms official statistics alone
specifically **where survey/statistical infrastructure is weakest** (their African-country
subsample). This paper is the foundational cite for essentially every subsequent "use nightlights
as an economic proxy" study, India real-estate-adjacent applications included.

**VIIRS vs. DMSP-OLS** [RECALL — unverified on exact spec numbers, high confidence on the
qualitative contrast]. Legacy **DMSP-OLS** (used through Henderson-Storeygard-Weil, most
1990s-2013 nightlights work) has **coarse ~1km-class resolution**, a **6-bit range that
saturates over bright urban cores** (cannot distinguish central Mumbai from central Delhi once
both are "maxed out"), **no cross-satellite/year radiometric calibration**, and pronounced
overglow "blooming." The newer **VIIRS Day/Night Band** (Suomi NPP, data from ~2012, via NOAA)
offers **finer native resolution (~500-750m)**, a **wider, better-calibrated range** (less
saturation), and **monthly cloud-free composites**, at the cost of more noise (flares, fires,
moonlight) needing its own cleaning pipeline. For India, **VIIRS is the right sensor for
anything post-2012**; the two series are **not directly splice-comparable** without explicit
inter-calibration — the same caveat class as the desk's own 2021 NSE index-splice issue.

**JRC Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL)** [RECALL — unverified on exact epoch years/
resolutions, moderate-high confidence on the product's existence and general shape]. Produced
by the EU Joint Research Centre, GHSL derives **built-up-area/volume grids from multi-decade
Landsat (later Sentinel-2) time series**, epochs recalled roughly **1975, 1990, 2000,
2014-2015, and a 2020-class update**, resolution improving from ~30-38m toward finer
Sentinel-era grids. **Free and open** (Copernicus/EU terms), globally gridded — the standard
input for "how much built-up area appeared in polygon X between year A and B," directly
relevant to nowcasting **housing supply/construction volume** ahead of any permitting or
completions statistic. **I am not aware of, and did not find (budget exhausted before I could
check), a specific published paper using GHSL or an equivalent series to nowcast property
SUPPLY in China or India** — a genuine gap, not a hedge, to log as an open question. Weaker,
more speculative recall: China "ghost cities" work identifying vacant developments via
**nightlight-vs-built-up mismatches** (built-up area expanding without matching light rise) —
plausibly real but no confident author/title, flagged **[RECALL — unverified, low-confidence]**.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Do not build an India Google-Trends housing signal on the strength of Wu-Brynjolfsson
   alone.** The Google Flu Trends failure and the weaker European replications mean any such
   signal needs its own out-of-sample validation on Indian data before being trusted, and should
   be expected to decay or drift as search platforms and behavior change (people now search
   inside Housing.com/99acres/MagicBricks apps, not raw Google, for much of this intent — the
   "index" itself may already be measuring the wrong channel in India).
2. **If search/listings data is ever sourced for India (99acres, MagicBricks, Housing.com listing
   counts, price cuts, days-on-market), treat it exactly as the Anenberg-Laufer design treats
   Zillow listings**: a *nowcast of the turning point*, not a price-level model — and validate it
   against a strict chronological holdout, never a random split.
3. **The random-split-vs-time-split trap is the single most important methodological guardrail
   for any AVM/ML valuation component in this program.** Per CONTRACT process note on purged CV,
   any hedonic-ML India valuation model (should the India fundamentals/real-estate data ever
   support one) must be scored on a forward chronological holdout across at least one regime
   break, not a k-fold random split — a random-split accuracy number from this literature is not
   evidence it will hold up in the one setting (a live, forward, capital-committing call) that
   matters.
4. **The Zillow Offers collapse is the load-bearing cautionary case for the whole program, not
   just for AVMs.** It is a real, dollar-denominated demonstration that a model with a good
   *average* validated error can still fail catastrophically on the *specific forward slice* a
   business (or a book) actually trades on, especially across a regime shift — directly
   analogous to this desk's own OOS refusals in the option-program optimization grids.
5. **Nightlights/VIIRS/GHSL are a genuinely untried, plausibly valuable India construction-supply
   nowcast** — free, global, high-frequency (VIIRS monthly, GHSL multi-year), and specifically
   suited to answering "how much new built-up area/inventory is coming in market X" ahead of any
   Indian permitting or completions statistic (which are sparse, lagged, and often unavailable
   at all at the city level). This is a genuine RUNSHEET-candidate data source, not merely a
   literature note — no egress block applies to satellite data providers already in the free-data
   universe (VIIRS via NOAA, GHSL via JRC/Copernicus), pending an explicit vault-authentication
   pass.
6. **Any India AVM/price-prediction component should report and publish its off-market error
   separately from on-market error, and expect off-market to be materially worse** (2-3x, per
   the Zestimate pattern) — an India engine that only validates against currently-listed
   properties will overstate its real-world accuracy on the harder, more decision-relevant,
   off-market case.
7. **Do not let a single well-marketed error statistic (a MAPE headline) stand in for a
   regime-tested track record.** The Zestimate's steadily improving *reported* MAPE coexisted
   with the same model family's catastrophic Zillow Offers failure — a clean, desk-relevant
   demonstration that a published accuracy number and a trading-relevant accuracy number are not
   the same claim.
8. **This entire dossier needs a verification re-pass before any figure in it enters the trial
   ledger or a published lesson.** Flag A6 to whichever later synthesis agent builds the India
   engine: re-run the ~20 planned queries (Wu-Brynjolfsson exact figures, Zestimate MAPE by year,
   Zillow Prize outcome, Zillow Offers write-down total, Henderson-Storeygard-Weil elasticity,
   GHSL epoch/resolution specifics, any China/India satellite-supply paper) with fresh search
   budget before this dossier is treated as evidentiary rather than as a research lead list.
