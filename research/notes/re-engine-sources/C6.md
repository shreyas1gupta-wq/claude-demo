## §1 — The residential gross yield landscape: two irreconcilable families

The handoff prompt for this whole engine (`research/register/handoff-prompt-india-realestate-engine.md` §4.2) already names this as the single highest-stakes unresolved number in the programme, and this pass cannot close it — only bound it more precisely than before.

**Family 1 — Global Property Guide (GPG), asking-price ratio method.** Median annual asking rent ÷ median asking purchase price across a city's listing stock, 1/2/3-BR units, published biannually. National blended figure: **5.16%, Q2-2026, up from 5.09% Q4-2025** `[1-SOURCE, per yields-reits-leverage.md]`. By this method Delhi (5.81%) and Kolkata (5.79%) rank **highest** among major cities — a second aggregator gives 6.19%/6.32% for the same two cities, agreeing on direction but not the point estimate `[2-SOURCE on direction, per yields-reits-leverage.md]`. Bengaluru and Hyderabad sit mid-table (3.6-4.6%, GPG-derived trend 2019→2026), Mumbai citywide at 3.84%.

**Family 2 — portal/broker micro-market surveys.** Track the specific premium corridors investors actually transact in, clustering distinctly lower: Mumbai 2.0-4.0%, Hyderabad IT-corridor 2.5-4.2%, Ahmedabad 3.9% (highest of any major city in that survey), Gurugram 4.1%, Noida 3.7%, Lucknow ~3%, Chennai 4.05-4.16% (Magicbricks Rental Index, Oct-Dec 2025, up from 3.78% a year earlier) `[1-SOURCE each, per yields-reits-leverage.md]`. Tier-2 hotspots run a modest premium: Indore's Nipania corridor up to 8% (Super Corridor ~6%), Kochi's Kakkanad IT belt 4.6-6%, a general tier-2 band of 4-6% against tier-1's 2-4% `[2-SOURCE, per candidate-screen.md §13]`. Surat's portal-reported 11-12% (Sachin/Surat North) was flagged and **not adopted** — the same portal's other Surat localities print 3-4%, a 3-4x internal spread read as measurement artifact.

**No source reconciles the two families.** The most defensible unsourced explanation (`yields-reits-leverage.md`): GPG's median-asking-ratio method is diluted toward older, cheaper stock (structurally higher rent-to-price ratio), while portal "city yield" figures are built from the newer, higher-capital-value stock dominating transaction volume — the same composition gap the CN programme found between GPG's China read and China's domestic-press figures. **Why it matters more than any model this engine will build**: the desk's own CN-D2 cross-country base rate (JST 18-country panel `[booked, trial-ledger.md]`) puts historical property-boom **peak yields at 3.29%** and **trough yields at 4.94%** (modern era). Family 2's premium-corridor numbers (Mumbai 3.84%, Bengaluru/Hyderabad prime 3.5-4.2%) sit near the **peak** band — a compressed-yield warning; Family 1's national blend (5.16%) sits **above** the **trough** band — priced like a post-bust market. One family says Indian metros are priced where markets historically top out; the other says where they bottom. A verdict-deciding 2-3pp gap, not a footnote.

**Numbeo**: no figure from this specific aggregator survived any search this program has run — not in `yields-reits-leverage.md`, not in `A2.md`, not elsewhere on file. Any Numbeo-specific India number in this file would be pure `[RECALL — unverified]` and is deliberately omitted rather than guessed at a city level; flagged as a concrete gap for the next live-search pass.

**Knight Frank, Anarock, Magicbricks/Housing.** Anarock reports India's top housing markets' **capital values up ~128% cumulative 2021-2024**, with rents in Bengaluru/Delhi-NCR/Hyderabad "lagging significantly" — yield compression at the premium end even as absolute rents also rose `[1-SOURCE, per yields-reits-leverage.md]`. Knight Frank's half-yearly "India Real Estate" report is recalled as including a city price index and (jointly with CREDAI-MCHI) registration-based Mumbai data above pure tracked-listing samples `[RECALL — unverified, per C3.md]`. Magicbricks' **PropIndex** (quarterly) gives locality-level price/rent for "prime" micro-markets, one of the few sub-city, free, regularly updated India series `[RECALL — unverified structural claim, per C3.md]`; its Chennai print (4.05-4.16%, above) is the one Magicbricks figure this pass can tag `[1-SOURCE]` directly.

**RBI/NHB rent data.** NHB RESIDEX is a **house-price** index (assessment- and market-price legs, both capital value, not rent); it "now spans HPI/Land/Building-Materials/a Housing Rental Index, though no granular HRI print surfaced" `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]` — a rental component exists in name, no printed value reached this program. India's CPI (Combined) Housing group IS a genuine **rent** series (actual reported rents, sampled urban dwellings) but is **urban-only** `[RECALL — unverified, moderate-high confidence, per C1.md]`, weight 21.67% urban / 10.07% all-India `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]`, and mid-overhaul toward monthly rural-and-urban collection (~2025-26). **This is the one genuinely rent-based official series with national reach, never used by this program to build a yield** (no matched price series at the same geography/vintage) — a cheap follow-up: splice CPI-Housing against RBI HPI/NHB RESIDEX growth as a rent-growth proxy.

**Academic estimates.** No paper specifically estimating an India rental-yield level was located. The closest is structural evidence: NSSO's 2012 round found **71% of Indian rentals have no written contract**; 2008-09 found only **~5%** used formal agreements, roughly a quarter registered `[2-SOURCE, two NSSO rounds converging, per A2.md, via Knight Frank 2019/an ICRIER blog]`. Some states run over 90% informal (AP/Telangana ~94%, Bihar ~92%). Implication: observed, contract-bearing rents are a small, likely non-representative slice of the true rental universe — a selection-bias problem biasing every survey-based yield figure in an unknown direction.

## §2 — Gross to net: an explicit waterfall, illustrative not measured

**No source has published a single India net-yield figure.** `yields-reits-leverage.md` built its own back-of-envelope estimate (~2.0-2.4% after-tax off a 4.0% gross start), flagged as "computable but not published anywhere" `[per that dossier's §4]`. This section rebuilds it with the brief's line items explicit, so sensitivity to each assumption is visible rather than buried in one bundled number. **Every figure below is `[COMPUTED]` on stated, illustrative assumptions — none is a sourced empirical figure.**

Starting point: gross yield = 4.0% of property value/year (a representative, non-outlier prime-urban figure from §1's table). Set property value = 100 units, so annual rent R = 4.0.

```
GROSS YIELD                                                          4.00
  less society/maintenance charges (illustrative ~7.5% of rent)     -0.30
  less property tax (effective, capital-value basis, likely
    sub-1% of property value per A2.md's correction to the
    commonly-misapplied "5-20%" ARV-basis figure — assume 0.2%)     -0.20
  less vacancy allowance (5-10% of rent cited as a practitioner
    cost-calculator input, per yields-reits-leverage.md §4;
    mid-point 7.5% of rent)                                         -0.30
  less repairs/upkeep (NOT covered by the 30% tax deduction in
    cash terms — see below; illustrative ~0.75% of property value)  -0.75
  less brokerage, WORST CASE: one month's rent charged at every
    11-month leave-and-license renewal = 1/11 of annual rent
    (9.09% of R)                                                    -0.36
                                                                     -----
  CASH NET YIELD, PRE-TAX (worst-case brokerage)                     2.09
  CASH NET YIELD, PRE-TAX (amortized brokerage, ~2.5yr tenancy,
    brokerage charged roughly once per ~2.7 renewal cycles,
    ≈3.4% of annual rent)                                            2.32
```

**Brokerage is the single most assumption-sensitive line here, and the brief is right to flag it.** Convention charges a broker one month's rent (sometimes split landlord/tenant) at tenancy *start*. Whether that recurs every 11-month renewal (worst case, 9.09%/yr of rent) or only once every few years at actual turnover (amortized, roughly a third of that) moves the cash-net figure by about a quarter-point of property value — comparable to the property-tax or vacancy lines. No source states which is empirically closer; a named gap, not resolved.

**The 30% standard deduction is a tax benefit, not a cash cost — do not net it against cash flow.** Confirmed mechanics `[2-SOURCE, per yields-reits-leverage.md §4]`: Section 24(a) allows a flat 30% deduction of Net Annual Value (rent minus municipal property tax paid), regardless of actual expenses, with **no separate repairs/maintenance deduction on top**. This cuts both ways: an owner spending *below* notional 30% on repairs gets a shield bigger than their real cost; one exceeding 30% (a leaky premium building, an old independent house) gets no extra relief. Society/maintenance charges get **no separate deduction at all** beyond the flat 30% `[2-SOURCE, same source]` — worse than many landlords assume.

Applying income tax: taxable NAV = (rent − property tax) × (1 − 0.30) = (4.0 − 0.20) × 0.70 = 2.66. For an owner in the top marginal slab (30% + 4% cess = 31.2%, a stable, long-standing Indian bracket `[RECALL — unverified, high confidence in the statute, not confirmed by search this session]`): tax owed ≈ 2.66 × 0.312 ≈ 0.83.

```
CASH NET, PRE-TAX (worst-case brokerage)      2.09
  less income tax (top bracket, unlevered)   -0.83
                                              -----
AFTER-TAX, IN-POCKET YIELD                    1.26  (≈1.3% of property value/year)
```

Using the amortized-brokerage cash-net figure (2.32) instead lands after-tax around **1.5%**. A lower-bracket owner, or one sheltering income via Section 24(b)'s uncapped interest deduction on let-out property (vs. the ₹2 lakh self-occupied cap) `[RECALL — unverified statute, not search-confirmed this pass]`, lands closer to the pre-tax cash-net ~2.1-2.3%. **Bound, not point estimate: an unlevered, top-bracket, worst-case-brokerage owner nets roughly 1.2-1.5% after-tax on a 4.0% gross start; a best-case owner nets closer to 2.1-2.4%.** This spans and slightly widens the source dossier's single-point 2.0-2.4% estimate — this build adds an explicit repairs line the source's bundled figure likely under-weights, and tests the brokerage-recurrence assumption the brief specifically flags.

## §3 — City spread and the apartment-versus-independent-house question

The city ranking is internally consistent across both yield families even where their levels disagree: premium/IT-corridor micro-markets (Mumbai, Bengaluru, Hyderabad prime) sit at the low end (2-4.5%); Delhi-NCR splits by measurement family (GPG's citywide blend highest in the country at 5.8%, portal NCR-corridor surveys — Gurugram 4.1%, Noida 3.7% — mid-table); Pune's IT corridors (Hinjewadi/Kharadi) show an unresolved internal split, one cluster at 4.0-4.4%, another at 5.5-6% `[1-2 SOURCE, internally conflicting, per yields-reits-leverage.md, cross-referencing ahmedabad-pune.md §8]`; tier-2 hotspots (Indore, Kochi) run 4.6-8%.

**Apartment versus independent house: the evidence on file is thin and price-based, not yield-based.** No dossier directly compared apartment vs. independent-house *yields* in the same locality. Two price-level data points hint at direction but disagree: in Kompally (Hyderabad NW), transacted flats averaged ₹5,844/sq ft against independent houses at ₹2,596/sq ft `[2-SOURCE, per hyderabad-lucknow.md]` — a large gap consistent with a land-heavy product paying disproportionately for non-rent-generating land, so lower yield; in Karjat (Mumbai outer), the same comparison shows almost no gap (builder-floor ₹6,527 vs. independent house ₹6,776 `[1-SOURCE, per mumbai-outer.md]`), which does not fit. **Unresolved — two anecdotal price points in opposite directions, no yield comparison at all.** A clean, cheap gap-map candidate: a same-locality apartment-vs-independent-house rent AND price pair from one portal would directly answer this.

## §4 — Maharashtra's registered leave-and-license route: does an aggregate exist?

The handoff prompt itself (§4.2) singles this out as **"the route that may resolve the yield conflict"** — Maharashtra is the one state requiring compulsory registration of leave-and-license (rental) agreements, on the same SRO/registration-department geography as sale-transaction data, meaning price and rent could in principle be pulled from the same registry for the same locality and the same period — a transaction-based yield rather than a listing-based one, unique in India.

**Confirmed to exist, at RECALL level only** (`C2.md`, itself zero-live-search): Maharashtra's IGR portal (`igrmaharashtra.gov.in`) supports **e-registration of leave-and-license agreements** — Aadhaar e-KYC/e-sign, no Sub-Registrar visit. Rent-agreement duty is low, plausibly weakening under-declaration incentive versus sale transactions (unverified interpretation, directionally sound). **Not confirmed by any search pass**: any published **aggregate** (counts, median rent/tenure/deposit, by locality or citywide) from this registry, or any **bulk-downloadable/API** dataset. The cross-dossier finding for every Indian state registration system (`C2.md` §1, `C3.md` §3) is a per-record, CAPTCHA-gated, UI-paginated search interface — no state is known to expose bulk registered-transaction microdata, a negative finding of moderate-high confidence, not a documented fact.

**Practical implication**: a transaction-based Maharashtra rental yield is plausibly *constructible* — the registry looks the right shape — but is **not currently accessible** as a ready aggregate. It needs either (a) a scripted, respectful per-record scrape of the e-search index (village/CTS + rent + tenure + deposit + date), run from the principal's machine given this environment's measured 36-of-37 portal unreachability (`_PROBE.md`), matched against the same-geography Annual Statement of Rates for a price comparator, or (b) an RTI/data-sharing approach. **This is the single highest-value, state-specific RUNSHEET item for resolving the yield conflict** — the only India source that could deliver a genuinely transaction-based, locality-grain yield.

## §6 — REITs versus the 10-year G-sec: the negative-carry claim, pinned

The desk's own repeated headline — "every listed Indian REIT yields below the 10-year G-sec" — is confirmed and pinned: India's 10-year G-sec traded **6.96-7.10%, September 2026** `[2-SOURCE, per yields-reits-leverage.md]`, against the four REITs' computed distribution yields of **4.83-6.24%**. Every one sits below the sovereign; spread roughly **−0.7pp to −2.2pp**, computed. Only SM REITs (§7), projected 8-9%, clear the G-sec — at the cost of single-asset concentration, a sub-two-year track record, and much thinner liquidity a bare G-sec comparison doesn't price in. `[per yields-reits-leverage.md §8]`: this is a starker negative-carry picture than China's own C-REIT/bond gap found by the CN programme — India's institutional property-income vehicles currently pay *less* than a risk-free government bond, a striking, sourced finding, not a folk claim.

## §7 — SM REITs and fractional-ownership platforms

SEBI notified the **Small and Medium REIT (SM REIT)** framework in **March 2024** `[2-SOURCE, per yields-reits-leverage.md]`, formalizing fractional-ownership platforms: SPVs pooling investors into single/few commercial assets valued ₹50-500 crore, mandatory exchange listing, 95% cash-flow distribution, independent trustee, **₹10 lakh minimum lot**. Every SM REIT scheme found (all via PropShare) is **single-asset, commercial-office** — none residential: PropShare Titania (Thane office, listed Aug-2025, projected yield ~9.0% FY26-28 → 8.7% FY29), PropShare Platina (IPO Jul-2025, ₹473cr, 1.61x subscribed), PropShare Celestia (Ahmedabad office, listed Apr-2026, projected 8.1%→8.9% FY26-29, reportedly listed at a discount) `[1-SOURCE each]`. Combined AUM "tripled to ₹1,070 crore," >₹68cr cumulatively distributed `[1-SOURCE]`. **These are prospectus projections on sub-two-year track records, not realized multi-year history** — a materially weaker evidence grade than the large REITs, and never comparable to §5-6's figures as equivalent evidence.

## §8 — International comparators: what is sourced versus what is not

**Sourced, this program.** Taiwan (~1.9-2.3%) and Singapore (~3.1%) both run *below* most cited India figures, contradicting a bare "India has the world's lowest yields" claim `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]`. Shanghai's community-level price-rent ratio sat at 78-79 raw (67-70 hedonic-adjusted) 2016Q4/2017Q1, implying gross yields ~1.3-2% — an extreme, sustained compression, >90% of Shanghai communities in the 50-80 ratio band as of 2018 `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]`. China's own national GPG-methodology yield runs approximately **2.6%** `[booked, via yields-reits-leverage.md referencing c-yield.md]` — India's GPG national figure (5.16%) runs roughly double China's, like-for-like. Prime London yields compressed to 3-4% as prices ran +180% since 2005 against rents +40% `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]`.

**Not sourced, deliberately not stated as fact.** Commonly-cited comparators — Philippines, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa, Turkey — did not surface in any search pass. Any specific figure for these here would be `[RECALL — unverified]` and is omitted rather than guessed; the only defensible general statement is that India's low yields are **not unique** but cluster with other high-asking-price, ownership-preference Asian markets (China, Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan) rather than typically higher-yielding Latin America/Africa — itself `[RECALL — unverified]`, a hypothesis for a follow-up search, not a finding.

## §9 — What A2's international literature implies for a yield this negative

Three findings from `A2.md`'s sourced literature bear directly on a negative-carry, compressed-yield market like India's. First, **Himmelberg-Mayer-Sinai's (2005, *JEP*) user-cost framework** — the standard tool for "is this yield/price level justified" — already failed a real out-of-sample test: applied to 46 US metros pre-2008, it judged most markets not overvalued right before a 30%+ crash, because its expected-appreciation input was fed by the very bubble it was meant to test `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]`. A user-cost defense of India's low yields carries the identical self-referential risk. Second, **Campbell-Davis-Gallin-Martin (2009, *Journal of Urban Economics*)** found 65% of the US 1997-2007 boom's falling price-rent ratio came from a *falling housing premium* (risk/illiquidity discount), not rates or rent optimism `[1-SOURCE, per A2.md]` — plausible for India too, where thin securitized alternatives (REITs only since 2019), historic capital controls, and ownership preference could suppress the required premium on physical property specifically. Third, most relevant to §5-6: **Plazzi-Torous-Valkanov (2010, *Review of Financial Studies*)** found cap rates forecast realized returns well for apartments/retail/industrial but **not offices** in-sample `[2-SOURCE, per A2.md]` — a direct warning against porting the residential price-rent playbook onto India's overwhelmingly office/retail REIT sector.

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Never quote a single "Indian rental yield" without naming the family.** GPG's asking-ratio method and portal/broker micro-market surveys disagree 2-3pp and rank cities in different orders; pick one method per series and hold it fixed, never blend levels across families.
2. **Treat the Maharashtra leave-and-license registry (§4) as the single highest-value India-specific data probe available** — the only source that could deliver a transaction-based, locality-grain yield; budget a scripted pull from the principal's machine, not another portal-scrape.
3. **Never net the 30% standard deduction against cash flow as a cost** — it's a tax benefit. Real undeducted costs are society charges, property tax, vacancy, repairs, brokerage (§2's waterfall: after-tax bound ~1.2-2.4% off a 4.0% gross start, driven mostly by brokerage-recurrence and tax-bracket assumptions).
4. **Do not port the residential price-rent playbook onto India's REIT sector** — Plazzi-Torous-Valkanov (§9) found cap-rate return forecasting fails for offices even in the US, and India's REITs are almost entirely office/retail.
5. **Pin every REIT number to its as-of date.** Distribution yields (4.83-6.24%, all below the 6.96-7.10% G-sec) are this file's most reliable numbers; since-listing total returns are the least reliable (inconsistent for three of four REITs) and need the caveat attached whenever quoted.
6. **Flag NOI, GAV, in-place-vs-market rent, and market cap as unfilled REIT gaps** — none found in any pass; a direct pull from each REIT's own investor presentation would close this cheaply.
7. **Treat apartment-vs-independent-house yield as unanswered, not merely under-documented** — the two data points on file disagree and are price-level, not yield-level; a same-locality rent-and-price pair for both types is a cheap gap-map candidate.
8. **Anchor India's yield levels against CN-D2's cross-country base rate, not intuition**: premium-corridor yields sit near the JST panel's peak band (3.29%); the national GPG blend sits above its trough band (4.94%) — the same evidence supports "priced like a peak" and "priced like a trough" depending which series is used, why this conflict is verdict-deciding.
