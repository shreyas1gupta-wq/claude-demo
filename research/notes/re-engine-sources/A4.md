# A4: Supply elasticity, land-use regulation and why cycle amplitude differs

As of 2026-09-11. Compiled via WebSearch only (20 successful queries; the last few of a planned ~25 failed when this session's shared search budget was exhausted — noted inline where a check could not be completed).

## 1. Core mechanism: elasticity is why identical demand shocks produce different price paths

A city housing market is supply-and-demand; the price response to any demand shock (income, migration, rate cuts, credit) is inversely related to the elasticity of new supply. Elastic supply (flat marginal cost — plentiful land, permissive rules) absorbs a shock mostly in **quantity**; inelastic supply absorbs it mostly in **price**.

The canonical statement for booms/busts is **Glaeser, Gyourko & Saiz, "Housing Supply and Housing Bubbles"** (NBER WP 14193, 2008; *Journal of Urban Economics* 64(2), 198–217) [2-SOURCE: NBER and ScienceDirect/JUE listings agree — https://www.nber.org/papers/w14193, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094119008000648]. Result: **places with more elastic supply have fewer, shorter, smaller price bubbles; 1980s-era US price run-ups concentrated almost exclusively in inelastic cities; elastic-city booms, when they occur, are brief** [2-SOURCE]. Key nuance for "amplitude": **the welfare cost of a bubble can be LARGER in elastic cities, because they overbuild more in response** — low price-amplitude substitutes price risk for vacancy/capital-stock risk, it is not free [1-SOURCE]. For an India engine: "inelastic = bad" is the wrong read; inelastic cities get bigger booms AND (per the model) sharper, faster busts, while elastic cities get muted price cycles but real overbuilding risk.

## 2. Measuring elasticity: geography (Saiz) and regulation (WRLURI)

**Saiz, "The Geographic Determinants of Housing Supply,"** *QJE* 125(3), 2010, 1253–1296 [2-SOURCE: QJE and NBER-hosted PDF — https://academic.oup.com/qje/article-abstract/125/3/1253/1903664, https://fpeckert.me/teaching/readings/SaizQJE10.pdf]. Method: satellite terrain/water data within 50km of 269 US metro centers build a land-unavailability index (underwater or >15% slope) [2-SOURCE]. Finding: elasticity is jointly geographic AND regulatory, and the two are endogenous to each other — regulation is often itself a political response to scarcity-driven appreciation in already-constrained places [1-SOURCE]. Magnitudes: San Francisco's long-run elasticity ≈ 0.66, among the most inelastic in-sample [1-SOURCE — not independently re-verified against Saiz's primary table this session]; secondary characterizations put the range at **below 1 in constrained coastal metros (Miami, SF, LA, NY) to ~3+ in unconstrained interior metros (Houston, Atlanta, Dallas)** [1-SOURCE, general characterization; specific per-metro Houston/Atlanta/Dallas figures NOT confirmed — treat as [RECALL — unverified] if reused]. A planned direct check of Saiz's national mean elasticity failed when the search budget ran out — **flagged gap for the next pass**.

**Gyourko, Saiz & Summers, WRLURI,** *Urban Studies* 45(3), 2008 [2-SOURCE — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0042098007087341, https://realestate.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/03/558.pdf], the first standardized survey index of local US regulatory stringency (approval delay, veto points, density limits, exactions, growth caps, litigation). **2018 update — Gyourko, Hartley & Krimmel,** NBER WP 26573; *JUE* 2021 [2-SOURCE — https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w26573/w26573.pdf, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S009411902100019X]: ~2,500 communities re-surveyed, WRLURI2018 standardized mean 0/SD 1, ~800 communities tracked in both 2008 and 2018 waves — first nationwide panel on how local regulation *changes* [2-SOURCE]. Near-average markets: Houston, Columbus, San Antonio, Pittsburgh [1-SOURCE]. **No equivalent standardized regulatory-survey index exists for Indian cities** — see §6.

## 3. The regulatory tax made concrete: Manhattan

**Glaeser, Gyourko & Saks, "Why Is Manhattan So Expensive?"** NBER WP 10124 (2003); *Journal of Law and Economics* 48(2), 2005 [2-SOURCE — https://www.nber.org/papers/w10124, https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/429979]. Home-building is competitive with low natural barriers, yet Manhattan prices sat at **more than twice physical construction cost** — the gap attributed to a **"zoning tax"** [1-SOURCE, single paper, widely mirrored]. **An India engine should treat FSI/FAR caps as literally the same object: a wedge between construction cost and market price that is a policy choice, not a land-scarcity fact, wherever the underlying land is not physically scarce.**

## 4. Refinements to the base model

- **Hsieh & Moretti, "Housing Constraints and Spatial Misallocation,"** NBER WP 21154 (2015); *AEJ: Macro* 11(2), 2019 [2-SOURCE — https://www.nber.org/papers/w21154, https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257%2Fmac.20170388]. Spatial-equilibrium model, 220 US metros: housing constraints in high-productivity cities (NY, SF Bay) **lowered aggregate US GDP growth by 36%, 1964–2009**; relaxing regulation to the median level could raise GDP ~2% [2-SOURCE]. A distinct *labor-misallocation* channel, not just price-volatility. **No equivalent Hsieh-Moretti calculation for Indian metros was found — an open gap, not a finding; do not quote an Indian number here.**

- **Baum-Snow & Han, "The Microgeography of Housing Supply,"** *JPE*, 2024 [2-SOURCE — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374579687_The_Microgeography_of_Housing_Supply, https://ungated.research.bowdoin.edu/article/4015]. Census-tract-level US elasticities: **floor-space ≈ 0.5, housing-units ≈ 0.3**, with **within-metro variation exceeding between-metro variation** [2-SOURCE]. New construction is only ~50% of the unit-supply response (teardowns/renovations matter); response rises with CBD distance via more land, flatter terrain, lighter regulation [1-SOURCE]. Implication: Mumbai's own island-city (1.33) / suburb (0.5–1) / MHADA-redevelopment (2.5) FSI tiers are already an India instance of this within-city heterogeneity (§6).

- **Hilber & Vermeulen, "The Impact of Supply Constraints on House Prices in England,"** *Economic Journal* 126(591), 2016, 358–405 [2-SOURCE — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/ecoj.12213, https://ideas.repec.org/p/cep/sercdp/0119.html]. Panel of 353 English planning authorities, 1974–2008, instrumenting regulation with a policy reform, vote shares and historical density. **Regulatory constraint substantively raises the house-price/earnings elasticity; pure land-scarcity effects are confined to highly urbanized areas; topography matters far less than regulation** [2-SOURCE]. South East England prices would have been **~25% lower in 2008, ~30% lower by 2015** under North East England's laxer rules [1-SOURCE]. Clearest non-US evidence that regulatory (not geographic) constraint dominates — relevant since India's constraint is almost entirely regulatory (FSI/FAR + approvals) outside Mumbai's coastline.

- **Nathanson & Zwick, "Arrested Development: Theory and Evidence of Supply-Side Speculation in the Housing Market"** [1-SOURCE — https://www.frbsf.org/wp-content/uploads/ArrestedDevelopment.NATHANSON-AND-ZWICKpdf.pdf]. Land speculation is easier than housing speculation (no rental-yield drag from banking raw land), so **it amplifies booms MOST in cities with intermediate/ample undeveloped land** — the "elastic" cities the base model calls safe [1-SOURCE]. (The brief's "Buchler" could not be matched to a specific paper this pass — closest hit was an unrelated Swiss elasticity paper; treat as [RECALL — unverified].) Implication: developer land-banking on Bangalore/Hyderabad/NCR peripheries and Mumbai's exurbs (Thane/Panvel/Navi Mumbai) plausibly carries its own land-price speculative cycle, distinct from and potentially larger than the built-unit cycle.

## 5. India's supply constraint is FSI/FAR — the direct analog of the "zoning tax"

**Alain Bertaud's "Mumbai FSI Conundrum: The Perfect Storm — The Four Factors Restricting New Floor Space in Mumbai"** (2004, updated ~2011) [1-SOURCE, venue unconfirmed — https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228886373] names four compounding constraints: **(1) topography, (2) restrictive land-use legislation (the FSI cap), (3) muddled property rights, (4) deficient infrastructure**, with (2) — a pure policy choice — identified as the largest [1-SOURCE]. Consequence: Mumbai households consume **~2.9 sqm floor space per person**, among the lowest in the world for a city this size [1-SOURCE].

Numbers (Bertaud/Tabarrok lineage) [1-SOURCE — https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2018/01/skyscrapers-slums-floor-space-index.html]: **Mumbai base FSI fell from ~4.5 (1964) to ~1.0–1.33** through the 1990s–2010s [1-SOURCE, time series not independently re-derived]; comparators: **Manhattan ≈15, Hong Kong ≤12, Tokyo ≈20, Singapore ≈25** [1-SOURCE]. Trade-press current ranges [1-SOURCE, industry blogs — Godrej Properties, 99acres, addressofchoice, fourquarters; **flag: these blend base/premium/TDR-loaded FSI inconsistently — treat as upper-bound "permissible," not "as-built"**]: Mumbai island 1.33 (suburbs 0.5–1.0, MHADA up to 2.5); Delhi 1.2–3.5 (redevelopment to 4); Bangalore 1.5–2.75 residential (2.5–4 commercial); Chennai 1.5–2.0; **Hyderabad has no fixed cap, built projects reach FSI 6–7** [1-SOURCE]. If correct, Hyderabad is the one major metro whose regulatory constraint is closest to "elastic" by design — a checkable prior.

**Brueckner & Sridhar (2012),** *Regional Science and Urban Economics* 42, 1061–1067 [2-SOURCE — SSRN and ScienceDirect: https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/SSRN_ID4136410_code1009447.pdf, https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0166046212000737]. Applies the Bertaud-Brueckner (2005) spatial model to India: **CBD FAR in Mumbai/Delhi/Chennai is at most 3.5, below 1.5 in Mumbai and Chennai specifically — versus 10+ in other world CBDs** [2-SOURCE]. Welfare gain from a moderate height-limit relaxation: **106 million rupees** in their case city [1-SOURCE].

**Bertaud & Brueckner, "Analyzing Building Height Restrictions... Bangalore, India"** (World Bank WP; *Regional Science and Urban Economics*) [2-SOURCE — https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=610334, https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/6780eca4-cf57-5d8b-8491-9666dc9bbf88/download]. Height caps force lower center density, forcing sprawl; welfare cost = extra edge-household commuting cost. **Bangalore welfare cost: 3–6% of household consumption**, large enough to push marginal households into poverty [2-SOURCE]; **spatial footprint would shrink 191→159 km² without the restriction** [1-SOURCE].

## 6. Mumbai's natural experiment: Nagpal & Gandhi (the closest thing to an India elasticity estimate)

**Nagpal & Gandhi, "Relaxing Floor Area Ratio: Housing Supply & Affordability in India"** (CEPR/STEG WP; **2025 Edwin S. Mills Best Paper Award**, AREUEA) [2-SOURCE — https://grp.cepr.org/publications/steg-working-paper/relaxing-floor-area-ratio-housing-supply-affordability-india, https://cprindia.org/events/relaxing-floor-area-ratio-housing-supply-affordability-in-india/; full text https://papers.geetika-nagpal.com/FAR_Mumbai_Nagpal_Gandhi.pdf]. Granular panel data, spatial/time variation in a Mumbai FAR relaxation. **FAR utilization +17%; housing units supplied in treated areas +58%; prices in treated areas −24%** [2-SOURCE]. No average change in unit size, but the most-relaxed areas saw smaller units — the supply response there came from more, smaller, cheaper units [1-SOURCE]. **This +58%-quantity/−24%-price pairing is the one causally-identified India magnitude an engine can anchor to, rather than relying solely on cross-sectional FSI-level comparisons.**

## 7. Disconfirming evidence: deregulation is necessary but NOT sufficient in India

Per the desk's discipline, this negative finding must be as prominent as §6's positive one. **Dutta, Gandhi & Green, "Is Land-Use Deregulation Enough to Deliver Housing?: Institutional Frictions in India,"** *Real Estate Economics* [2-SOURCE — https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/1540-6229.70009, https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/publications/is-land-use-deregulation-enough-to-deliver-housing-the-case-of-in-2/]. Studies **ULCRA (1976)** repeal — a cap of ~500–2,000 sqm on individually-held vacant urban land in ~70 large cities, with "surplus" surrendered to government below market rate [1-SOURCE]; repealed federally 1999, state-adopted 1999–2008 (Maharashtra only in 2007) [1-SOURCE]. Diff-in-diff across 200+ cities: **the repeal did NOT deliver the formal housing-supply growth theory predicts, because unresolved property-rights disputes over the frozen "surplus" parcels triggered fresh litigation that kept construction frozen** [2-SOURCE]. **This qualifies every FSI/FAR argument above: raising a legal floor-space cap on clean-titled plots (Nagpal-Gandhi: worked) is a different animal from releasing legally-encumbered land (ULCRA: largely did not), because only the latter runs into title/litigation frictions.**

Compounding this: **Gandhi, Tandel, Tabarrok & Ravi, "Too Slow for the Urban March,"** *JUE* 2021 (Brookings India WP 2019) [2-SOURCE — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0094119021000127, https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/Too-Slow-for-the-Urban-March1.pdf]. Across ~3,000 Mumbai projects: **27.3% of projects / 42.9% of built-up space under litigation; average construction 8.5 years; litigated projects ~20% slower; delay raises total cost ≥30%** [2-SOURCE], identified via an instrument (neighborhood litigiousness). A third friction layer beyond the legal cap and ULCRA title problems: **even with sufficient FSI and clean title, courts add years and 30%+ cost — Mumbai's effective elasticity is lower than nominal FSI numbers suggest, and this is close to unmeasurable from FSI data alone.**

Two further constraints, industry/planning sources [1-SOURCE each]: **(a)** Of Mumbai's ~5,379 acres of salt-pan land floated for affordable housing, only **~25 acres (0.5%) are actually developable** (wetland/mangrove/disputed); CRZ rules still bind even after a 2024 rule excluded salt pans from the formal "wetlands" definition [architexturez/ICSF/Testbook/Solomon&Co — one source-family, same policy episode]. **(b)** **DCPR 2034** (effective Sept 2018) raised FSI via premium payment plus expanded TDR loading, targeting ~10 lakh new affordable units, plus redevelopment incentives (extra 10 sqm/member or 15% of built-up area for buildings >30 years old) [Knight Frank / CREDAI-MCHI]. Longest-standing distortion: **rent control** — **Tandel, Patel, Gandhi, Pethe & Agarwal, "Decline of Rental Housing in India: The Case of Mumbai,"** *Environment and Urbanization* 28(1), 2016 [2-SOURCE — https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956247815620316, https://marroninstitute.nyu.edu/uploads/content/Decline_of_Rental_Housing_in_India.pdf]: frozen rents deteriorated existing stock, halted new rental construction, produced informal *pagdi* (key-money) workarounds; spatially concentrated rent-controlled tenements are mapped and a gradual-rationalization path proposed [2-SOURCE]. A demand-suppression-via-supply-destruction channel distinct from FSI, compounding Mumbai's low-elasticity story.

## 8. Observed India price-cycle amplitude (context, not yet linked causally to the above)

**NHB RESIDEX** (National Housing Bank's official HPI; piloted 2005 in Mumbai/Delhi/Bangalore/Bhopal/Kolkata, launched 2007, now 50 cities, base year 2012–13) [1-SOURCE — https://residex.nhbonline.org.in/, IIMB WP 477 methodology background] shows "considerable volatility with significant inter-year and inter-city variation" [1-SOURCE]. One snapshot: **2018 YoY — Hyderabad +13.3%, Bengaluru +11.1%, Mumbai +6.1%** [1-SOURCE, single wire item]. Composite HPI **83 (Jun 2013) → 105 (Dec 2018)**, double-digit growth 2010–2016 (peak +26.3% Q4 2011), then **+3.7%/yr 2017–2020** post demonetization/GST/RERA [1-SOURCE]. **A genuine city-by-city peak-to-trough amplitude comparison was not completed this pass — an open data task, not a finding; do not treat the single-year snapshot as validating any city ranking yet.**

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Treat FSI/FAR as India's "zoning tax" analog, scored on three layers, not one**: (a) legal FSI/FAR cap, (b) title/litigation friction (Dutta-Gandhi-Green; Gandhi-Tandel-Tabarrok-Ravi), (c) physical land availability (CRZ/salt-pan share, Mumbai specifically). A single "FSI = X" overstates true elasticity almost everywhere, especially Mumbai.

2. **Do not import a specific Saiz/WRLURI numeric elasticity for any Indian city** — no validated geographic or regulatory-survey index exists for India in sources found. The one causally-identified India magnitude is Nagpal-Gandhi's Mumbai pairing (**+58% units / −24% price** for **+17%** FAR utilization) — anchor to this for Mumbai specifically, not as a national constant.

3. **Rank prior (regulatory side, from FSI ranges found): Hyderabad (uncapped) > Bangalore/Delhi (1.2–4) > Chennai (1.5–2) > Mumbai (tightest, 0.5–1.33)** — implying, on Saiz/GGS logic, Mumbai should show the LARGEST amplitude to an identical demand shock and Hyderabad the smallest, before adjusting for §7's frictions. This is a testable hypothesis for a future registered design, not yet validated by amplitude data.

4. **Split "deregulation" into two non-interchangeable categories**: FSI/FAR relaxation on clear-title plots (worked, Nagpal-Gandhi) vs. land-ownership-based release (largely did not, ULCRA/Dutta-Gandhi-Green). Score future India policy events by which category they fall into.

5. **Expect a land-speculation cycle distinct from the built-unit cycle on India's peripheries** (Nathanson-Zwick): Bangalore's outer ring, NCR's periphery, Navi Mumbai/Thane, Hyderabad's IT corridor are "elastic" by FSI/geography yet plausible sites of developer land-banking cycles orthogonal to apartment-price amplitude — track land-parcel prices separately if data becomes available.

6. **Always record the FSI concept and area basis before comparing across cities/time** — none of the trade-press figures (§5) disclose base vs. premium vs. TDR-loaded FSI, directly analogous to the carpet-vs-super-built-up ambiguity that moves headline per-sqft numbers 20–35%.

7. **Within-city elasticity variation likely dominates between-city variation** (Baum-Snow & Han) — Mumbai's own island/suburb/MHADA FSI tiers (1.33/0.5–1/2.5) already show this. Prefer a ward/zone-level elasticity proxy over one city-wide number when data allows.

8. **Named, unfilled gaps for the next pass**: no Hsieh-Moretti-style misallocation estimate exists for Indian metros; no completed city-by-city NHB RESIDEX peak-to-trough amplitude comparison; Saiz's exact per-metro elasticity table not re-verified.
