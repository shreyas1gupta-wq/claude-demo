## 1. Loss aversion and nominal anchoring by sellers

**Genesove & Mayer (2001), "Loss Aversion and Seller Behavior: Evidence from the Housing Market," *QJE* 116(4), 1233–1260** (NBER WP 8143). Downtown Boston condo sellers, 1990s, post boom-bust. Owners facing a nominal loss vs. their purchase price **set list prices 25–35% of the loss amount higher**, **realize 3–18% of the loss in higher final sale prices**, and have a **substantially lower hazard of sale** (longer time-on-market, some never transact). [2-SOURCE — QJE, NBER, Wharton PDF, multiple secondary citations agree on the exact figures]. Read as direct evidence of prospect-theory reference dependence around the *nominal* purchase price, and offered as one explanation for the price-volume correlation (§5).

**Genesove & Mayer (1997), "Equity and Time to Sale in the Real Estate Market," *AER* 87(3), 255–269.** Same Boston condo market, early 1990s, equity-constraint focus. High-LTV (low-equity) owners set a **higher asking price**, have a **longer expected time-on-market**, and, if they sell, get a **higher price** than low-LTV owners — explained by the **down-payment requirement facing purchasers but not incumbent owners** (a low-equity seller must bring cash to close, behaves as liquidity-constrained). Explicitly framed as support for **equity-based theories of price-volume co-movement**. [2-SOURCE]. Read together, 1997 (equity/down-payment constraint) and 2001 (pure prospect-theory loss aversion) isolate two distinct frictions that push the same direction on the same dataset — why later papers try to separate them.

**Ross & Zhou (2021), "Loss Aversion in Housing Sales Prices: Evidence from Focal Point Bias," NBER WP 28796.** Authors/title/venue confirmed [1-SOURCE]; specific findings not retrieved beyond the title. The title implies a reframing of some Genesove-Mayer-style patterns as partly **round-number/focal-point anchoring** rather than pure reference dependence — mechanism detail is **[RECALL — unverified]**; pull before citing.

**Bokhari & Geltner (2011), "Loss Aversion and Anchoring in Commercial Real Estate Pricing," *Real Estate Economics* 39(4), 635–670.** Extends Genesove-Mayer to **US commercial property, 2000s cycle**: loss-facing owners set higher ask, get higher transaction price, longer time-on-market — effect **roughly the same order of magnitude** as the residential estimate. Two points matter for a systematic desk: (i) **larger, more experienced institutional investors show equal-or-greater loss aversion**, not less; (ii) the effect is **regime-dependent** — strongest at the **2007 cycle peak**, **"virtually ineffective" in the 2008–09 crash**, and the **aggregate market cycle was only mildly affected** by the individual-level bias once aggregated. [2-SOURCE].

## 2. Down payments, liquidity constraints, and the price-volume model

**Stein (1995), "Prices and Trading Volume in the Housing Market: A Model with Down-Payment Effects," *QJE* 110(2), 379–406** (NBER WP 4373). The theory Genesove-Mayer test empirically. A minimum down payment ties a seller's ability to move to their current equity: a price shock that erodes equity **mechanically cuts volume** (fewer owners can fund a new down payment), and this volume drop **further depresses prices** — a **multiplier** producing volatility beyond fundamentals and, in some parameterizations, **multiple equilibria**. This is the theoretical basis for treating **volume as the first-moving signal** in a downturn (§5). [2-SOURCE].

**Anenberg** — two related pieces. (a) "Loss aversion, equity constraints and seller behavior," *Regional Science and Urban Economics* 41(1) (2011), 67–76 — long **San Francisco Bay Area panel**, attempts to **separately identify** loss aversion from equity constraints (which 1997/2001 Genesove-Mayer could not cleanly separate); reportedly finds effects **at least as large as**, by some estimates larger than, the original estimates [1-SOURCE for magnitude]. (b) "Information Frictions and Housing Market Dynamics," Fed FEDS WP 2012-48 (→ *Journal of Urban Economics*) — sellers **don't know the market-clearing price** and learn slowly from their own time-on-market/offer history, generating listing prices that decline **gradually** rather than jumping to clear the market — a rational-learning complement to (not substitute for) loss aversion; both predict sticky list prices for different reasons. [2-SOURCE].

## 3. Extrapolation, momentum traders, and inflation illusion in beliefs

**Piazzesi & Schneider (2009), "Momentum Traders in the Housing Market: Survey Evidence and a Search Model," *AER (P&P)* 99(2), 406–411** (NBER WP 14669). Cluster analysis of the **Michigan Survey of Consumers** during the 2000s boom finds a **small, distinct cluster who say it's a good time to buy specifically because prices will rise further** — genuine extrapolative belief, not generic optimism — and this **cluster's share roughly doubled toward the end of the boom**. Their search model shows a **small minority of such buyers can move aggregate prices substantially** without buying much of the stock, because the marginal (not average) buyer sets price under search frictions. [2-SOURCE].

**Piazzesi & Schneider, "Inflation Illusion, Credit, and Asset Pricing" / "Money Illusion and Housing Frenzies" (NBER WP 12810).** Models **"illusionary" investors who mistake nominal for real rate changes** alongside "smart" Fisher-equation investors. Predicts a **non-monotonic price-to-rent vs. nominal-rate relationship**: booms can occur **both when nominal rates are unusually low and unusually high**, because both tails widen the smart/illusionary disagreement about the real rate. Noted: many countries boomed in the **high-inflation 1970s** and again, more strongly, in the **low-inflation 2000s**. [2-SOURCE for mechanism; 1-SOURCE for the comparative-boom-size claim].

**Case, Shiller & Thompson, "What Have They Been Thinking? Home Buyer Behavior in Hot and Cold Markets"** (NBER WP 18400; Brookings Papers on Economic Activity, extended 2022). Buyer surveys, **four US metros**, 1988 and annually 2003–2014+. **Short-run (1y) expectations, if anything, underreact** to the actual YoY change; but **long-run (10y) expectations reached extreme levels relative to mortgage rates right at the 2000s peak**, proposed as a **bubble diagnostic** analogous to a yield-curve inversion. [2-SOURCE]. Reconciles with Piazzesi-Schneider: the extrapolative belief lives in a **minority sub-cluster** and in the **long-horizon** expectation, not the median short-run forecast — consistent, not contradictory.

**Guren (2018), "House Price Momentum and Strategic Complementarity," *JPE* 126(3), 1172–1218.** A pricing-mechanism (not belief-based) explanation for momentum: sellers face a **concave demand curve** (pricing above-average sharply cuts sale probability; pricing below-average barely helps), so list prices adjust only gradually, one seller partially following another — **strategic complementarity** that **amplifies search-friction momentum ~2–3×**. [2-SOURCE]. Complements §1: loss aversion explains *why* a seller anchors; Guren explains why prices adjust sluggishly and amplify momentum even without any psychological bias.

**Bailey, Cao, Kuchler & Stroebel (2018), "The Economic Effects of Social Networks: Evidence from the Housing Market," *JPE* 126(6), 2224–2276.** Facebook social-graph data merged with housing transactions and survey evidence. Individuals whose **geographically distant friends** saw larger recent house-price gains view local property as more attractive, especially if they discuss it with those friends. A **5pp larger friend-experienced price increase** over 24 months → **3.1pp higher probability of transitioning renter-to-owner**, buying a **1.7% larger house**, paying **3.3% more** for a given house, and a **7% larger down payment**. [2-SOURCE, numbers explicit and consistent across sources]. This is a clean, quantified expectations-contagion channel distinct from both Piazzesi-Schneider (own-belief clusters) and Case-Shiller-Thompson (survey expectations).

## 4. Speculators, out-of-town buyers, and flippers as identifiable populations

**Chinco & Mayer (2016), "Misinformed Speculators and Mispricing in the Housing Market," *RFS* 29(2), 486–522** (NBER WP 19817). Deed-level records identify **out-of-town second-home buyers** as misinformed speculators in **Phoenix, Las Vegas, Miami, mid-2000s**: their purchases predict **both future price appreciation and future appreciation in the implied-to-actual rent ratio (IAR)** — the IAR being the paper's pure-mispricing proxy, since it shouldn't move if buyers price the rental fundamental correctly. Versus **local** second-home buyers, the out-of-town group had **worse exit timing** and were **less able to consume the "dividend"** of ownership — consistent with speculation, not consumption. [2-SOURCE].

**Bayer, Geissler, Mangum & Roberts, "Speculators and Middlemen: The Role of Flippers in the Housing Market"** (NBER WP 16784, 2011; *RFS* 33(11), 2020, 5212–5247). LA transactions, 1988–2009. Splits investors into **"middlemen"** (buy low, sell high throughout the cycle, add liquidity/improve the capital stock) vs. **"speculators"** (enter mostly **during the boom**, transact near market price both legs, show **no price-timing skill**, earn little more than market appreciation). **Speculative activity rose sharply in the boom and was strongly associated with subsequent price bubbles at both metro and neighborhood level.** [2-SOURCE].

**DeFusco, Nathanson & Zwick, "Speculative Dynamics of Prices and Volume"** (NBER WP 23449, 2017; *JFE* 146(1), 2022, 205–229). **50 million US home sales**, last full cycle. Cities with **larger speculative booms** had **larger price booms, sharper unsold-listing increases as the market turned, and more severe busts**. Mechanism: **predictable price increases disproportionately attract short-horizon buyers**, who **amplify volume** (sell sooner) and **destabilize prices via positive feedback**; the calibrated model matches the empirical **lead-lag price-volume relation** and the **sharp inventory spike** at turning points. [2-SOURCE].

**Gao, Sockin & Xiong (2020), "Economic Consequences of Housing Speculation," *RFS* 33(11), 5248–5287** (NBER WP 26457). Uses **cross-state capital-gains-tax variation** as an instrument for extrapolation-driven speculation intensity. More speculation → **greater price appreciation, stronger local expansion, more construction** in 2004–06, **and more severe downturns** in 2007–09 — speculation transmits into the real economy, not just prices, via a **supply-overhang channel** and a **local household-demand channel**. [2-SOURCE].

**Giglio, Maggiori & Stroebel, "Very Long-Run Discount Rates"** (NBER WP 20133; *QJE* 130(1), 2015). Exploits **UK and Singapore leasehold-vs-freehold** pricing (leases of 99–999 years vs. perpetual ownership of otherwise identical units): **100-year leaseholds price >10% below freeholds**, implying **discount rates below ~2.6%** for very-long-run housing claims. [2-SOURCE]. Less directly behavioral than the rest of this dossier, but it is the cleanest revealed-preference read of how housing markets price the far future — relevant to any India engine module valuing very-long-tenure leasehold land (common in Indian metros via development-authority leases).

## 5. Volume leads price; time-on-market and listings-to-sales as state variables; downward stickiness

**Price-volume causality.** Stein (1995, §2) is the theoretical backbone: down-payment/equity constraints tie transaction **quantity** to equity, so a shock shows up first in **volume**. Empirically the picture is genuinely mixed and regime-dependent — which is itself the finding: **Clayton, Miller & Peng, "Price-Volume Correlation in the Housing Market: Causality and Co-Movements,"** *Journal of Real Estate Finance and Economics*, plus a separate US 1999–2015 study, together show (i) in an average-relationship test **price tends to unilaterally lead volume**; (ii) the link is **asymmetric** — **price decreases reduce volume; price increases have little effect on volume** (consistent with Stein's mechanism and with sellers "sitting tight" per Genesove-Mayer rather than selling into a loss); (iii) **volume becomes the informative/leading variable specifically when price is rigid** (demand-supply imbalance shows in falling volume/rising unsold inventory before it shows in the printed price); and (iv) **volume Granger-causes price mainly where supply is inelastic**. [2-SOURCE].

**Time-on-market / listings-to-sales as the classic search-theoretic state variable. Wheaton (1990), "Vacancy, Search, and Prices in a Housing Market Matching Model," *JPE* 98(6), 1270–1292.** The foundational model: the **vacancy rate** (≈ listings relative to sale flow) determines **expected time-on-market**, which sets both parties' **reservation prices**. Higher vacancy → longer time-on-market → **lower seller reservation price → lower transaction prices** — the theoretical basis for every "months of unsold inventory" indicator (§7). [2-SOURCE]. **Han & Strange (2015), "The Microstructure of Housing Markets: Search, Bargaining, and Brokerage,"** *Handbook of Regional and Urban Economics* Vol. 5. The standard survey: one-sided search / random-matching / directed-search models; **bargaining** post-match (bilateral negotiation and multi-party bidding wars — itself a tight-market, low-time-on-market signal); and the **brokerage/agent** literature (matching role, entry/competition, incentive problems — links to §6). [2-SOURCE].

**Downward nominal rigidity.** A study on **three centuries of Norwegian housing transactions (1850–2019)** finds the **fraction of repeat sales at exactly zero nominal price change rises specifically during downturns** — long-run, non-US evidence that prices stick at reference levels precisely under downward pressure, with **prospect theory/loss aversion** the leading explanation, corroborating Genesove-Mayer/Bokhari-Geltner on a far longer sample. [2-SOURCE]. **This is the direct evidentiary basis for "busts show up first as collapsing volume, not price."**

## 6. Agent information asymmetry

**Levitt & Syverson (2008), "Market Distortions When Agents Are Better Informed," *Review of Economics and Statistics* 90(4), 599–611** (NBER WP 11053). Clean identification: compare an agent selling **someone else's** home (distorted incentive — small marginal commission share, full cost of holding out) against the same agent selling **their own** home (no distortion). Result: **agent-owned homes sell for ~3.7% more** and stay on market **~9.5 days longer** than comparable client-owned homes, controlling for characteristics. [2-SOURCE, exact figures corroborated across abstract and multiple citing records]. Direct quantification of the standard-commission agency wedge — relevant to discounting broker-quoted asking prices anywhere brokerage-mediated listings stand in for a market-price series (as in India, §7). No standalone Han-Strange paper titled specifically "the market for real estate agents" was separately confirmed beyond the Handbook chapter (§5); a narrower title should be treated as **[RECALL — unverified]**.

## 7. India-specific microstructure: registration data, circle rates, and the area-basis problem

This is the operational payoff: India **price** series are sparse, lagged, and area-basis-inconsistent, but registration/transaction **counts** are frequent, granular, and comparatively trustworthy.

**Registration counts as a near-real-time volume proxy.** Maharashtra's **Department of Registrations and Stamps (IGR)**, analyzed monthly by **Knight Frank India**, publishes Mumbai registration counts and stamp-duty totals within days of month-end. Recent prints: **May 2026 — 12,315 registrations, a 14-year high for the month, ₹1,051cr stamp duty**; **June 2026 — 13,302, +15% YoY**; **August 2026 — 12,503 projected, +11% YoY, 14-year high, ₹1,123cr (+12% YoY)**; **October 2025 — registrations fell 14% YoY**, showing the series moves both ways in near real time. [2-SOURCE, four independent outlets]. **December 2020's temporary stamp-duty cut produced Mumbai's highest single month of registrations in over a decade** — a clean, dated natural experiment on the **transaction-cost elasticity of registered volume**. [2-SOURCE].

**The circle-rate/market-value gap as a structural floor and cash-component signal.** States set **circle rates** (guidance/ready-reckoner values); stamp duty is charged on the **higher of declared price or circle rate**. Infrequent revision leaves circle rates **structurally below market** — reportedly **up to ~2× in parts of Mumbai**, **~30% in Delhi** [1-SOURCE, single explainer; confirm against a primary valuation-authority table before modeling]. This produces a well-known **cash-component practice**: white (registered) payment at/near circle rate, balance in cash. A **LocalCircles survey** reportedly found **~2 in 3 recent buyers admitted paying part in cash**, **~1 in 4 saying over half the deal was off-books** [1-SOURCE, self-selected online survey]. **Section 50C, Income Tax Act (via Finance Act 2002)**, deems the higher of circle rate or declared price as taxable consideration, specifically to curb under-declaration. [2-SOURCE for mechanics/purpose]. **Implication: registered transaction PRICE in India is a biased, downward-truncated signal with a state/city/time-varying bias; registration COUNT is a comparatively clean volume signal.**

**RERA's carpet-area mandate — a partial fix, primary-market and post-2017 only.** RERA (2016) requires developers to **quote and contract price per sqft on carpet area**, not super built-up area (which is always larger, making the same total price look cheaper per sqft); carpet area must be disclosed in marketing material and the sale deed, with **>3% variance a legal violation** carrying buyer recourse. [2-SOURCE]. It does **not** retroactively standardize the **resale market** or **pre-2017 stock**, where carpet/built-up/super-built-up are still quoted inconsistently. **Any India engine ingesting per-sqft series must carry an explicit, version-controlled area-basis field and RERA-status flag, and must never pool across that boundary** — per this brief, basis alone can move a reported figure an estimated 20–35% [**RECALL — unverified** as a calibrated constant, though directionally consistent with every carpet-vs-super-built-up source found].

**Unsold inventory / months-to-sell — India's directly available Wheaton-style state variable.** **Anarock** and **Liases Foras** publish a **"months of unsold inventory"** figure for top India cities — exactly the listings-to-sales-flow ratio Wheaton's model treats as driving time-on-market and reservation prices. Confirmed time series: **~55 months end-2020** (COVID trough) → **~32 months end-2021** → **five-year low ~20 months, Q1 2023** (Bengaluru lowest ~13 months, Hyderabad ~21, Pune ~20) → **rising again by Q1 2026**: unsold inventory **+7% YoY to 6+ lakh units**, with **new launches (126,265) outpacing sales (101,675)** — the metric turning upward, buyer leverage reportedly returning. **18–24 months is the conventional "healthy" benchmark.** [2-SOURCE across the full series, multiple independent outlets per period]. This is the single most directly usable India analogue to "listings-to-sales as a state variable," published with a short lag and already vendor-structured as a leading/coincident indicator.

**NHB RESIDEX — official but slow, and not a registration-price index.** Launched July 2007 (base year 2007); revamped index has **two legs**: **HPI@Assessment Prices** (bank/HFC loan-valuation data, 50 cities) and **HPI@Market Prices for Under-Construction Properties** (market survey of unsold primary stock, 50 cities); **Laspeyres methodology**, 4-quarter weighted moving average; classified by **carpet-area size bands** (≤60 sqm, 60–110 sqm, >110 sqm) in INR/sqft. [2-SOURCE]. Useful as a slow, low-frequency official benchmark; the IGR count series and the Anarock/Liases Foras inventory series are both timelier and, on the volume side, less basis-contaminated.

## 8. Caveats on this dossier's own evidence quality

Every citation rests on titles, journal/working-paper metadata, and search-engine abstract summaries, cross-checked across independent outlets where possible — no PDF was opened. A number appearing only once, or supplied by the search tool's own synthesized prose rather than a quoted abstract sentence, is tagged **[1-SOURCE]** and needs primary-source re-verification before use as a model parameter. The India circle-rate-gap percentages, the LocalCircles survey figures, and the 20–35% area-basis heuristic are the weakest-sourced numbers here; the IGR/Knight Frank registration counts and the Anarock/Liases Foras inventory series are the strongest (multiply corroborated, dated, internally consistent across years).

## WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Build a registration-COUNT-based volume/momentum layer as the primary India nowcast, not a price-based one.** Per Stein (1995) and the price-volume asymmetry literature, volume is the first-moving variable in a downturn; IGR-style monthly counts (proven for Mumbai, extendable state-by-state) are the highest-value India data asset this literature points to.

2. **Never pool registered transaction PRICES across states, circle-rate regimes, or time without a versioned gap-correction factor.** The bias is state/city-specific and shifts discretely whenever a circle rate is revised — log each revision as a dated breaks-registry item.

3. **Tag every India per-sqft figure with area basis (carpet/built-up/super-built-up) and RERA status (post-2017 primary vs. resale/pre-2017); never blend across that boundary.** This is probably the single highest-leverage, lowest-cost data-hygiene rule available.

4. **Wire Anarock/Liases Foras "months of unsold inventory" as a leading indicator for price direction and time-on-market**, not just a builder-health descriptor. The 55→32→20→rising path (2020–2026) is an existing calibration series; request vendor city-level time series (not just press releases) as a Priority pull.

5. **Expect loss aversion and equity/down-payment constraints to bind at least as tightly in India as in the US comparables** — mortgage LTV norms, a thin resale market, high transaction frictions (stamp duty + brokerage + capital-gains treatment) plausibly amplify both the Genesove-Mayer and Stein mechanisms — but this is **[RECALL — unverified]**: no India-specific loss-aversion study was found; a clearly registrable design once transaction-level data with holding-period and purchase-price fields exists.

6. **Map Chinco-Mayer (out-of-town speculators) and Bayer-Geissler-Mangum-Roberts (flippers vs. middlemen) onto India NRI and non-resident-city buyer flows.** Classify registration records by buyer-registered-address-vs-property-location distance, where available, to test the same worse-exit-timing signature — a novel, currently unrun, data-gated design.

7. **Downward nominal rigidity implies India busts will show up as stalled registration volume and rising unsold-inventory months well before any registered-price index falls.** This is the single most load-bearing behavioral fact for early-warning design, and why NHB RESIDEX (slow, quarterly, valuation-based) is the wrong instrument for that job while IGR/Anarock are the right ones.

8. **Discount broker/portal asking-price series per the Levitt-Syverson agency wedge (agent-owned homes sell ~3.7% higher, ~9.5 days slower) when such listings stand in for a market-price series**, as they often must in India given registered-price lag — direction implies ordinary client-side asking prices may run slightly hot relative to true reservation value; US magnitude, India size unverified.
