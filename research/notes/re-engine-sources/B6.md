## 1. The long-run academic panels

### 1.1 Knoll, Schularick & Steger (2017) — the hockey stick and the land decomposition

**Katharina Knoll, Moritz Schularick & Thomas Steger, "No Price Like Home: Global House Prices,
1870-2012," *American Economic Review* 107(2), 2017, pp. 331-353** [RECALL — unverified].
Fourteen advanced economies with a continuous 1870-2012 run — recalled list: Australia,
Belgium, Canada, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, Norway, Spain, Sweden, UK,
US. Partial cross-check (not search): JST's 18-country superset (1.2) includes exactly four
countries whose `hpnom` starts too late — Finland (1905), Switzerland (1901), Ireland (1945),
Portugal (1988) — to plausibly belong to a "1870-2012" paper, which raises confidence the
remaining 14 are KSS's actual sample.

**The hockey stick**: real house prices were essentially **trendless from 1870 to WWII**, then
**turned up sharply from ~1950, accelerating after ~1970**. Full-sample (1870-2012) average real
appreciation is the famous **close to 1%/year** figure the brief flags — widely repeated in
secondary commentary, moderate-high confidence. The 1950-onward sub-sample average is materially
higher, recalled as roughly **1.5-2%/year real** — digit not trusted past one significant figure.

**The land-price decomposition — the most India-relevant finding**: KSS combine house prices
with construction-cost indices to back out an implied land-price series (house price =
structure cost + land value). Real construction costs grew slowly, tracking near-stagnant
construction-sector productivity; **land prices did essentially all the postwar and
post-1970 work** — recalled as roughly a **six-fold real rise in land since 1950**, with land's
share of total home value rising from roughly a third toward half or more by the 2000s in
several countries (approximate, not an exact reported statistic). Mechanism: rising land
**scarcity** from urbanization meeting inelastic land-use regimes (echoing Glaeser-Gyourko
housing-supply-elasticity work), not rising construction cost. This decomposition was picked up
by the capital-share literature (Section 3) as the mechanism behind Piketty's (2014) rising
capital/income ratio.

### 1.2 Jordà-Schularick-Taylor Macrohistory Database, Release 6 — [VAULT-VERIFIED]

Opened directly this session: **18 countries, annual, 1870-2020** — Australia, Belgium, Canada,
Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Japan, Netherlands, Norway, Portugal, Spain,
Sweden, Switzerland, UK, USA. Descended from, and overlapping, the KSS house-price collection,
extended through 2020.

**Fields confirmed by direct read of `Legend` and `JRT6 Data`**: `hpnom` (nominal house-price
index, 1990=100) — **no `hpreal` field**; real series must be built by deflating with `cpi`
(also in the panel). Per-country `hpnom` starts: 1870 (Australia/France/Germany/Netherlands/
Norway); 1875 (Denmark/Sweden); 1890 (US); 1899 (UK); 1901 (Switzerland); 1905 (Finland); 1913
(Japan); 1921 (Canada); 1945 (Ireland); 1970 (Italy); 1971 (Spain); 1988 (Portugal). All run to
2020. `housing_capgain`, `housing_rent_rtn`, `housing_rent_yd` (rental yield), `housing_tr`
(total return), with interpolation flags for wartime gaps — **exactly 16 of 18 countries carry
this full decomposition; Canada and Ireland are the two missing** (confirmed by a direct
per-country presence check on `housing_tr`). This 16-country subset is what the related
Jordà-Knoll-Kuvshinov-Schularick-Taylor return-on-everything work (1.3) uses. Adjacent fields:
`crisisJST` (systemic-crisis dummy, all 18, full span), `debtgdp`, `eq_tr`/`eq_dp` (equity
return/yield, same 16-country footprint), `capital_tr`/`risky_tr`/`safe_tr` (aggregate wealth
returns). **No India, no China** — confirmed directly; an advanced-economy panel only.

### 1.3 JKKST, "The Rate of Return on Everything, 1870-2015"

[RECALL — unverified] *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2019 (working-paper predecessor
earlier). Consumes the JST housing-return fields (16 countries) alongside equity/bond/bill
series to compute total, not just capital-gain, real returns across asset classes. Headline
recalled at moderate-high confidence: **housing delivered total real returns comparable to or
exceeding equities (roughly ~7%/yr real, panel average) with materially lower volatility** —
rental income, not price appreciation, does most of the work, and rents are much less volatile
than either house or equity prices. Also documents the safe real rate (r) as far more volatile
and more downward-trending than commonly assumed, feeding the Piketty r>g debate. Held at lower
confidence than 1.1/1.2 — not re-read this session, exact figures could be conflated with
adjacent work.

### 1.4 The Amsterdam Herengracht Index and its extensions

**Piet Eichholtz, "A Long Run House Price Index: The Herengracht Index, 1628-1973," *Real
Estate Economics*, 1997** [RECALL — unverified]. A repeat-sales index from centuries of
transaction records for one Amsterdam canal, ~345 years. The most extreme long-run demonstration
of the "housing is not a great real appreciator" thesis, predating KSS by two decades: full-span
real appreciation recalled as **close to zero** — the index instead shows enormous multi-decade
swings (Golden Age boom, 18th-century multi-generational decline, 19th-20th century recovery)
around a flat trend, not compounding growth. **Brent Ambrose, Piet Eichholtz & Thies Lindenthal,
"House Prices and Fundamentals: 355 Years of Evidence," *Journal of Money, Credit and Banking*,
c. 2013** [RECALL — unverified] extends the series with matching long-run rent data and tests
price-rent mean reversion; recalled as supportive of eventual reversion but only at **multi-
decade half-lives** — a caution against any short-horizon India valuation-timing rule.

### 1.5 Shiller's long US series

Shiller's US real house-price index (*Irrational Exuberance*, 2005, updated ongoing; spliced
onto Case-Shiller — see B1) [RECALL — unverified] extends to **1890**, annual pre-modern. US
real house prices were **flat/cyclical 1890 to the mid-1990s**, then an unprecedented runup
1996-2006. A third, independent (US-only) confirmation of the same shape KSS finds across 14
countries and Eichholtz across 350 Amsterdam years: **absent an identifiable structural break,
long-run real appreciation is close to zero to ~1%/yr**, far below the ~5-7%/yr many investors
assume from lived experience of the post-1996 or post-1950 eras.

## 2. The standing institutional data products

Maintained, ongoing series — useful for a live nowcast, unlike the one-off academic
compilations above. All of Section 2 is **[RECALL — unverified]**, including any URL fragment
(none was seen in a snippet this session, so none is stated as confirmed).

**2.1 BIS Residential Property Price Statistics.** A "long" series for ~20-ish countries
(generally to the 1970s, methodologically consistent, continuously maintained) plus a broader
**"selected" quarterly series covering ~60 economies**, start dates varying by national capacity.
Free, no login, quarterly, one-to-two quarter lag. BIS mostly does **not** publish its own real
(CPI-deflated) variant — users deflate themselves. **India**: moderate confidence BIS's
"selected" set carries an Indian series, most likely the **RBI's quarterly All-India House
Price Index** (roughly ten cities, sourced from registration authorities, starting ~2010, base
2010-11=100) rather than NHB's older Residex — not confident which, flagged **[VERIFY: BIS
India source, exact start date, city list]**. **China**: moderate-high confidence BIS carries a
Chinese series, almost certainly NBS 70-city — this desk's China Property Crash Atlas (row 64)
already documents that source's smoothing/under-reporting caveats; not re-derived here.

**2.2 Dallas Fed International House Price Database (Mack & Martinez-Garcia).** "A Cross-
Country Quarterly Database of Real House Prices: A Methodological Note," Federal Reserve Bank
of Dallas Globalization and Monetary Policy Institute WP (number/year approximate, ~WP99, c.
2011). Coverage on the order of 20-plus advanced/liquid economies (possibly expanded later);
quarterly nominal AND real (CPI-deflated) indices plus price-to-income (and likely
price-to-rent) ratios, explicitly correcting for cross-national methodology heterogeneity.
Free, Excel, quarterly with ~1-quarter lag; historical depth short (1970s-1990s starts mostly).
Lean toward **India/China NOT included** in the original vintage — flagged **[VERIFY]**, not
asserted.

**2.3 OECD Analytical House Price Indicators.** Nominal/real indices plus normalized
price-to-income and price-to-rent ratios (each indexed to its own long-run average = 100, an
explicit over/undervaluation frame) for OECD members, quarterly, longest series to the 1970s-80s.
Free. India/China **probably absent** (non-members) — flagged **[VERIFY]**. Chief value to the
India engine: a ready-made normalization *methodology* to imitate, independent of whether India
is in the panel.

**2.4 IMF Global Housing Watch / Global House Price Index.** Aggregates house-price and related
indicators (price-to-income, price-to-rent, household credit/GDP) for ~60 countries, mostly
re-publishing BIS/OECD/Eurostat/national sources rather than primary collection; a quarterly
Global House Price Index (weighting basis — GDP-PPP vs population — not confidently recalled).
Free. Given ~60-country EM-inclusive breadth: **moderate confidence both India and China are
included** (via the same BIS/RBI and NBS feeds as 2.1) — flagged **[VERIFY]**. Value: a fast
cross-check/aggregation layer, not a primary India source.

**2.5 Eurostat House Price Index.** EU-harmonized quarterly HPI, full harmonization mostly from
the mid-2000s. Free. No India/China relevance; useful only as the source underlying the Irish/
Spanish 2007-2013 busts already inside the JST panel.

## 3. The capital-share literature: Rognlie vs. Piketty

**Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, Harvard University Press, 2014**
[RECALL — unverified]: rising aggregate capital/income ratio and capital share across rich
countries since the 1970s-80s, the r>g argument. **Matthew Rognlie, "Deciphering the Fall and
Rise in the Net Capital Share," *Brookings Papers on Economic Activity*, Spring 2015** [RECALL —
unverified]: decomposing Piketty's series, the entire rise is attributable **almost exclusively
to housing** — rising land/house value relative to income and a rising net rental-income share
— while the net capital share of **non-housing** reproducible capital stayed roughly flat once
depreciation is netted out. This is the macro/national-accounts confirmation of KSS's own
micro/land-price decomposition (1.1): both isolate **land**, not capital broadly, as the asset
whose scarcity price rose. A narrower, lower-confidence complement: **Morris Davis & Jonathan
Heathcote, "The Price and Quantity of Residential Land in the United States," *Journal of
Monetary Economics*, c. 2007** [RECALL — unverified] — a US-specific residential land-value
series showing the same rising land share.

## 4. Base rates: drawdowns, frequency, duration, and the ~1%/yr anchor

All of Section 4 is **[RECALL — unverified]** — the section a later auditor should re-verify
first, since the engine leans on it hardest.

- **Long-run appreciation anchor**: KSS's ~1%/yr is independently echoed by Shiller's US
  1890-1996 flat period and the Herengracht 350-year series (closer to zero) — three
  geographically and methodologically independent series converge: **absent a specific
  structural break, real house prices are not a reliably compounding asset**; "housing always
  goes up ~5-7%/yr real" is a recency artifact of the post-1950 (or post-1996 US) window.

- **Classic bust base rate (IMF WEO, April 2003, Ch. II, "When Bubbles Burst")** [RECALL,
  moderate confidence]: ~20 advanced economies, 1970-2002, ~20 identified housing busts
  averaging roughly **-30% real peak-to-trough over ~4 years** — versus a typical equity bust
  of **larger decline (~45%) but shorter duration (~2.5 years)**. Housing busts are less
  frequent, fall less, take longer, and are followed by deeper, more prolonged output/credit
  damage (banking-system leverage).

- **Cross-country boom-bust dating (Agnello & Schuknecht, *Journal of Housing Economics*,
  2011)** [RECALL — unverified]: ~20 OECD countries, 1970-2010; cycle length on the order of
  **once every 10-13 years per country** (echoing the classic ~15-20yr Kuznets/long-swing
  housing cycle), bust amplitude **-20% to -30%** real for the more severe episodes.

- **Frequency of 20%+ real declines per country-decade**: no single citable number exists in my
  recall; stating a precise percentage would be fabrication. Best-effort synthesis across the
  above: roughly **one country-decade in six to ten** sees a 20%+ real decline — most countries
  see at least one serious housing downturn roughly every 30-50 years, a **generational** event
  (which is exactly why buyers systematically underprice the risk — the last one predates most
  active buyers' adult memory). This is a synthesis, not a reported statistic — a natural,
  zero-new-pull follow-up would recompute it directly from the vaulted JST `hpnom`/CPI series.

- **Modern severe anchors**: US Case-Shiller national index fell roughly **-27% nominal / -33
  to -35% real, peak 2006 to trough 2012** (~5-6y); Ireland **-50 to -55% real, 2007-~2013**,
  among the worst modern episodes; Spain **-35 to -45% real** over a similar window. All three
  sit inside the JST panel already and could be recomputed exactly, at zero pull cost.

## 6. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE INDIA ENGINE

1. **Do not import a ~5-7%/yr real appreciation assumption from folklore.** KSS, Shiller-US and
   Herengracht converge near 0-1%/yr long-run real; any India forecast above that needs its own
   explicit justification, not an assumed universal housing premium.

2. **The KSS/Rognlie land-scarcity mechanism is the right lens for India, not the wrong one**:
   India's post-1991/2000s urbanization + metro land-use constraint + RERA formalization looks
   like the rich world's own 1950-1970 structural break, not a reversion to a flat mean. Any
   India valuation percentile should distinguish trend-break from mean-reversion explicitly.

3. **Size the India stress scenario off IMF-WEO/Agnello-Schuknecht base rates (-20% to -30%
   real, 4-5yr duration)**, not a domestic anecdote — India has no own multi-decade bust in the
   free-data record yet, so the cross-country base rate is the only defensible prior.

4. **The 16-country JST `housing_tr`/`housing_rent_yd` fields are already vaulted and unused** —
   this desk could compute its own exact 20%+-decline frequency, peak-to-trough distribution,
   and housing-vs-equity total-return comparison directly from `JSTdatasetR6.xlsx` today, at
   zero new pull cost, replacing Section 4's recall-based synthesis with a real print.

5. **Any India price the engine ingests needs an explicit area-basis tag** (carpet/built-up/
   super-built-up/plot) — none of the international series above face this ambiguity, so a
   naive India-vs-panel comparison risks a 20-35% basis error before any real difference exists.

6. **BIS's India series (RBI HPI, most likely) is the free, standing, already-open input to
   prioritize for a live India valuation percentile** — quarterly, ~15yr history; resolving its
   exact source/city list is a cheap [VERIFY], not a new RUNSHEET pull.

7. **Treat OECD/IMF price-to-income/price-to-rent normalization (index to own long-run average
   = 100) as a template to imitate for India**, not a dataset to join — consistent with
   CONTRACT.md's own no-magic-number, provenanced-quantile-rank discipline.

8. **The Ambrose-Eichholtz-Lindenthal multi-decade reversion half-life cautions against any
   short-horizon India valuation-timing rule** — even the best-documented 350-year price-rent
   series reverts only over decades, reinforcing this desk's own ER-arc conclusion (valuation as
   a state, never a point forecast); size any India housing-valuation signal Tier-C/narrative
   until a much longer domestic series exists.
