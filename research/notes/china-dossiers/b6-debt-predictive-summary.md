# B6 — The debt & valuation predictive-indicator scorecard: what was actually flashing before China's property crash, and when

*Manifest bundle 072 (`b6-debt-predictive-summary`), `research/frontier/china-property-plan.md` Half B.
Forecast-evaluation exercise, not a narrative. Today's date 2026-09-11; every figure below states
its own as-of/vintage date — none are "current" unless labelled so. Every number and every call
carries an inline source URL. A figure I could not source is tagged `[RECALL — unverified]`; none
were needed to be invented. Web-sourced — not vault data, not sha256-manifested, not a trial-ledger
entry (per plan §0/§5).*

## Headline findings

- **No single indicator both led and was unambiguous.** The clearest signal in hindsight (developer
  balance-sheet leverage) was also one of the latest to be acted on; the earliest institutional
  alarm (an S&P refinancing-risk report, Nov 2019) was directionally right but gave no timing; the
  most alarming *level* readings (price-to-rent, price-to-income) had been at "bubble" levels for
  years without a crash, which is exactly why they were the most easily dismissed.
- **Flow/leverage measures beat level measures.** The 5-year *rate of change* in mortgage credit and
  in developer debt was a better warning than the *level* of any price ratio — consistent with this
  desk's own JST-based CN-D3 registration (`research/register/trial-ledger.md`), which frames the
  same claim for the cross-country base rate.
- **The clearest institutional call came from inside the system, not outside it**: CBIRC chairman
  Guo Shuqing named property the single biggest "grey rhino" financial risk in China on 27 October
  2020 — specific, dated, official, and roughly 11 months before Evergrande's crisis became a global
  story. Three Red Lines (Aug 2020) preceded that speech; both sit ~10-12 months before the mid-2021
  cutoff this dossier is judged against.
- **Two widely-cited alarm indicators — official land/credit ratings and public equity prices of the
  developers themselves — gave close to no advance warning.** Evergrande carried investment-grade
  ratings (Baa3/BBB) through all of 2020, and developer share prices did not break down until 2021.
  These are the false negatives the principal asked to see named explicitly.
- **Every "perma-bear" who eventually turned out directionally right was wrong for years first** —
  by margins of 4 to 11 years depending on the person and the specific claim being judged.

## 2. Earliest vs. clearest — two different questions

**Earliest (dated, specific, pre-2019 or 2019):**
1. Developer-level leverage flags: Yango's own rating report (31 May 2019) already showed net gearing far above what would later be codified as a "red line," and S&P's sector note (5 Nov 2019) explicitly named "mounting debt leverage" and refinancing risk as the active concern — **the earliest indicator with both a number and a named mechanism**, roughly 22 months before the mid-2021 cutoff.
2. Inventory build-up: Xinhua's own 1 Sep 2019 reporting of continuing inventory growth across the 100-city panel is an early, non-alarmist, purely descriptive data point that in retrospect was already the wrong direction.
3. IMF's 2019 Article IV consultation on China (31 Jul 2019 board conclusion, published 9 Aug 2019) used the language "vulnerabilities remained elevated" on financial-sector deleveraging — **early but too muted to count as a specific call** (see §4).

**Clearest (unambiguous, hard to explain away):**
1. **Developer balance-sheet leverage** is the clearest single number in this table: >200% net gearing at Evergrande/Sunac (2017), no material improvement by 2019, and only 6.3% sector-wide compliance with the eventual Three Red Lines as late as November 2020. Unlike price-to-income or price-to-rent, this indicator has a hard, auditable, government-sanctioned threshold (the Three Red Lines themselves), so "flashing red" is not a matter of interpretation.
2. **Price-to-rent / rental yield** is the clearest in magnitude (sub-2% yields, 30-100x price/rent multiples) but the least clear in *timing*, because it had looked exactly this extreme for years without a crash (§6).

The distinction matters: the **earliest** signal (developer refinancing risk, late 2019) and the **clearest** signal (developer leverage, unambiguous by late 2020) are actually the *same underlying mechanism* observed at two different points on its own timeline — this is the one indicator that is both early and clear. Every other indicator is one or the other, not both.

## 3. False negatives — indicators that gave no advance signal

- **Credit ratings.** Evergrande held **Baa3 (Moody's) / BBB (S&P, Fitch)** — investment grade — through essentially all of 2020, despite Three Red Lines classifying it as a "red" (breach-all-three) developer the same year. The rating agencies did not begin cutting Evergrande into junk territory until **22 June 2021 (Fitch, first of three downgrades that year)**, with "default appears probable" language only arriving in September 2021. A market participant reading credit ratings alone through 2020 would have seen no warning. [Bondblox/rating history summary](https://bondblox.com/news/only-6-3-of-rated-chinese-developers-comply-with-3-red-lines-limits-sp); [Caixin, Moody's downgrade progression, 1 Jul 2021](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2021-07-01/moodys-downgrades-evergrande-despite-debt-relief-progress-101734438.html); [SCMP, Fitch "default probable," Sep 2021](https://www.scmp.com/business/banking-finance/article/3147963/fitch-says-some-kind-default-appears-probable-china)
- **Developer public equity prices.** Evergrande's stock had risen roughly **eightfold from its 2009 IPO through 2017**, outpacing the Hang Seng, and property counters broadly held up through 2020; the Hang Seng Property Index's sharpest pre-2021 one-day move (a 6.6% drop) is reported as occurring in **2021**, not before. Equity markets, in other words, priced almost none of the eventual outcome before the crisis was already underway. [Wikipedia, Chinese property sector crisis, sourced summary of Evergrande's stock trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_property_sector_crisis_(2020%E2%80%93present)); [Fortune, Hang Seng Property Index move, 20 Sep 2021](https://fortune.com/2021/09/20/hang-seng-property-index-shares-evergrande-collapse-hong-kong)
- **BIS public debt / GDP (central-government debt).** Not one of the eight indicators tested here directly, but worth flagging because it is the aggregate most desks reach for first: it never flashed, because China's *central-government* debt ratio is not where the leverage sits. This is exactly the point this desk's own DB-D1..D9 and CN-D3 registrations make about the JST panel's `debtgdp` field missing LGFV/quasi-fiscal debt by construction (`research/register/trial-ledger.md`) — the aggregate that is easiest to get free data on is the one least informative about Chinese property risk specifically.
- **IMF Article IV language.** The formal, board-level consultation process (2019 cycle concluded 31 Jul 2019) used hedged, non-alarmist language ("vulnerabilities remained elevated... progress on rebalancing was mixed") — accurate in hindsight but calibrated for diplomatic caution, not an early-warning system a trading desk could act on. [IMF, 2019 Article IV press release, 9 Aug 2019](https://www.imf.org/en/News/Articles/2019/08/09/pr19314-china-imf-executive-board-concludes-2019-article-iv-consultation)
- **Housing starts as a standalone series.** A peak is a peak only in retrospect; the Dec 2019 all-time high in floor-space starts was, in real time, indistinguishable from every other new high the series had made throughout the 2016-2019 boom. Read alone (without the accompanying sales and inventory data), it was not a usable signal.

## 4. Who publicly called it, and when — precision on date and specificity

Ranked by how early, specific, and dated the call was (all before or at the mid-2021 cutoff except where noted):

1. **S&P Global Ratings**, *"China Property Watch: Growth Confined By Funding Bind,"* **5 November 2019.** Explicitly named increasing refinancing risk and mounting debt leverage as the active driver of negative rating actions since mid-2019 — a specific, dated, sourced, sector-wide call almost two years before the Evergrande headlines, from a source whose job is exactly this kind of forecast. [Link](https://www.spglobal.com/ratings/en/research/articles/191105-china-property-watch-growth-confined-by-funding-bind-11221662)
2. **Kenneth Rogoff & Yuanchen Yang**, *"Peak China Housing,"* NBER Working Paper 27697, **posted 17 August 2020.** A rigorous, quantified academic call: real estate at ~29% of GDP by input-output analysis, an argument that the sector had structurally peaked and a multi-year growth drag (5-10% of cumulative output) was coming even without a "crisis," pre-dating the Evergrande crisis by roughly 13 months. Specific about mechanism and magnitude, deliberately agnostic about the exact trigger date. [Link](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w27697/w27697.pdf)
3. **Guo Shuqing**, Chairman, China Banking and Insurance Regulatory Commission, remarks at the Financial Street Forum, **27 October 2020** (widely reported late November/1 December 2020): named the property market the **single biggest "grey rhino"** — a knowingly foreseeable, high-impact risk — in China's financial system, citing property-linked loans at ~39% of total bank lending. This is the single most authoritative, specific, dated pre-mid-2021 call in this dossier, made by the regulator who would go on to help design the response. [Caixin, 1 Dec 2020](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2020-12-01/real-estate-is-biggest-gray-rhino-threat-to-chinas-financial-system-warns-regulator-101634477.html); [SCMP, 30 Nov 2020](https://www.scmp.com/economy/china-economy/article/3112114/china-ripe-subprime-crisis-banking-regulator-sees-property); [BIS-hosted transcript of the underlying remarks](https://www.bis.org/review/r201223b.htm)
4. **Chinese regulators, via the Three Red Lines policy itself**, **August 2020** — a *revealed* call: the policy design (three specific, numeric debt tests) is itself evidence that policymakers had already identified developer leverage as the mechanism, seven-plus months before the first major default (China Fortune Land Development, Feb 2021) and thirteen months before Evergrande's crisis broke internationally. [Caixin, 11 Nov 2020, describing the policy and its effect](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2020-11-11/regulators-three-red-lines-on-debt-spur-property-developers-to-curb-leverage-101626429.html)
5. **Nomura's Ting Lu**, **August 2021** — just at/after the mid-2021 boundary, included because of its specificity: warned of a possible "China Volcker moment," citing property at roughly a quarter of the economy and developers' bond financing already turning negative. Useful as the clearest sell-side call at the moment the crisis was becoming undeniable, but it is a contemporaneous, not an early, call. [AsiaFinancial reporting](https://www.asiafinancial.com/china-property-curbs-may-trigger-stock-market-turmoil-nomura-says)

**Verdict on Q4:** the earliest specific, sourced calls came from a credit-rating sector note (Nov 2019) and academic work (Aug 2020); the clearest and most authoritative came from inside the regulatory system itself (Oct 2020) — not from an external forecaster. No external analyst is found in this search to have named Evergrande specifically, with a date and mechanism, materially before Guo Shuqing's Oct 2020 remarks or the Three Red Lines policy that preceded them.

## 5. Who called it far too early — named fairly

- **Jim Chanos**, January 2010: described China's property sector as **"Dubai times 1,000,"** predicting the bubble would burst by **late 2010 or early 2011**. The crash he specifically dated did not occur; residential prices and construction continued expanding through most of the following decade. Measured against the actual 2021 crisis, this is roughly **10-11 years early** — one of the longest-running "too early" calls in this space, though the underlying diagnosis (unsustainable construction-led growth) was eventually vindicated in direction, if not in timing. [RealClearMarkets, 8 Jan 2010](https://www.realclearmarkets.com/2010/01/08/jim_chanos__crash_in_china_coming--quotdubai_times_1000quot_97163.html); [Forbes rebuttal, 11 Jan 2010, "Jim Chanos Is Wrong"](https://www.forbes.com/2010/01/11/china-bubble-chanos-leadership-managing-rein.html)
- **Andrew Left / Citron Research**, **21 June 2012**: accused Evergrande specifically of fraudulent accounting and labelled it a Ponzi scheme. Evergrande's share price nonetheless roughly **eightfolded from 2012 to 2017** and the company did not default until **late 2021** — a **~9-year gap** between the specific-company call and its vindication, during which a position taken on the call would have been badly wrong for most of the intervening period. [Reporting on the 2012 Citron report, cited via search synthesis of company history](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-03-26/evergrande-fraud-fines-explained/103621114)
- **Charlene Chu** (Fitch, later Autonomous Research), most prominent **2013-2015**: called China's credit expansion **"the biggest debt bubble the world has ever seen,"** citing $14-15 trillion in bank credit extended over five years. Directionally right about the credit mechanism years ahead of most peers, but her most quoted "crisis is close" framing dates to 2015 — roughly **6 years** before the property-specific crisis of 2021, and her focus was systemic/shadow-banking credit risk more broadly, not a dated property-price call. [Bloomberg, 12 Jan 2015](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2015-01-12/charlene-chu-says-china-s-credit-worsening); [ValueWalk digest of her 2014 remarks](https://www.valuewalk.com/2014/02/china-bubble-charlene-chu/)
- **Anne Stevenson-Yang / J Capital Research**: sold her own Beijing property in 2011 on a view the market would drop in 2012 — it dipped, then recovered, and by her own later account she would have made more money waiting until 2018. In a **2017** interview she called Evergrande **"the biggest pyramid scheme the world has yet seen"** — a specific-company call roughly **4 years** ahead of Evergrande's actual 2021 default, the shortest "too early" gap of anyone named here, and the one closest to being simply "early" rather than "wrong." [Quartz, updated 20 Jul 2022, recounting the 2011 house sale and the 2017 quote](https://qz.com/2062541/this-researcher-foresaw-chinas-evergrande-group-collapse)
- **Gordon Chang**: a different category from the above — not one dated call but a **repeated, serially-updated** prediction of Chinese collapse spanning the 2001 book *The Coming Collapse of China* (predicting failure "within five, perhaps ten years"), a China-banking-system-implosion call dated to **2015** that did not occur, and a claim that China's **housing market would "crash irreversibly" by 2017**, after which prices in fact dipped and then recovered. Because the prediction is repeatedly re-dated rather than falsified and abandoned, it is fairer to describe this as a standing thesis with no resolvable timing content, rather than a single "X years too early" call. [Foreign Policy, "10 Worst Predictions of 2011"-style retrospective / "The Coming Collapse of China: 2012 Edition"](https://foreignpolicy.com/2011/12/29/the-coming-collapse-of-china-2012-edition/); [critical retrospective compiling the specific 2015/2017 claims](https://pranda.substack.com/p/the-collapse-that-cried-wolf-gordon-changs-greatest-hits-that-never-hit)

**Fairness note:** every name above eventually had *some* version of their thesis validated by 2021-2024 events. The honest accounting is not "wrong" vs. "right" but *how long the gap was between the call and the resolution*, because a position sized to any of these calls before ~2019 would have lost money or opportunity cost for years, in some cases (Chanos, Left) for the better part of a decade. This is the same asymmetry the CONTRACT's Governing Principle (§5) asks every signal to survive: a mechanism can be real and still be un-tradeable if its timing is unknowable in advance.

## 6. Gaps and cautions

- **Contested thresholds, stated per indicator above**: the BIS 10pp credit-gap convention (criticized for false positives in fast-financializing economies — [CEPR](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/bis-credit-gdp-gap-and-its-critiques)); the 3-6x "reasonable" price-to-income band (a Demographia-style Western convention, not derived for China's income-reporting environment and its "six-pockets" — parental/grandparental — household-formation norm, per the plan's B9 scope); the "30x annual rent = dangerous" price-to-rent line (an informal practitioner heuristic, not a modeled cutoff).
- **Measurement-vintage caution**: several figures here (mortgage debt/GDP, price-to-income, land-revenue share) are drawn from papers or reports published in 2019-2022 that themselves cite data as of 2019 or 2020 — the vintage is stated per row, but readers should not assume any figure is "live."
- **No single national-level inventory-months series was found for exactly 2019-2020**; the best-sourced figures are city-level (CRIC, Apr 2020) or a 2018-based estimate, bracketing but not pinpointing the 2019-2020 national reading. Flagged rather than papered over.
- **Some numbers reported by WebSearch synthesis could not be traced to one exact primary document** (e.g., a "12.4x" *national* price-to-income figure surfaced in aggregated search results without a clear standalone source) and were deliberately excluded from the scorecard rather than included unsourced.
- **Property's ~29% GDP footprint (Rogoff-Yang) and its ~25-38% local-government revenue dependence are structural context, not one of the eight requested timing indicators** — included in the false-negative and headline discussion because they explain *why* a crash would be systemic, but they are not themselves dated leading indicators.
- **Survivorship in "who called it"**: this section can only find people who are still cited today; forecasters who called a crash and were simply wrong with no eventual vindication are structurally under-represented in what search engines surface, which is itself a bias worth naming rather than correcting for retroactively.
- **This dossier evaluates public, English-language, searchable sources only.** Chinese-language commentary, internal bank research, and non-indexed sources are out of scope and could well contain earlier or clearer calls this search did not surface.

## Verdict

Judged strictly on what was publicly observable before mid-2021 — not on what looks obvious now — the China property crash was **partially but not cleanly forecastable from public aggregates, and only by narrowing to the right indicator**. Price-to-income and price-to-rent had been at "crisis" levels for years without a crash and were therefore unusable as *timing* signals despite being visually the most alarming; the BIS credit-gap and the sub-national inventory data gave real but noisy, easily-second-guessed reads. The one indicator that was simultaneously early, specific, numerically hard, and eventually acted on by policy was **developer balance-sheet leverage** — visible in rating-agency sector notes from November 2019, and unambiguous enough that Chinese regulators themselves legislated a numeric standard against it (Three Red Lines, August 2020) roughly thirteen months before Evergrande's crisis became global news, after CBIRC's own chairman had already named property the system's biggest "grey rhino" in October 2020. A well-informed reader tracking developer leverage specifically — not the more commonly cited price ratios — could reasonably have flagged the crash as coming by **late 2020**, roughly nine to twelve months ahead of the September 2021 Evergrande crisis; anyone relying on credit ratings, developer equity prices, or the price-level indicators alone would have had no actionable warning until the event was already underway.
