## Headline findings

- **Every tracked Grade-A office market in the four tier-1 cities is running above its 2019
  vacancy level, and three of the four (Shanghai, Shenzhen, Guangzhou) are at or near their
  highest vacancy readings on record** `[2-SOURCE]` — Shenzhen is the tier-1 worst at
  **~26-30%** (sources disagree on the exact current print, see City Table), Shanghai is at a
  **"two-decade high" of ~23.6%**, and Guangzhou has gone from a resilient ~5-11% in 2020-early
  2025 to **~20.7-21.2%** by end-2025 — only Beijing stays under 20% (~16-17%), and even Beijing's
  own vacancy has been rising for five straight years, not falling `[2-SOURCE]`.
- **Tier-2 vacancy is often WORSE than tier-1, not better**: Wuhan printed **40.6% (Q2 2025) rising
  to 43.2% (Q1 2026)** and Chengdu **28.8%**, while Hangzhou is the genuine outlier at **under
  10-12%** `[2-SOURCE]` — the folk "tier-1 is worse" framing does not hold city-by-city. No single
  source explicitly crowns one city "the world's highest," but **Changchun (~43.6-44%, per a 2021
  JLL white paper still cited in 2023 reporting) and Wuhan (~40-43%, 2025-2026)** both sit far
  above the highest reading found for any non-China market in this search pass (San Francisco
  ~26-28%) — an inference from comparing separately-defined trackers, not a single sourced claim,
  flagged accordingly `[1-SOURCE each, inference not directly sourced]`.
- **Rents are down materially in nominal terms at every tier-1 city, roughly -20% to -34%
  cumulative since 2019** on this dossier's own (Colliers-2019-baseline vs current CBRE/JLL)
  calculation — Guangzhou's decline is the steepest and most recently accelerating: **-10.1% in
  2025 alone, its steepest drop since 2010** `[2-SOURCE]` per CBRE/Caixin.
- **Cap rates present a genuine, unresolved source conflict** — the best-attributed figure
  (Cushman & Wakefield, cited independently by two search passes) has Beijing/Shanghai CORE
  Grade-A cap rates **stable at 4.2-4.8% through 2024**, direction RISING (yields expanding,
  values falling) even as most of Asia-Pacific compresses `[2-SOURCE]`; a lower-quality aggregator
  (Mordor Intelligence) separately claims yields have "reset to ~6.2%" from "sub-5% peaks in
  2019" — internally inconsistent with its own 2019 claim once set against the 4.2-4.8% figure,
  and NOT used as the dossier's headline number. **No clean, well-attributed 2019 baseline cap
  rate was found** — named as a real gap against the task's explicit ask.
- **Foreign capital's office-specific footprint has gone from buyer to seller since the 2019-2020
  cycle peak**: multiple named BlackRock/Standard Chartered/Blackstone disposals at 34-42%
  discounts to purchase price, only one (date-ambiguous) Brookfield office acquisition found in
  the same window, and — the cleanest single directional fact in this dossier — **domestic buyers
  accounted for ALL of Beijing's and 92% of Shanghai's major-property deals in H1 2026**
  `[1-SOURCE, Caixin/CBRE]`.
- **Whole-building transaction volume roughly halved from its ~RMB200bn (2020) level to
  ~RMB134bn (2024), then rebounded sharply — Beijing +50% YoY and Shanghai +18% YoY in H1 2025,
  and nationwide +76.2% YoY to RMB121.2bn in H1 2026 alone** — but the buyer base that rebuilt
  the market is insurers, state-owned platforms and self-use corporates, not the foreign funds
  that drove the prior cycle `[2-SOURCE on the shape; individual year figures mostly 1-SOURCE,
  see §9]`.

## Q3. Office cap rate / net initial yield: now vs pre-crash

**This question has the dossier's largest genuine source conflict.** The best-attributed figure —
Cushman & Wakefield, corroborated by two independent search passes (one via the sibling
`c-assetclass-core.md` dossier's own citation, one via a fresh Chinese-language search in this
pass) — has Beijing/Shanghai **CORE Grade-A office cap rates at 4.2-4.8%, stable through 2024**,
with non-core assets in the same cities pricing 30-50bps higher (cheaper) `[2-SOURCE]`. Direction:
**cap rates are RISING (yields expanding, capital values falling) in Greater China even as most of
the rest of Asia-Pacific is compressing** — a genuine regional outlier, corroborated by CBRE's own
cap-rate-survey language plus a separate Chinese capitalization-rate survey both describing
sellers' price expectations falling `[2-SOURCE]`.

Separately, Mordor Intelligence (a generic market-research aggregator, lower editorial standard
than a named brokerage survey) states that "the re-pricing wave is resetting yields closer to
6.2% for Grade A core CBD assets compared with sub-5% peaks in 2019." **This is presented but NOT
adopted as the headline** for two reasons: (a) it is `[1-SOURCE]` and from a lower-quality
aggregator; (b) it is internally hard to reconcile with the C&W figure — if 2019 was genuinely
"sub-5%" and now is genuinely 4.2-4.8% (also sub-5%), the two data points describe almost NO
cap-rate movement, which contradicts Mordor's own framing of a "reset." **Net answer: cap rates
have very likely widened somewhat since 2019 (consistent with falling capital values against
roughly-flat-to-slightly-lower rents), but no single clean, well-attributed BOTH-DATES number was
found — named as a gap**, not filled with a guessed figure.

## Q5. New supply pipeline and why vacancy stays high

- Grade-A office inventory across 21 major Greater China cities: **99.2 million sqm** at Q4 2025
  `[1-SOURCE]`. New supply is expected to **peak in 2026**, i.e., the glut is not yet past its
  worst point on the supply side `[2-SOURCE]`.
- Guangzhou alone: **3.33 million sqm** of new supply due by 2027 (Financial Town, Pazhou
  districts) `[1-SOURCE]`.
- Nationally: **over 300 million sqm (3.2 billion sq ft) of office space under construction or on
  hold** — described as "equivalent to ten years of average supply delivery" into a market already
  at 20-40% vacancy `[1-SOURCE, Oxford Economics via Mingtiandi]`. In **every year except 2023**,
  developers started more office projects than they completed — a two-decade pattern, not a
  post-2020 anomaly `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Why vacancy stays high despite the downturn** (each strand independently sourced): (i) weak
  corporate hiring/expansion demand — landlords report relocations/upgrades, not net expansion, as
  the dominant 2025 leasing driver (39% of Guangzhou leasing was relocation/upgrade, "minimal"
  expansion-led demand) `[1-SOURCE]`; (ii) large tenants (tech, finance, real estate) reducing
  leased space or moving to self-built HQs, especially in Shenzhen `[1-SOURCE]`; (iii)
  government-subsidized industrial-park/business-park space competing directly with commercial
  Grade-A stock for the same tenant pool (see Q8) `[1-SOURCE]`; (iv) Beijing-specific
  decentralization — CBD tenants relocating to Xiong'an New Area, a policy-driven exodus distinct
  from ordinary vacancy dynamics `[1-SOURCE]`.

## Q7. Headline vs effective rent: rent-free periods and incentives now vs pre-crash

**This is the dossier's cleanest quantified face-vs-effective gap.** Pre-crash (2019) norm:
**1-3 months rent-free** for a standard ~2,000 sqm Shanghai/Beijing tenant `[1-SOURCE, later
corroborated directionally by a second, 2024-era source describing "typical rent-free periods of
2-3 months" as the STILL-standard baseline reference point landlords are now exceeding]`
`[effectively 2-SOURCE on the pre-crash baseline]`. Current (2024-2025) market: incentive packages
of **6-12 months rent-free plus turnkey fit-outs**, alongside CapEx reimbursements and — a newer
wrinkle — landlords contributing physical fit-out MATERIALS as part of lease negotiations
`[2-SOURCE]`. Separately, one source describes discounts on NEW developments in the most
competitive submarkets running **"as high as 40-50%"** off asking rent through combined
concessions `[1-SOURCE]`. **Net read: the rent-free component of the face-to-effective gap has
roughly TRIPLED to QUADRUPLED in duration (1-3 months -> 6-12 months) since 2019** — meaning every
headline/asking-rent figure in this dossier's City Table (and in every brokerage report generally)
understates the true decline in what landlords actually collect, in a way that has gotten
substantially worse, not better, since the crash began. No index in this search pass separates
face from effective rent numerically at the city level — this dossier's own rent figures should be
read as face/headline rents unless a house explicitly states otherwise, per the task's own
framing.

## Q8. The business park / 产业园 (industrial-office) category

- **Shanghai**: H1 2025 vacancy **29.2%**, the **12th consecutive quarter of rising vacancy** —
  500,000 sqm of new supply added in H1 2025, only 67,000 sqm absorbed `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Shenzhen**: H1 2025 vacancy **22.1%** (down QoQ on 220,000 sqm of fresh leasing), but **840,000
  sqm/year of new supply is expected through 2028** — the report explicitly warns oversupply will
  persist despite the near-term improvement `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **National range for industrial-park vacancy: 30-50%** `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Built for industrial-policy reasons, not demand — the clearest evidence found**: since March
  2025, **Shenzhen, Hangzhou, Guangzhou, Huizhou and Suzhou have all launched "zero-rent"
  policies — waiving lease costs for 2-5 years in selected STATE-OWNED industrial parks** to
  attract startups, explicitly framed as a response to a **national ban on more traditional
  incentive types**, and driven by inter-city/intra-city competition as local governments face
  pressure to protect jobs and fill unused factory/park space `[2-SOURCE]`. This is a direct,
  dated, named policy mechanism — parks being built and then given away rent-free — that
  corroborates the "supply exists for industrial-policy reasons, not demonstrated tenant demand"
  reading better than any indirect inference could.
- **Beijing submarket detail**: Zhongguancun/Shangdi submarkets have vacancy **exceeding 30%**,
  even though Zhongguancun was simultaneously Beijing's single biggest absorption bright spot in
  2025 (53% of the city's total net Grade-A absorption) `[1-SOURCE]` — illustrating that even the
  best-performing submarket in the capital has a very high vacancy floor to work through.

multiple large, dated, discount-priced SELL transactions (BlackRock x2, Standard Chartered,
Blackstone) against only one BUY transaction (Brookfield/Greenland) whose date is itself
uncertain. On RETAIL, foreign/global PE capital (PAG-led consortium) is genuinely and recently
BUYING at large scale. The cleanest single directional fact remains Q9's finding that domestic
buyers now dominate the office transaction market outright (100% of Beijing, 92% of Shanghai,
H1 2026) — foreign capital's role in OFFICE specifically has shrunk to near-absence on the buy
side, even as select foreign/PE capital is active on the RETAIL buy side.
