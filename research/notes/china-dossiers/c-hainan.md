## Headline findings
- Hainan 1988-93 is China's own, and first, modern property bubble: prices near a small SEZ island
  of ~6 million people rose roughly 5-25x in four years and then collapsed by five-sixths; by 1998
  the province alone reportedly carried **7.8% of the entire country's non-performing property
  assets** `[1-SOURCE]` — a figure traced to one academic paper, not independently re-confirmed,
  and different from (though possibly the ancestor of) the vaguer "30 billion yuan bad loans"
  headline that circulates everywhere without a pinned source.
- Wenzhou 2011 is the cleaner analogue for TODAY's credit mechanism: a private, informal-lending
  driven bust (interest as high as 90%/yr, "boss runaway" 跑路 flight, at least one recorded
  suicide) that needed a personal visit from the Premier, and whose peak-to-trough price decline
  (**63.14% on the longest measure**) is still the deepest of any tracked Chinese city.
- Both busts are barely cited in Anglophone commentary on the current crisis — a deliberate,
  dedicated search for 2020-2021 citations of Hainan as a warning came back essentially empty,
  which is itself a finding, not a search failure.
- Beihai (Guangxi) is Hainan's overshadowed twin: prices fell ~95% (RMB500-600/psf to RMB20-30/psf)
  and half-finished luxury villas and hotels were occupied not by buyers but by the unpaid
  construction workers who built them.
- The Wenzhou 2016 land-lease incident is not really resolved — Beijing patched it administratively
  (automatic renewal, no fee) rather than legislating what a residential land lease is actually
  owed at term's end, leaving the underlying question open for the much larger wave of 70-year
  grants that begin expiring mid-century.
- On balance, this dossier argues **Hainan is the closer structural analogue** for the 2021-present
  bust on the supply side and resolution path (state absorption of a systemic overbuild), while
  **Wenzhou is the closer analogue** on the credit/demand side (private leverage, household
  exposure, informal-credit contagion) — see Q9 for the argued case, not an assertion.

## 1. The Hainan bubble, 1988-1993: size, prices, wreckage
Hainan was made a province and Special Economic Zone in 1988, and capital poured in on the promise
of a tax-and-land arbitrage "Hong Kong of the south." Reported prices ran from roughly 300 yuan/sqm
in 1989 to about 7,500 yuan/sqm by 1992 `[1-SOURCE — Forbes/Rapoza 2011]`; a separately-sourced
account instead frames it as "more than five times" from a 1988 base to the same ~7,500 yuan/sqm
peak `[1-SOURCE — Caixin-linked]`. These two framings do not arithmetically reconcile (a "5x" move
implies a much higher 1988 starting price than 300 yuan/sqm) — flagged explicitly as the kind of
imprecise, repeated-without-reconciliation figure the task asked to surface. The number of
registered real-estate firms rose from about 12,000 to nearly 20,000 in the single year 1993, with
total real-estate investment up 143.5% that year, on a population of only ~6-7.3 million
`[2-SOURCE — Reading/CentAUR thesis + the Wu/Reed/Robinson PRRES paper independently give the
~20,000-firm figure]`. When Beijing tightened credit at the end of 1993, prices fell roughly
six-fold to about 1,000 yuan/sqm `[1-SOURCE]` and stayed near that level "until the early 2000s"
`[1-SOURCE]`.

**The NPL share.** The specific, citable figure is from Wu, Reed & Robinson's PRRES paper on the
1990s Hainan property cycle: **in 1998, Hainan's nonperforming property assets were at least RMB
40.6 billion, equal to 7.8% of the national total** — against a provincial population of 7.3 million
and provincial GDP of only RMB 43.9 billion that year `[1-SOURCE — this is one academic paper;
despite dedicated searching this session could not find a second, independent source quoting either
the RMB 40.6bn figure or the 7.8% share]`. Separately, a "30 billion yuan in bad bank loans" figure
circulates very widely across popular sources (Forbes, China Daily-linked pieces, general
retrospectives) `[widely repeated, but every hit traces to what looks like a single original
lineage — origin document not located; treated as a popular-press figure distinct from, and smaller
than, the academic paper's RMB 40.6bn]`. Whether "30bn" and "40.6bn/7.8%" are the same underlying
number rounded differently, or genuinely different accounting scopes (bank loans vs "property
assets"), could not be determined from search snippets alone.

## 2. Hainan specifics: developers, unfinished construction, workout time, recovery date
~20,000 property firms were active in Hainan at the 1993 peak (Q1, above) `[2-SOURCE]`. One source
puts total unfinished/aborted floor space at **16.3 million square metres** by 1998 `[1-SOURCE —
architecture/Domus-linked piece; not independently corroborated]`. The workout is repeatedly
described as taking "nearly 10 years" to digest the uncompleted stock `[1-SOURCE, widely repeated —
same provenance caveat as the 30bn figure above]`, with the Hainan Development Bank itself not
closed until June 1998 (below), consistent with a multi-year, not multi-quarter, resolution. On
recovery: prices stayed depressed into the early 2000s `[1-SOURCE]`; the exact year Hainan/Haikou's
nominal price level first re-crossed the 1993 ~7,500 yuan/sqm peak was **not located** in this
session's searches — a genuine gap. What can be said is that by 2022 Haikou's overall property
price index hit an all-time high of ~16,019 RMB/sqm, and residential specifically hit ~16,659
RMB/sqm in 2023 (both on the free-trade-port re-rating), before falling back to ~12,294 (2024) and
~10,423 (2025) `[1-SOURCE — CEIC-sourced synthesis]`. So in nominal terms the 1993 peak was
eventually exceeded several-fold — but 1993 yuan and 2022 yuan are not comparable without deflating,
and no deflated (real) comparison was found.

One institutional marker of how serious the resolution was: **Hainan Development Bank**, formed in
1995 from five distressed trust-and-investment companies, became in June 1998 the first commercial
bank in China's post-reform era to be forced to close, over real-estate-driven insolvency; the
central government took over roughly US$2 billion of its debt and used ICBC as trustee for deposits
and obligations. It remained China's only forced bank closure until Baoshang Bank in 2018-19
`[2-SOURCE — Caixin Global's "Five Things to Know" retrospective + corroborating banking-history
summaries]`.

## 3. The Beihai (Guangxi) bubble and its abandoned buildings
Beihai, an "obscure peninsula" in then-impoverished Guangxi, ran the same 1992-93 cycle in
miniature: developers from across China rushed in, prices reportedly reaching RMB 500-600 per
square foot before collapsing roughly 95% to RMB 20-30 per square foot once Beijing restricted bank
financing `[1-SOURCE — SCMP's "Boom and bust in Beihai" retrospective, via a secondary citation;
this appears to be the sole traceable source for Beihai's numbers in this session's searches]`.
Concretely, half-built luxury villas and hotels stood as "giant concrete skeletons," some projects
were demolished/blown up outright, and the buildings that remained were occupied not by the
intended buyers but by the unpaid construction workers who had built them, living in the windowless
shells. No developer count or project count for Beihai specifically was located — a clear gap
relative to Hainan, where at least the ~20,000-firm figure exists. One notable echo: recent (2020s)
data flags Beihai as the worst-performing major Chinese city for new-home prices in a given year
(-8.8%), suggesting the city's property market has again been unusually exposed in the current cycle
`[1-SOURCE]`.

## 4. What was learned institutionally, what was forgotten, and 2020-21 citations
Institutionally, Hainan is credited (in retrospective pieces, not contemporaneous 1990s sources) as
one of the pressures behind China's move toward market-priced, auction-based land sales in the
early-to-mid 2000s — land revenue is reported to have risen 977% from 2000 to 2004 once local
governments were required to sell land at market/auction rather than through backroom deals
`[1-SOURCE]`; the specific claim that this reform was a *direct, named* institutional response to
Hainan (as opposed to a broader fiscal-reform push) was **not confirmed** by this session's searches
— flagged as `[RECALL — unverified]` rather than asserted. A 2018 Caixin piece ("Hainan shows it may
have learned its housing lesson") does discuss the province revisiting its own history as the
free-trade-port announcement landed `[1-SOURCE]`. But a dedicated search for **explicit 2020-2021
citations of Hainan 1993 as a warning about the unfolding Evergrande-era crisis came back empty** —
none of roughly six queries aimed at this surfaced a contemporaneous op-ed or commentary piece
naming Hainan. Given how aggressively Anglophone commentary reached instead for Japan 1990 and the
US 2008 (the premise of this whole dossier), this is read as a genuine negative finding: Hainan
appears to have been *institutionally* remembered (in land-policy retrospectives) but *publicly*
forgotten as a live warning at the moment it would have mattered most. One almost-too-neat detail:
Evergrande itself built one of its most infamous megaprojects, **Ocean Flower Island** (a ~RMB 100
billion / US$15.5 billion artificial archipelago near Yangpu), on Hainan itself `[1-SOURCE]` —
without any located evidence that the company or its critics drew the obvious historical parallel
in real time.

## 5. The Wenzhou crisis of 2011: property decline, private lending, "boss runaway"
Wenzhou's crisis was fundamentally a **private/informal credit** event layered on a cooling property
market, not a state-bank NPL event like Hainan. By October-November 2011, at least 80 business
owners had fled the city and gone into hiding to escape debts to underground lenders and loan sharks
`[NPR]`, with a separate account putting the figure at "90 out of 400,000" factory owners
`[Reason]` `[2-SOURCE — the two figures differ, 80 vs 90, which is itself mild evidence the numbers
were independently reported rather than copy-pasted]`. Private lenders reportedly charged interest
up to 90%/year `[1-SOURCE]`; a separate figure cites entrepreneurs paying as much as 7%/month to
illegal lenders (≈84%+/yr annualized) `[1-SOURCE]` — broadly consistent. The human cost was real:
Shen Kuizheng, owner of the Zhengdeli shoe factory, jumped to his death from a 22-story building in
late September 2011 while carrying more than $60 million in debt `[1-SOURCE — NPR]`. The crisis was
taken seriously enough that Premier Wen Jiabao personally flew to Wenzhou in October 2011 with the
finance minister and PBOC governor, and a provincial support/bailout fund of up to RMB 100 billion
was established `[2-SOURCE]`. On scale: the PBOC's Wenzhou branch put local private lending at
RMB 110 billion as of end-July 2011 `[1-SOURCE]`, while other press estimates put total Wenzhou
private borrowing as high as RMB 500 billion (~US$79bn) `[1-SOURCE]` — a wide range reflecting how
hard the shadow market was to measure even at the time. Nationally, PBOC estimated underground
lending at RMB 3.38 trillion as of May 2011 `[1-SOURCE]`, while a separate estimate put all informal
lending at over RMB 14 trillion by end-2011 (~26% of formal bank loans) `[1-SOURCE]` — again a wide
range, flagged rather than resolved. On the formal-banking side, Wenzhou's own NPL ratio reached
4.52% (RMB 33 billion) of total local loans by end-Q1 2012, the majority property-linked
`[1-SOURCE — academic, Journal of Banking Regulation via a Taylor & Francis piece]`.

## 6. Wenzhou peak-to-trough, and whether prices ever recovered
Wenzhou's property market peaked in 2009-2010; one account has luxury apartments falling from
roughly 60,000 yuan/sqm at the peak to about 35,000 yuan/sqm at a subsequent low, a near-50% decline
`[1-SOURCE — SCMP]`. Officially tracked year-on-year data show a 14% slump by mid-2012 and an 18.03%
year-on-year fall in Q3 2012 specifically `[1-SOURCE each, consistent in direction and rough
magnitude]`. Separately, and on a longer horizon, China Housing Price Network data (cited via a
secondary source) puts Wenzhou's **all-time peak-to-trough decline at 63.14%**, the largest of any
tracked Chinese city `[1-SOURCE]`. These numbers do not describe the same window: the ~50%/~18%
figures are 2011-2012 point-in-time reads, while 63.14% is a longer, later-measured cumulative
figure. Reconciling them (own interpretation, not a sourced claim): Wenzhou prices bottomed and
partially stabilized through 2013-2016 (national destocking-era support measures, sales "bottomed
out" in H1 2015 per one source), but the city never durably reclaimed its 2009-2010 peak before the
national 2021+ downturn arrived and pushed prices to a fresh, deeper trough — meaning Wenzhou is
best read as one long, interrupted down-cycle from 2010 to today rather than a full boom-bust-
recovery loop. That is exactly why question 9 treats Wenzhou as "the best single-city case study of
a complete Chinese property cycle": it is complete in the sense of having gone all the way through
private-credit collapse, informal-lending contagion, a partial policy-assisted stabilization, and
then a renewed decline — not complete in the sense of having recovered.

## 7. The 2016 Wenzhou 20-year land-lease expiry incident
In early 2016, a Wenzhou homeowner discovered their residential land-use right — granted for only 20
years, an unusually short term Wenzhou used in the early 1990s pilot period rather than the 70-year
term later standardized nationally — had expired on March 4, 2016, and that the city was demanding a
renewal fee of roughly RMB 300,000 (~US$46,314), reportedly around 33% of the home's value; roughly
1,700 more sets of residential land-use rights in the city were due to expire by 2019
`[2-SOURCE — Mingtiandi's contemporaneous reporting, corroborated by SCMP and Foreign Policy pieces
on the same episode]`. The resolution came from Beijing, not Wenzhou: in December 2016, China's
Ministry of Land and Resources declared that such expiring residential rights would be **renewed
automatically, without an extra fee** `[2-SOURCE — Mingtiandi + Taipei Times, Dec 2016]`. What it
revealed (own synthesis of the sourced facts): China's property law guarantees automatic renewal for
the standard 70-year residential grant, but says essentially nothing about what happens, or what if
anything is owed, when a *shorter*, non-standard grant like Wenzhou's 20-year pilot-era leases
expires — because such short leases were a local anomaly the national framework never anticipated.
Beijing's answer was an administrative patch for this one case, not a legislative resolution of the
underlying question: what a Chinese leasehold is actually worth, and what (if anything) is owed to
the state, at the moment it runs out. That question remains formally open for the much larger,
slower-moving wave of standard 70-year urban residential grants — the first tranches of which, from
the earliest post-1988 sales, will not begin expiring in volume until roughly the 2050s-2060s.

## 8. Other Chinese property busts before 2021
- **The 2008 mini-crash**: national property sales fell roughly 20% in 2008 amid the global
  financial crisis; Beijing's response (a large stimulus announced November 2008, plus loose
  monetary policy) is widely credited with arresting the decline quickly and, per several
  retrospective critiques, seeding the much larger subsequent credit/property boom `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **The 2014-2015 downturn**: national inventory (months-to-sell) peaked around 30 months in 2014;
  prices fell more than 5% year-on-year by February 2015, with 47 of 70 tracked cities below the
  national average decline; the PBOC cut the benchmark rate four times from November 2014 and RRR
  three times from February 2015 to arrest it `[1-SOURCE — PIIE policy brief]`.
- **Ordos/Erdos, Kangbashi (2011-2012)**: prices reportedly fell from over US$3,000/sqm to about
  $470/sqm in the district, variously described as a 62.5%-70% collapse; the purpose-built
  Kangbashi satellite city (32 sq km, construction begun 2004) ended up with more than 70% of its
  properties left unfinished, and a population of only ~70,000 against plans for a much larger city
  (just 30,000 residents as of 2009) `[2-SOURCE — Forbes' Wade Shepard update + a separate
  Mish Talk/vagabondjourney-sourced account, consistent on direction and rough magnitude]`. This is
  the best-known "ghost city" internationally, after a 2009 Al Jazeera segment.
- **Shenmu/Yulin (Shaanxi), coal-boom private lending**: Shenmu and neighboring Fugu county are
  reported to have produced roughly 4,000 individual coal-wealth billionaires at the height of the
  boom `[1-SOURCE]`, and Shenmu is independently known (this desk's own background knowledge) for a
  coal-money-fuelled private-lending bust around 2012 broadly contemporaneous with Wenzhou — but
  this session's dedicated searches could not source that specific Shenmu private-lending collapse
  with figures; what surfaced instead was an adjacent but distinct Shanxi-province episode
  (Liansheng Group, a large private coal firm, defaulting on four of six tranches — RMB 764 million
  of a RMB 1 billion trust product sold via Jilin Trust, 2011-12). **The Shenmu-specific bust is
  therefore flagged `[RECALL — unverified]` and named as a real gap**, not asserted with figures.

## 9. Hainan 1993 vs Wenzhou 2011 vs the current bust — arguing the better analogue
Neither is a perfect match, and the honest answer is that they are better analogues for *different
parts* of the current crisis.

**The case for Wenzhou** (credit/demand mechanism): Wenzhou's bust ran through private household
savings, informal peer-to-peer lending, and personal entrepreneurial leverage — not the state banking
system directly. That is structurally closer to today's crisis than Hainan's was, because today's
stress genuinely involves household-level exposure (presale deposits trapped in unfinished units,
mortgage strikes, wealth-management-product losses) and a private-sector credit chain (developer
bonds, trust products, informal guarantees) under strain, rather than one state bank's balance sheet.
Wenzhou was also explicitly treated by Beijing, in real time, as a *national* early-warning signal
(hence the Premier's personal visit) — much as Evergrande's 2021 crisis was treated as a national,
not merely regional, signal.

**The case for Hainan** (supply-side and resolution mechanism): Hainan's bust was a province-wide,
policy-fuelled overbuild — 20,000 developers building far beyond any real demand, resolved only after
nearly a decade by absorbing the losses institutionally (a bank closure, central-government debt
takeover, and years of physically working through unfinished stock). That sequence — overbuild, long
public-sector-mediated absorption, unfinished construction as the visible symptom — maps far more
directly onto the current bust's actual shape: a nationwide inventory glut, hundreds of millions of
square metres of unfinished 烂尾楼 (a direct echo of Hainan's 16.3 million sqm, just at a different
order of magnitude), and a resolution path so far built on slow, state-guided mechanisms (Three Red
Lines, PSL, state purchase of unsold units, LGFV-mediated absorption) rather than a fast private-
sector clearing of the kind Wenzhou went through.

**Verdict, argued**: on the mechanism the desk already has a standing view on — CI-D1..D5's finding
that "crises belong to credit" — Wenzhou's private-credit-driven bust is the more mechanistically
apt comparison. But on the dimension the current crisis is actually failing to resolve — an
overbuilt supply side being absorbed slowly by the state rather than cleared quickly by private
defaults — Hainan is the closer structural analogue, and it is the one being cited far less. That
asymmetry (Wenzhou fits the *cause*, Hainan fits the *resolution*) is itself the most useful
takeaway of this dossier, and argues for citing **both**, deliberately, rather than reaching for
Japan or the US by default.

## 10. The genuinely interesting detail: what Hainan's ruins became, and Hainan today
Concrete evidence of what actually happened to Hainan's abandoned buildings is thin, but one
specific, sourced example: **Haikou Yazhou Gu Cheng**, a complex built around a former temple to a
noted general and expanded during the 1993 boom, lost its official tourist-attraction status in 1997
for dilapidation, suffered a serious fire in 2005, and remains derelict today — with parts of the
site now used informally for small businesses such as auto repair `[2-SOURCE — Wikipedia entry,
returned consistently across two independent searches]`. More broadly, unfinished Haikou high-rises
reportedly "stood empty for a decade" after 1993 `[1-SOURCE]`. Hainan's subsequent reinvention is the
real twist: rebranded from bust-era cautionary tale to "China's Hawaii," it became a Free Trade Port
in 2018-2020, triggering a fresh property boom that pushed Haikou prices to fresh nominal all-time
highs in 2022-2023 — before that boom itself began rolling over into 2024-2025. And in one of the
more pointed ironies this dossier turned up: Evergrande — the developer whose 2021 default opened the
current national crisis — built one of its largest and most extravagant projects, the artificial
Ocean Flower Island, on Hainan itself, on the very island that hosted China's first property bust,
three decades later.
