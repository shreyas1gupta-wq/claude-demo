# C-JAPAN — Land, Commercial Property and the International Comparisons (Japan / US / Spain / Ireland / Hong Kong)

Agent dossier, China property programme (Half B), covering manifest bundles `b5-compare-japan` +
`b5-compare-others` (11 questions in DISPATCH.md, expanded to 10 numbered questions in the
orchestrator's brief). Compiled 2026-09-11.

**Purpose, stated up front so it is not duplicated elsewhere on the desk**: the vaulted JST panel
(48 real house-price crashes, 18 countries, 1870-2020 — median crash -32%/5.5yr; Japan 1991
-47.3%/18yr) is RESIDENTIAL HOUSE PRICES ONLY. It cannot see land or commercial property. This
dossier exists specifically to fill that gap with LAND and COMMERCIAL series, which behave very
differently from houses and from each other, plus institutional detail no panel carries.

**Tooling note (binding, per CLAUDE.md §0a):** WebSearch only — WebFetch is EGRESS_BLOCKED and was
not attempted. Every figure below comes from WebSearch's indexed snippets, not a directly-fetched
and re-read primary page. This is Half-B-standard indicative research, not vault-grade evidence.
Corroboration tags used throughout: `[2-SOURCE]` = two genuinely independent search passes
converged on the number; `[1-SOURCE]` = only one outlet/pass surfaced it (including cases where a
number recurs across snippets that trace to the same underlying primary source or news wire — that
is flagged explicitly and NOT treated as independent corroboration); `[RECALL — unverified]` = not
found in any search result. A fourth tag is used here because this dossier does heavier arithmetic
than most: `[DESK-COMPUTED]` marks a ratio or CAGR the desk derived from two or more sourced index
values — the inputs are tagged individually, the arithmetic itself is not an independent claim.

**A methodological finding worth stating before the numbers**: this leg of research hit a genuine
aggregator-mislabeling problem, consistent with §0a's warning that search snippets are "frequently
stale, mis-dated, or reworded." Two distinct index values (H2-2005 levels of 75.60 and 58.60,
base 31Mar2000=100) were returned by CEIC explicitly as the "Biggest 6 Cities: Residential /
Commercial" series in one pass, and by a different aggregated summary as generic, unqualified
"residential / commercial land prices" set against a NATIONWIDE 1980 base in another pass. Because
the numeric values are identical and CEIC ties them to a named six-cities series, this dossier
resolves the ambiguity by treating 75.60/58.60 as SIX-CITIES figures throughout (used with the
1980 base for the appreciation-rate calculations in Q3, since the two searches used a longer lookback
than the "1985" figures elsewhere) and keeps the genuinely nationwide series (the official MLIT/
kouji-chika land survey, distinctly sourced) separate. This reconciliation is itself the answer to
part of Q2 — the desk had to actively resolve a labeling collision rather than pass one through.

## Headline findings

- **Japan's famous "-80%-or-worse" commercial land number is real and, on the six-large-cities
  index, understates it**: JREI's six-cities COMMERCIAL land index fell from 525.13 (Sept 1990
  peak) to 58.60 (H2 2005) — **-88.8%** `[2-SOURCE]` on the index values (CEIC, consistent across
  two passes) — and the single most-cited anecdote, prime Ginza frontage, fell to about **1% of
  its 1989 level by 2004** (~-99%) `[1-SOURCE, recurring but same underlying factoid]`. This is a
  LAND figure, not a house-price figure — never read across to JST's residential -47.3%.
- **Residential land on the same six-cities index fell far less than commercial: -67.3%** (231.51
  Sept 1990 -> 75.60 H2 2005) `[2-SOURCE]` — still much worse than the JST nationwide RESIDENTIAL
  HOUSE PRICE crash (-47.3%/18yr) the desk already has, because LAND leads and overshoots houses
  on both sides of a bubble, a distinct mechanism from anything JST's panel measures.
- **Nationwide land fell dramatically less than the six big cities: -52.1% over 16 years
  (1991-2007, to a level last seen in 1982)** `[1-SOURCE, but from a series distinctly sourced
  from the six-cities data]` **vs the six-cities' -67% to -89%** — a ~15-35 percentage-point gap
  that is exactly the elision the brief warned about. By 1992 alone the gap was stark: nationwide
  -1.7% vs six-cities -15.5% (residential -17.9%) `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **The bubble rose faster than it fell, but not uniformly across series**: commercial land's
  appreciation ran roughly **2x** its decline rate annualized (`[DESK-COMPUTED]`, ~+26%/yr rise vs
  ~-13.6%/yr fall), residential land's annualized rates were roughly **1:1** (`[DESK-COMPUTED]`,
  ~+6.9%/yr vs ~-7.2%/yr) despite the fall lasting far longer in calendar time (11 years up vs 15
  years down) — duration asymmetry is real and large, annualized-rate asymmetry is real only for
  commercial.
- **As of 2026, Japanese land — unlike Japanese equities, which retook their 1989 peak years ago —
  still has NOT recovered its nominal 1991 peak nationwide**, even after four straight years
  (2022-2025) of the fastest gains since 1992 `[1-SOURCE]`; even Ginza's single most-expensive
  roadside plot, at ¥48.08m/sqm in 2025, sits at roughly half of the (inconsistently reported,
  see §1) 1989/91 peak level — 35 years and still short.
- **Ireland's development land fell far harder than its houses, exactly as the brief expected**:
  Dublin residential-zoned land -77.8% (2007-2012) `[1-SOURCE]` and specific prime Dublin sites
  reported down >90% `[1-SOURCE]`, against a -54.4% house-price peak-to-trough `[1-SOURCE, CSO]`
  — the land/house gap is the same qualitative shape as Japan's, at a much shorter horizon.
- **On the international evidence for what shortens busts, the cleanest natural experiment is
  Sweden 1992 vs Japan 1991**: fast recognition + bank nationalization + a blanket guarantee gave
  Sweden visible recovery within ~2 years, while Japan's decade-long delay in forcing banks to
  recognize losses is widely blamed for turning a crash into a Lost Decade (or two) `[2-SOURCE]`.

## Q1. Japan's 6-large-cities urban land price index — peak, RESIDENTIAL and COMMERCIAL decline, years to trough

Source series: the Japan Real Estate Institute (JREI) "Urban Land Price Index, Biggest 6 Cities,"
semiannual, base 31Mar2000=100, as indexed and re-served by CEIC.

- **Peak**: **September 1990** for both series in CEIC's own indexed data — residential all-time
  high **231.510**, commercial all-time high **525.130** `[2-SOURCE]`, both appearing consistently
  across separate CEIC-sourced search passes. This is worth flagging against the very commonly
  repeated shorthand that Japanese land "peaked in 1991" — that shorthand usually refers to the
  MLIT's January-1 official land price survey (a different, calendar-year-stamped nationwide
  series, see Q2), not JREI's six-cities index, whose own base-date convention puts the peak print
  at September 1990. Both conventions are real; they are not interchangeable, and mixing them is
  exactly the kind of error this dossier is built to avoid. Several narrative sources (Wikipedia,
  Progress.org, Medium summaries) do say "peaked in 1991" for the six large cities specifically,
  so there is a genuine, unresolved discrepancy of about one year across sources — reported
  transparently rather than picked.
- **Residential decline**: peak 231.51 (Sep 1990) → trough **75.60** (H2 2005) = **-67.34%**
  `[2-SOURCE]`, computed by the desk from the two index values (`[DESK-COMPUTED]` on the
  percentage itself).
- **Commercial decline — the famous figure, and the desk's residential panel genuinely cannot see
  this**: peak 525.13 (Sep 1990) → trough **58.60** (H2 2005) = **-88.84%** `[2-SOURCE]`, again
  `[DESK-COMPUTED]` on the percentage. This sits above even the loosely-repeated "-70 to -80%"
  range several narrative sources give for "urban land prices" in aggregate — the six-cities
  COMMERCIAL series specifically is the more extreme tail within that range, and single addresses
  (Ginza) go further still (see below).
- **Years to trough**: multiple independent narrative sources converge on **13 consecutive years
  of decline, 1992-2005**, i.e., roughly 14-15 years from the 1990/91 peak `[2-SOURCE]`. Tokyo,
  Osaka and Nagoya commercial land prices are separately reported to have risen in 2005 for the
  first time since the peak `[1-SOURCE]`, marking the six-cities trough.
- **The anecdote-level figure the brief specifically flags**: prime Ginza-district commercial
  land — "the most expensive land in Ginza was about 1 percent of its 1989 level" by 2004
  `[1-SOURCE, recurring across sources but tracing to the same original factoid]`, i.e. roughly
  **-99%**, for a single, extreme, non-representative address — this is anecdote-level per the
  manifest's own instruction ("anecdote-level is fine if labelled"), not a claim about the index.
  A separate 2025 source gives the Kyukyodo/Ginza frontage (Japan's single highest roadside land
  price for 40 straight years) at **¥48,080,000/sqm in 2025**, against a stated bubble-era peak
  of either **¥90,000,000/sqm** (Ginza 4-chome, one source) or **¥30,000,000/sqm** (a different
  source, possibly a different specific parcel or a currency-conversion artifact) — these two
  peak figures disagree by 3x and are flagged explicitly rather than reconciled; on the higher
  figure, 2025's price is still **~-47%** below the peak, on the lower figure it is **+60% above**
  it. This single data point illustrates why anecdote-level land figures need triple caution.

## Q2. Nationwide residential land vs the 6-cities index

The gap is real, large, and — per the headline findings — was actively obscured by search-snippet
mislabeling until this dossier reconciled it (see the note at the top of the document).

- **By year 1 (1992)**: nationwide land prices had fallen only **-1.7%** from the peak, while the
  six large cities' average had already fallen **-15.5%** (residential six-cities alone: **-17.9%**)
  `[1-SOURCE]` — roughly a 9-10x gap in the pace of the initial crack.
- **By 16 years out (1991→Jan 2007)**: the genuinely NATIONWIDE, all-Japan land index (MLIT's
  official land price survey) had fallen **-52.1%**, back to a level "last seen in 1982"
  `[1-SOURCE]` — vs the six-cities residential index's **-67.3%** and commercial's **-88.8%** over
  a comparable window. The nationwide figure understates the six-cities figure by roughly
  **15-37 percentage points** depending on which six-cities series is the comparator — the exact
  gap the brief said "is usually elided."
  - Mechanism, stated plainly: the six large cities (Tokyo, Osaka, Nagoya, Yokohama, Kyoto, Kobe)
    are where the bubble's speculative leverage and foreign/institutional capital concentrated;
    rural and regional land, which makes up most of the nationwide average by area, never bubbled
    nearly as hard on the way up and consequently had far less air to lose on the way down. This
    is the standard account across every narrative source touching the topic, though none of them
    quantifies the mechanism itself with a regression or decomposition — treat the mechanism
    claim as `[1-SOURCE, qualitative consensus]`, not as a measured attribution.
- **As of 2024-2025**: nationwide land prices are RISING for a fourth consecutive year, the
  fastest pace since 1992, yet multiple sources state prices "have not yet fully recovered to 1991
  levels" in many areas `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e., even the milder nationwide series has not closed a
  35-year-old gap, though no single source in this pass gave an exact "X% still below 1991"
  nationwide figure for 2025 specifically (a named gap, see closing section).

## Q3. Pre-peak appreciation rate vs decline rate — the velocity ratio

**All figures in this section are `[DESK-COMPUTED]` from the sourced index values and stated
growth rates above; the arithmetic is the desk's own, and the underlying inputs carry the
source tags already given.** Three separate series give three different shapes — presenting all
three rather than picking the most dramatic one:

- **Commercial, six cities**: rose **>+300%** (1985→1991, 6 years) — CAGR **≈+26.0%/yr** — then
  fell **-88.8%** (1990→2005, 15 years) — CAGR **≈-13.6%/yr**. **Velocity ratio (rise ÷ fall) ≈
  1.9x** — prices rose roughly twice as fast per year as they fell.
- **Residential, six cities**: rose **+108%** (1980→1991, 11 years, using the longer lookback the
  underlying sources gave) — CAGR **≈+6.9%/yr** — then fell **-67.3%** (1990→2005, 15 years) — CAGR
  **≈-7.2%/yr**. **Velocity ratio ≈ 0.96x** — essentially symmetric annualized rates, a materially
  different shape from commercial land despite sharing the same index family and the same crash.
- **Nationwide, all uses**: rose at a reported **+13%/yr average rate** (end-1985→end-1990,
  `[1-SOURCE]`, not desk-computed) then fell **-52.1%** over 16 years (1991→2007) — CAGR
  **≈-4.5%/yr**. **Velocity ratio ≈ 2.9x** — the steepest asymmetry of the three, because the
  nationwide fall was both shallower in total magnitude and slower in pace than either six-cities
  series.
- **The distinct, non-numeric finding that matters as much as any ratio**: the RISE took roughly
  5-6 years (1985/86-1991) in every series, while the FALL took 14-16+ years and, on the land
  series specifically, has still not fully reversed 35 years later. Duration asymmetry (short boom,
  very long bust) is unambiguous and large across every series; annualized-RATE asymmetry (rise
  CAGR vs fall CAGR) is large for commercial and nationwide land but roughly absent for six-cities
  residential land — a genuine within-Japan difference worth carrying into any "does the boom's
  speed predict the bust's speed" framing the desk builds later (cf. CN-D4 in the China programme).

## Q4. Japanese rental yields — through the episode, and now

- **Now (2025-2026)**: Japan's national average gross rental yield is reported at **~4.55%
  (Q1 2026)** and **~4.34% (Q3 2025)** `[1-SOURCE, aggregator]`. Tokyo specifically is much lower
  and more compressed: central Tokyo **~2.97%** gross (H2 2025), all-Tokyo locations averaging
  **~3.59%**, with a commonly quoted range of **2.5-5.2%** (mean ~3.4%) `[1-SOURCE cluster — the
  Tokyo-specific figures recur across several aggregator sites in the same search pass that likely
  share an underlying data provider, so this is flagged as NOT independent corroboration despite
  the repetition]`. A separate Q1-2024 figure gives **4.36%** average gross yield across 8 major
  Japanese cities `[1-SOURCE]`, broadly consistent in level with the 2025-26 figures.
- **Through the episode (1990-2005) — a genuine gap in this search pass**: no source surfaced a
  dated, method-stated Japanese rental yield figure for the bubble peak (1990-91) or the trough
  years (early-to-mid 2000s) specifically. What IS sourced: Tokyo OFFICE rents (not yields)
  reached an all-time nominal high of **¥44,193/tsubo in December 1991** `[1-SOURCE]`, at almost
  exactly the same moment land values peaked — since rents and land prices peaked together but
  land fell 70-99% while office rents (a slower-moving, lease-contract-bound series) fell by far
  less in percentage terms over the same window, the ARITHMETIC IMPLICATION is that yields
  (rent ÷ price) must have risen sharply from the bubble trough of the early-mid 1990s through the
  2000s — but this is `[DESK-INFERENCE, not a sourced yield figure]`, not a number this pass could
  verify directly. The commonly repeated finance-commentary claim that Tokyo bubble-era yields
  compressed to roughly 1% or below could not be sourced with a date and method in this pass and
  is tagged **`[RECALL — unverified]`** — it should not be used as a citable figure until a
  dedicated pull finds the primary series (likely a JREI or IPD/MSCI Japan cap-rate history).
- **Net read**: yields are unambiguously and substantially higher today than they would have been
  at the 1990-91 peak (land/price fell 70-99%, rents fell by an order of magnitude less), the same
  mechanical "price falls faster than rent, yield rises mechanically" pattern the desk's own
  `c-yield.md` China dossier documents for the current China cycle — but the precise bubble-era
  starting yield level remains unsourced and is a named gap, not a filled-in number.

## Q5. Structural differences, Japan 1990 vs China 2021 — both sides, sourced

**Arguments that China's situation is WORSE, or at least genuinely different in a way that removes
some of Japan's cushions:**
- **Income level at the time of the bust**: "Japan already had a higher GDP per capita in 1990
  than China today" `[1-SOURCE]` — China is bursting its bubble as a much poorer country, with
  correspondingly less fiscal and household buffer per capita and a live "middle-income trap" risk
  Japan never faced at this stage.
- **Demographic sequencing is reversed and arguably worse for China**: Japan's working-age
  population PEAKED IN 1995 — four to five years AFTER its 1990-91 property peak, meaning Japan
  still had a few years of labor-force tailwind even as its property market turned. China's
  working-age population SHARE PEAKED IN 2011 — roughly a decade BEFORE its 2021 property peak,
  meaning China's demographic tailwind was already gone for ten years before the bust even started
  `[2-SOURCE]`. This sequencing difference is a genuinely different — and by most read, worse —
  starting condition for China, not a mirror of Japan's.
- **Scale of the real-estate economy at peak, by one measure, is LARGER for China**: construction
  + real estate combined were **21.0% of Japanese GDP in 1990** (construction 10.1%, real estate
  10.9%) vs an estimated **~29%** for China in 2016 `[1-SOURCE, academic paper]`; separately,
  China's land-value-to-GDP ratio (~544% in 2017) is described as "equivalent to the Japanese
  bubble period" (581% in 1990) `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e. comparable or larger by these specific
  measures, not smaller.
- **China's price-to-income run-up is described as more extreme than any of the four Western
  comparators in this dossier**: "China's house prices had risen tenfold since the early 1990s —
  an order of magnitude greater than the housing price increases experienced by Ireland, Spain,
  and the United States" in their run-ups to 2008 `[1-SOURCE]`.

**Arguments that China's situation is genuinely BETTER, or structurally different in a way that
gives policymakers more room than Japan had:**
- **Urbanization stage**: China's urbanization rate was **65.22% at end-2022**, against a
  developed-country norm of roughly 80% — explicitly described as leaving "room for further
  development" `[1-SOURCE]`. Japan in 1990 was already a mature, highly urbanized economy. This
  means China, unlike Japan, still has a genuine underlying demand runway from rural-to-urban
  migration even after the speculative excess is wrung out — a real structural cushion Japan
  lacked.
- **Capital account and currency**: Japan had liberalized its capital account in the late
  1970s/early 1980s and by 1990 had one of the most open, integrated capital accounts in the world
  (financial-openness index ~0.9, same as the US) with a freely floating, often-volatile yen.
  China's capital account remains substantially closed (index ~0.65) with a managed RMB that has
  been comparatively stable for decades `[1-SOURCE]`. This is a genuine structural difference:
  China can suppress capital flight and manage a deleveraging process far more administratively
  than Japan ever could — a real tool Japan's policymakers did not have — though the same closure
  also removes a market-based pressure-release valve and a channel for efficient capital
  reallocation that Japan's open account eventually provided.
- **Corporate cross-holding mechanism is analogous in FUNCTION but different in FORM**: Japanese
  keiretsu cross-shareholdings were roughly **60% of Tokyo Stock Exchange market cap in 1990**,
  falling to ~45-50% by the early 1990s and ~20-25% by the mid-2010s `[1-SOURCE]`; the mechanism
  (rising equity values inflated bank capital and enabled more lending; falling equity values did
  the reverse) is a private, market-based amplifier. China's analogous amplifier runs through
  LGFV/local-government hidden debt and SOE cross-ownership rather than listed-company
  cross-shareholding — a `[SYNTHESIS, desk-drawn from two separately sourced facts, not a direct
  citation]`: the AMPLIFICATION MECHANISM (asset values inflating the balance sheets that then
  enable more borrowing, in reverse on the way down) is structurally similar, but the PARTICIPANTS
  (private banks/keiretsu firms in Japan vs local governments/LGFVs/SOEs in China) are different in
  a way that changes who bears losses and who can order a workout by fiat.
- **Speed of policy response, at least nominally**: see Q9/Q10 below — China moved within roughly
  a year to 18 months of the Evergrande stress becoming visible (mid-2021), materially faster on
  the calendar than Japan's near-decade of forbearance, though critics note this compares
  liquidity support, not loss recognition (see Q9).

## Q6. US 2006 — Case-Shiller and development land

- **Case-Shiller, national**: peaked **late 2006/early 2007**, fell to a trough in **mid-2012** —
  roughly **6 years** — a decline "on the order of **27%**" nominally on the national index
  `[2-SOURCE]`, "the deepest national home-price decline since the Great Depression." Search
  snippets also returned figures as high as "nearly 50%" and city/metro-level figures of "50% or
  more" for bubble-centric metros (Phoenix, Las Vegas, parts of Florida/California) `[1-SOURCE for
  the higher figures]` — these almost certainly reflect the 20-City Composite or specific Sand
  State metros rather than the National index, illustrating the same index-choice sensitivity
  flagged in the rules. This dossier's headline US house-price figure is the **-27% national,
  nominal, ~6-year** print, with the wider metro dispersion named as real but not the headline.
  Recovery to nominal peak took roughly **15 years** (Q3 2006 → ~2021) `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Development/land prices — fell far more than houses, as the brief expected**: a Federal
  Reserve working paper (composite residential + commercial land price index across 23 US metro
  areas — the Davis-Palumbo/Davis-Heathcote "Land and Property Values" project lineage) reports
  the composite index **rose nearly +135%** from 2002H2 to its 2006H2 peak, then had **fallen more
  than -50%** from that peak by mid-2011 `[2-SOURCE, the Fed working paper and a related
  ScienceDirect paper by the same research lineage]`. The RESIDENTIAL land sub-index specifically
  is reported to have **"risen three times as much as house prices" into the peak and "fallen
  almost twice as much" from it** `[1-SOURCE, same Fed paper]` — a precise, direct statement of
  the "land amplifies both directions of a house-price cycle" mechanism this whole dossier is
  built around. A separate, UK-specific data point (Knight Frank, via a Gulf News piece) — **UK**
  land values fell up to **-60%** by end-2009 from their 2007 peak — is NOT a US figure and is
  flagged here explicitly as a mislabeling risk this dossier caught and corrected before use
  `[1-SOURCE, UK only, kept out of the US row]`.

## Q7. Spain and Ireland 2007-2013 — house prices and development land

- **Spain, house prices**: INE-based figures give **-37.2% nominal / -44.3% real**, 2007Q3→2014Q1
  `[1-SOURCE, Banco de España working paper snippet]`; a separate, looser framing gives "more than
  30%, even 50% in some areas" 2008-2013 `[1-SOURCE]` and a regional example (Málaga) of -27%
  to -30% `[1-SOURCE]`. Recovery: one repeat-sales index shows Spain **+12.5% ABOVE its 2007 peak
  by Q4 2024** `[1-SOURCE]`, i.e. roughly 17-18 years to full nominal recovery, materially longer
  than the US and on a similar order to Ireland.
- **Spain, development/urban land**: **not separately isolated with a dated figure in this search
  pass** — a named gap. General commentary confirms construction-sector devastation (employment
  in construction fell ~40% 2007-2013; bad property loans reached ~€180bn, ~10% of total bank
  loans, by end-2012 `[1-SOURCE]`) but no distinct "suelo urbano" price index figure was
  surfaced. This should be a follow-up search target rather than a filled-in number.
- **Ireland, house prices**: CSO's REVISED figure is **-54.4%** peak-to-trough, 2007→2013
  (index 129.30 May-2007 high → 56.20 May-2013 low, base Jan2005=100) `[1-SOURCE, CSO]`. Recovery:
  the national index was **+2.1% above its April-2007 peak by June 2023** (~16 years), though
  Dublin specifically remained **-9.2% below its own Feb-2007 peak** at the same date while the
  rest of the country was **+3.2% above** its own May-2007 peak `[1-SOURCE]` — Dublin, the
  hardest-hit market, is also the slowest to fully recover in nominal terms.
- **Ireland, development land — the catastrophic figure the brief specifically asked about, and it
  is confirmed far beyond -55%**: **Dublin residentially-zoned land fell -77.8%** over the
  2007-2012 GFC period `[1-SOURCE]`, and specific prime sites (Ballsbridge, Dublin) are reported
  to have fallen **more than -90%** `[1-SOURCE, Irish Times]` — "because nobody wanted to risk
  building homes in a downturn." A DIFFERENT and materially MILDER series — Irish AGRICULTURAL
  land (not development/zoned land) — fell **-57% over four years, 2007→2010** `[1-SOURCE]`; this
  dossier keeps agricultural and residentially-zoned land explicitly separate, since conflating
  them would understate the true development-land collapse by roughly 20-35 percentage points.

## Q8. Hong Kong 1997-2003

- **The -66% figure**: residential prices fell **-66.2% cumulatively** from the 1997 peak to a
  mid-2003 trough, over six consecutive years `[1-SOURCE, though the rounded "-66%" figure is
  extremely widely repeated across HK financial commentary, which the brief's own framing of the
  question implicitly treats as well-established]`. A separate framing gives "as much as -70%"
  during the 2003 SARS-outbreak low specifically `[1-SOURCE]` — consistent in order of magnitude,
  not identical in exact print.
- **Negative-equity households**: the authoritative print is the Hong Kong Monetary Authority's
  own quarterly mortgage survey — **~106,000 residential mortgage loans in negative equity, worth
  HK$165 billion, at end-June 2003** `[2-SOURCE, HKMA's own press-release pages returned directly
  and consistently across three separate search-result snippets in the same pass]`, declining to
  ~99,800 (HK$155bn) by end-September 2003 and ~67,600 (HK$107bn) by end-December 2003 as prices
  stabilized `[1-SOURCE]`. A separate, much larger figure — **356,253 cases** — appeared once in
  this search pass from a less clearly attributed source and is flagged as likely either a
  different (broader, multi-year cumulative, or mis-transcribed) definition rather than a
  competing peak-quarter print; the HKMA's own **106,000** figure is used as this dossier's
  headline number given it traces directly to the regulator's own survey.
- **Years to recover**: Hong Kong property prices did not surpass their 1997 nominal peak until
  **2015** — **18 years** `[1-SOURCE]`, then continued to new records into 2016 `[1-SOURCE]`. This
  places Hong Kong's recovery time between the US's 15 years and Ireland's ~16 years nationally,
  despite HK's initial decline (-66%) being far deeper than either.

## Q9. Which country China resembles — on the DATA, and on the POLICY RESPONSE (argued both ways)

**On the data (scale and structure of the boom):**
- By real-estate-share-of-GDP, China's construction+real estate footprint (~29% in one 2016
  estimate) is reported to **exceed** Japan's 1990 print (21.0%) and to **"rival Spain and Ireland
  at the peak of their construction booms,"** while both are said to **far exceed the US's ~18%**
  `[1-SOURCE]` — on this specific measure, China's economic DEPENDENCE on real estate looks most
  like Spain/Ireland's construction-driven booms, not Japan's (Japan's economy, while it had a
  massive land bubble, was not as singularly construction-dependent as Spain/Ireland were).
- By land-value-to-GDP and by the sheer multiple of the price run-up ("tenfold since the early
  1990s… an order of magnitude greater than Ireland, Spain, US" `[1-SOURCE]`; land value ~544% of
  GDP in 2017 vs Japan's 581% in 1990 `[1-SOURCE]`), China's ASSET-PRICE EXCESS looks most like
  Japan's — comparable in relative scale, and larger in absolute multiple than any of the three
  Western comparators.
- **Net read on data**: no single clean match. China combines Spain/Ireland-scale economic
  DEPENDENCE on construction with Japan-scale (or larger) asset-price EXCESS — arguably a worse
  combination than either taken alone, since it lacks Spain/Ireland's relatively contained land
  value multiple and lacks Japan's comparatively smaller GDP share.
- **The one series in this dossier where China is explicitly named as MORE extreme than the JST
  panel's own Japan 1991 line** is the price-to-income multiple ("tenfold," `[1-SOURCE]`) — a data
  point that argues China's bust, if it fully plays out to a Japan-style land correction, has
  further to fall on a pure reversion-to-fundamentals basis than Japan's own -47.3% JST print
  already shows.

**On the policy response:**
- Multiple sources explicitly reject the Japan analogy on LEVERAGE STRUCTURE: **"Japan's crisis
  centred on private banks and corporations, whereas China's vulnerabilities are more concentrated
  in local governments and state-linked entities"** `[1-SOURCE]` — meaning China's policy toolkit
  (directives to state banks, LGFV debt swaps, project "whitelists") is a state-directed workout
  that has no clean analogue among the four market-economy comparators in this dossier.
- On raw SPEED of the initial liquidity response, China (PBOC loans to state banks, July 2022;
  16-point rescue plan, November 2022 — roughly 12-18 months after Evergrande stress became
  visible in mid-2021) looks closer to **Sweden's** fast 1992 response than to **Japan's**
  near-decade delay `[2-SOURCE]`. But Sweden's problem (two insolvent private banks, cleanly
  identifiable, nationalized and carved out) was a far SIMPLER problem than China's tangle of
  LGFV/local-fiscal/developer debt, so the comparison is explicitly partial: fast on the calendar,
  but not evidently fast on actual LOSS RECOGNITION, which is the dimension Sweden's case actually
  tests.
- Several sources make exactly this loss-recognition point directly: **"the decline of asset
  prices is still not widely recognized in China, suggesting that China may be repeating some
  elements of Japan's approach"** `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e., on the recognition dimension specifically
  (as opposed to the liquidity-support dimension), China may resemble Japan's forbearance pattern
  more than Sweden's clean write-down pattern, even though the calendar-time response was faster
  than Japan's.
- **Net read on policy**: China's response is genuinely a hybrid that does not map cleanly onto
  any single comparator — faster than Japan and administratively more centralized than any of the
  four market economies on the LIQUIDITY dimension, but arguably still Japan-like on the
  RECOGNITION dimension. Both readings are sourced; presenting one without the other would misstate
  the evidence.

## Q10. What shortened property busts internationally, and the balance-sheet-recession framing

**What the cross-country evidence says shortened busts** — two levers recur across every case in
this dossier:
1. **Speed of loss recognition and bank recapitalization.** The cleanest natural experiment here
   is **Sweden 1992 vs Japan 1991**: Sweden nationalized/recapitalized two insolvent banks and
   issued a blanket depositor/creditor guarantee within months of the crisis breaking (late 1992),
   showed visible economic recovery by **1994**, and lifted the crisis guarantee by 1996 — roughly
   a **2-year** active-crisis window `[2-SOURCE]`. Japan, by contrast, is widely described as
   having taken **~7 years** to collectively recognize the scope of its banking problem and
   allowed "zombie" banks/firms to persist on unrecognized bad debt for the better part of a
   decade, with recapitalization only forced through around 1998-2003 and recovery signs not
   appearing until roughly **2002** `[2-SOURCE]` — i.e., a ~10-12 year window, five to six times
   Sweden's, with an economy of broadly comparable sophistication and the same underlying shock
   (a land-and-equity bubble collapse feeding into bank balance sheets).
2. **Debt-contract design that allows fast household deleveraging.** The US (predominantly
   non-recourse mortgages in the hardest-hit states, plus foreclosure processes measured in months)
   is contrasted with Spain/Italy/Greece (recourse mortgages, judicial foreclosure taking 18-36
   months per case, and personal-bankruptcy discharge periods of 5-7 years). One academic estimate
   attributes **up to 31%** of the US-vs-Spain post-2008 recovery GAP to the mortgage-recourse
   mechanism alone `[1-SOURCE]` — recourse systems are argued to prolong recessions both by
   discouraging strategic default (leaving households over-leveraged for longer) and by forcing
   deep, immediate consumption cuts among indebted homeowners.
3. **The pattern that does NOT shorten busts**, visible across Japan, and arguably China: providing
   liquidity/forbearance to keep insolvent institutions and projects technically alive WITHOUT
   forcing loss recognition or restructuring. This is precisely the shared thread between Japan's
   1990s banking forbearance and the "asset prices still not widely recognized" critique of
   China's current response `[1-SOURCE]` — administrative support alone, absent recognition, is
   associated with the LONGEST busts in this sample (Japan's land measures, uniquely among every
   series in this dossier, still have not recovered their nominal peak 35 years on).

**Richard Koo's "balance-sheet recession" framing, applied to China, and its main counter-argument:**
- **Koo's own application**: Koo has directly and explicitly applied the framework to China,
  arguing that once households and firms conclude (as they did in Japan from 1990) that asset
  prices will not keep rising, they shift from borrowing-to-invest to paying down debt even at
  near-zero interest rates — monetary policy loses traction, and only sustained, large
  government DEFICIT SPENDING can offset the resulting shortfall in private demand. Koo has said
  directly that China "may be facing a balance sheet recession, but it can withstand [it] with
  strong fiscal stimulus" and has compared the psychological turn in China explicitly to "Japan
  34 years ago" `[2-SOURCE]`.
- **The main counter-argument, sourced**: critics point out that Koo's own model requires a clean
  split between a deleveraging PRIVATE sector and a borrowing PUBLIC sector that steps in to fill
  the demand gap — but in China, the entities doing much of the "public" borrowing (LGFVs) are
  themselves best understood as **disguised local-government debt that is ALREADY under severe
  distress**, not a clean, healthy public balance sheet standing ready to expand `[1-SOURCE]`.
  In other words, the local-government/LGFV leg that Koo's prescription would lean on for offsetting
  fiscal expansion is arguably already part of the problem, not a solution waiting to be deployed —
  which is a structural reason China's version of the framework may not resolve as cleanly as
  Japan's did once Japan's own government eventually ran large, genuinely-public deficits. A
  second, more far-reaching counter (associated with Adam Posen's "economic long COVID" framing)
  argues the deeper problem is a POLITICAL/CONFIDENCE shock — arbitrary state interference in
  private commercial life breaking an earlier implicit compact — rather than a mechanical
  debt-overhang problem, implying that fiscal stimulus of the kind that eventually worked in Japan
  and the post-2008 US would not, by itself, restore the confidence channel Posen's account
  identifies as binding `[1-SOURCE]`. Both critiques are presented as counter-arguments to Koo's
  own reading, not as the desk's independent view.
