## Headline findings

- **Consensus has converged on "2026 is worse before better, 2027 is a floor, not a recovery"** —
  but the SPREAD across named institutions is wide enough to be a finding in itself: 2026 new-home
  price calls run from Morgan Stanley's -2% to -3% to UBS's -10%, a 3-4x range on the same year,
  same asset class `[2-SOURCE per figure, see FORECAST TABLE]`.
- **The bull case is a bet on BIFURCATION, not a national turn.** Every bull argument found this
  session (Citi, BofA, HSBC, Eurizon SLJ's Stephen Jen) is explicitly about tier-1 cities and a
  short list of stronger tier-2s recovering while lower tiers keep falling — none argues for a
  national price recovery. Shanghai is the one tier-1 city actually posting sustained year-on-year
  gains through 2026 `[2-SOURCE]`.
- **The bear case is structural, not merely "prices haven't stopped falling."** Rogoff-Yang, Pettis
  and Rhodium's Logan Wright each argue the mechanism that made property ~25% of the economy is
  gone for demographic and fiscal reasons that a price floor does not repair — the disagreement with
  bulls is about what "stabilization" can and can't fix, not about the next print `[2-SOURCE across
  the three]`.
- **Second-hand leads new-build in every recovery signal found this session, with no
  counter-example.** Resale overtook new-build in transaction share for the first time in the
  market's history in H1 2026 (50.4%) `[1-SOURCE]`, and city-level price turns in 2026 show
  secondhand prices in the four tier-1 cities rising before or alongside new-build, with new-build
  in three of the four tier-1 cities still falling on the same date `[2-SOURCE]`.
- **The ghost-city verdict is genuinely mixed, not a clean win for either camp**: of the four named
  cases, Zhengzhou's Zhengdong New District is an unambiguous fill-in (30k-era planning population
  to 945k-1.3M residents), Ordos/Kangbashi is a real partial recovery that still fell short of its
  design population, and Tianjin's Yujiapu sub-district is the one still-failing case as of the most
  recent sourced visit (2021) `[2-SOURCE across cases]`. Independent satellite evidence
  (peer-reviewed, 2018) puts a number on this: the phenomenon is real but **confined to 22 smaller
  cities**, not a national vacancy crisis `[1-SOURCE, single study, but consistent with the
  case-level pattern]`.
- **The most-cited analytical mistake is premature and repeated bottom-calling, in both
  directions** — China's second-biggest developer called the bottom in 2022 and was near default by
  2025; UBS retracted its own imminent-turnaround call in November 2025; Fitch and S&P both revised
  their 2026 sales forecasts MORE negative twice during the same year `[2-SOURCE]`. This desk's own
  CN-D3 (Half A, vaulted JST data, not a web source) gives the general form of the error a number:
  rapid mortgage-credit growth predicts a crash peak within three years only 12.8% of the time
  (an 87.2% false-positive rate) — the same error of over-reading a state variable as a timing
  signal, independently confirmed on a completely different panel `[DESK, CN-D3, trial-ledger.md]`.

## STABILIZATION CHECKLIST — specific, monitorable conditions named by analysts

Each item below is phrased so it could be tracked from a public data point, with the analyst/source
that named it:

1. **Tier-1 new-build prices turning positive YoY in more than one of the four tier-1 cities**
   (Shanghai already has; Beijing/Guangzhou/Shenzhen have not) — S&P and Morgan Stanley both frame
   tier-1-first as the leading indicator `[2-SOURCE]`.
2. **Secondary-market (resale) price momentum sustaining for 3+ consecutive months across all four
   tier-1 cities simultaneously** — UBS names "rising secondary sales" as one of its four inflection
   markers, and as of its Feb 2025 read only rental prices (of its four markers) had NOT yet turned;
   check whether all four have now turned `[1-SOURCE, UBS's own four-marker framework]`.
3. **Land-auction premium rates (the % over floor price paid at auction) turning positive in a
   sustained way, not just a low failure rate** — named by UBS as its "rising premium on land
   prices" marker `[1-SOURCE]`.
4. **National inventory (months-to-sell, or completed-unsold floor area) falling for 3+ consecutive
   months, not just decelerating its rate of increase** — S&P names this explicitly ("completed but
   unsold new housing reaching a sixth consecutive year of accumulation" as of Feb 2026 is the
   negative reading) `[1-SOURCE]`.
5. **New-home transaction VALUE (not just price) rising month-on-month for a sustained run** —
   named as a May 2026 positive data point (+11% MoM) by the same reporting that flagged the
   70-city YoY decline narrowing for the first time in eight months `[1-SOURCE]`.
6. **Rental prices turning up** — UBS's fourth inflection marker, and the one still lagging as of
   its own most recent read `[1-SOURCE]`.
7. **Developer offshore bond spreads / access to onshore bank credit normalizing** — named
   qualitatively by multiple sources (S&P's "policy support... financing conditions for
   developers") as a precondition for supply-side confidence, though no single spread threshold was
   sourced this session — flagged as a gap `[1-SOURCE, qualitative]`.
8. **Household confidence / income expectations, proxied by the job market** — repeatedly named
   (Fitch, S&P, Morningstar) as the demand-side precondition that price and inventory data alone
   cannot substitute for; no single named proxy metric was sourced, which is itself worth recording
   as a soft spot in the analyst community's own checklist `[2-SOURCE for the qualitative point, 0
   for a hard proxy]`.

**Note on structure**: items 1-5 are the ones an outside monitor could actually build from published
NBS/CREIS/Beike data without a subscription; items 6-8 are named by analysts but not reduced to a
trackable public threshold in anything sourced this session.

| **Tianjin Yujiapu (part of the Binhai New Area)** | Built from the early 2010s as a purpose-built "Chinese Manhattan" financial district — full skyline, subway station, wide boulevards, marketed on a Rockefeller-linked flagship tower (Rose Rock Financial Center) | As of the most recent sourced visit (~2021): the Rose Rock tower was never built; the nearby Goldin Finance 117 (designed as one of the world's tallest) stalled at 86 floors, an empty shell; the mall and IMAX cinema found "permanently shut"; the Yujiapu name was administratively retired in 2019 in favor of "Tianjin Binhai Station," and the Juilliard-Tianjin campus is the one genuine occupant named | **STILL LARGELY EMPTY — the one of the four that did NOT fill in, on the most recent evidence found** (Binhai as a whole, the broader district Yujiapu sits inside, DID attract population — this is specifically about the Yujiapu financial-district core) | `[2-SOURCE]` |
| **Kunming Chenggong** | Reported as one of Asia's largest ghost towns in 2012; operating at roughly 20% of planned capacity for years afterward | Population risen to **~709,700 (2023)**, up from 694,700 (2022); occupancy driven substantially by university relocations and central Kunming's own overcrowding pushing residents outward | **PARTIALLY FILLED, via a different mechanism (university/overflow demand, not the originally planned commercial function)** | `[1-SOURCE for the recent population figures; 2-SOURCE for the "was a real ghost town" baseline]` |

**The honest verdict, stated once and directly**: this is not a case of "it was all a myth" (Yujiapu
is still substantially empty on the latest evidence, and 2014-vintage Kangbashi vacancy was
genuinely ~70%) and not a case of "it was all true and permanent" either (Zhengdong is an
unambiguous success, Kangbashi and Chenggong both filled meaningfully even if short of plan). The
pattern that best fits the four cases: purpose-built districts anchored to a REAL adjacent demand
source (Zhengzhou's existing metro population needing overflow space; Kunming's universities and
its own overcrowded core) filled in over 10-15 years; a purpose-built district betting on a NEW
function with no organic anchor (Yujiapu's bet on becoming a financial center with no financial
firms to move there) did not. This is consistent with, and adds specificity to, the independent
academic finding below that the phenomenon is real but geographically confined rather than either
universal or illusory.

## Q1. The bull case, steelmanned

The bull case, at its strongest, is NOT "China property is fine" — every source found makes a
narrower, more defensible claim: **the worst of the national aggregate decline is behind, and a
specific, identifiable set of cities and market segments is already turning**, which is investable
even if the national index keeps drifting down for years.

- **Citi** upgraded the mainland China property sector from neutral to positive in 2026, arguing the
  market is "positioned for recovery," citing "stronger data and improving sentiment," and framing
  "a slow recovery in key cities as an optimal scenario for property names amid policy support"
  `[1-SOURCE, Bloomberg/Yahoo, dated ~May 2026]`. Citi separately projected 13-18% development
  margin recovery by 1H26 for Hong Kong-listed developers with mainland exposure `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **BofA Global Research** sees the recovery as already "taking hold," favoring developers with
  concentrated exposure to top cities and minimal legacy (lower-tier) inventory by name (China
  Resources Land, China Overseas Land & Investment, C&D International) `[1-SOURCE]` — the bull
  thesis operationalized as a specific, testable stock-picking rule rather than a macro call.
- **HSBC's** regional head of real estate research called 2026 a "critical year of inflection"
  `[1-SOURCE]`, and HSBC and Citigroup together were reported touting Chinese property stocks amid
  what HSBC framed as a "structural market recovery" `[1-SOURCE, SCMP]`.
- **Eurizon SLJ Capital (Stephen Jen)** made the most explicit bottom call: 2026 will "likely" be
  the year the market bottoms as demand recovers, arguing China is handling property deflation
  "quite well" — supply contracting in step with demand, with household savings providing fresh
  fuel `[2-SOURCE, Bloomberg + the firm's own site]`.
- **JPMorgan** is the most conditional of the bulls: analyst Karl Chan said "the real test is in
  July and August" 2026, and that continued solid data through that window would let JPMorgan "turn
  even more positive on the sector" `[1-SOURCE]` — a bull case stated as a falsifiable near-term
  test rather than a settled call.
- **The steelman, stated as its advocates would state it**: China is not one market. Tier-1 cities,
  led by Shanghai, are already showing sustained price gains, land-auction interest, and (per
  UBS's own framework) three of four inflection markers turning; policy has shifted decisively
  demand-side (lower down payments, eased purchase restrictions, mortgage-rate floors cut, VAT cuts
  on resale from Jan 2026); household savings are large and under-deployed; and the market segment
  bulls are actually betting on — prime, low-inventory, high-demand-density urban cores — is a
  small enough slice of the national stock that it can clear its own overhang years before the
  national aggregate does. A "China property is dead money" read that doesn't distinguish Shanghai
  from Yingkou is simply using the wrong unit of analysis, on this view.

## Q2. The bear case, steelmanned

- **Kenneth Rogoff and Yuanchen Yang** (Harvard/Brookings; "Peak China Housing," 2020, and "China's
  Real Estate Challenge," IMF Finance & Development, Dec 2024, Vol. 61 No. 4) make the highest-
  profile structural bear case: property (direct + indirect demand) was ~25% of China's economy in
  2021, rising to 31% with infrastructure included; cities that have already built up their stock
  gain far less from further investment (a mechanical, not merely cyclical, argument); and Rogoff's
  own framing, from a March 2026 Brookings paper presentation, is that "Three red lines was a
  trigger but the real estate slump was going to happen anyway because of the fundamentals —
  demographics and oversupply" `[2-SOURCE]`. Rogoff frames China as roughly HALFWAY through its
  adjustment if it follows Japan's ~60% peak-to-trough path, or two-thirds through if it follows the
  milder US 2008 path `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Michael Pettis** (Carnegie/Peking University) argues the deeper problem is macro-structural, not
  sector-specific: an economy that systematically channels resources toward investment and
  production rather than household income and consumption will keep generating oversupply
  somewhere, and property is simply where several decades of that channeling landed; his own
  characterization (March 2026) is that when production capacity outruns household/business
  purchasing power, prices must fall to clear inventory — a mechanism that a property-specific
  rescue does not fix `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Logan Wright** (Rhodium Group, China Markets Research) makes the bear case most concretely
  investable: property is ~20-25% of the economy and 30-35% of bank credit; Beijing's substitute
  growth engines (AI, robotics, EVs) added only 0.8 percentage points to output from 2023-2025 while
  real estate and other traditional sectors combined for a 6-point decline over the same period —
  "China's growth strategy isn't going to work" on current policy, in his words, because the
  replacement isn't big enough yet `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **The steelman, stated as its advocates would state it**: every bull argument is a claim about
  TIER-1 cities, which are a small fraction of China's housing stock and an even smaller fraction of
  its land-sale-dependent local government finances. Tier-3-and-below cities hold an estimated
  50-78% of national housing stock and unsold inventory (this desk's own `c-tier3.md`, Rogoff-Yang
  sourced) and have shown no comparable turn. A demographic decline in the prime home-buying cohort,
  an already-built stock that in many cities exceeds any plausible household-formation demand for
  decades, and a local-government fiscal model built on land sales that themselves depend on the
  property cycle recovering, together mean that "stabilization" in the bull sense (prices stop
  falling in the best cities) is compatible with the bear thesis (the sector never again plays the
  20-25%-of-GDP role it played from 2003-2021) being simultaneously true. The two camps may not
  actually disagree about the next 12 months; they disagree about what a stabilized market is worth
  to the growth model afterward.

## Q5. Any city or segment that has actually stabilized or risen over the last 12 months

**Shanghai** is the clearest, best-sourced case: the only one of China's four tier-1 cities to post
a year-on-year INCREASE in primary (new-build) home prices through the first four months of 2026,
and new-build prices up **+3.1% year-on-year in June 2026** — the same month the other three tier-1
cities were still down **-2.3% to -5.3%** over the same window `[2-SOURCE]`. Separately, Shanghai's
existing-home (secondhand) sales hit a nearly six-year high, with buyers concentrating on "old,
small, and cheap" units `[1-SOURCE]`. **Shenyang** is named alongside Shanghai as a "pillar of
stability" in June 2026 reporting `[1-SOURCE]`. At a broader level, **China's first-tier cities as a
class** posted new-home price gains month-on-month for a fourth consecutive month in May 2026, and
the 70-city index's year-on-year DECLINE narrowed for the first time in eight months that same month
`[1-SOURCE]`. No tier-2 or tier-3 city was found with a sustained 12-month RISE (as opposed to a
narrowing decline) — flagged as a real absence, not an oversight.

## Q6. Second-hand versus new-build: which leads?

**Second-hand leads, unambiguously, in every case sourced this session.** The structural reason
given (secondary market's greater liquidity and individual sellers' greater willingness/ability to
cut price versus developers constrained by local reference-price floors and presale price caps) is
consistent across sources `[2-SOURCE]`. The clearest single data point: **in H1 2026, second-hand
homes accounted for 50.4% of residential transactions nationally — the first time in the history of
China's modern property market that resale volume has overtaken new-build** `[1-SOURCE]`. At the
city level, pre-owned home prices across Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou and Shenzhen rose for a third
consecutive month as of May 2026 (+0.4% MoM), ending an 11-month consecutive secondhand decline,
while new-build in three of those four cities was still falling on the same date `[2-SOURCE]`. One
outlet frames this explicitly as "the secondary market leads recovery" as the "key turning-point
signal" for the sector `[1-SOURCE]`.

## Q7. The "L-shaped" versus "U-shaped" debate

- **The L-shaped camp is the larger and more prominently sourced one.** **Oxford Economics** titles
  its own house view "Look for an L-shaped property recovery with a long tail in China"
  `[1-SOURCE]`. **Goldman Sachs** analysts, per SCMP reporting (2022-vintage but restated in
  substantially the same terms through this session's more recent searches), assume "no quick fix"
  for the sector and model an L-shaped recovery given persistent lower-tier weakness and private
  developer financing constraints `[1-SOURCE, and consistent with Goldman's own 2026-27 forecast
  numbers in the FORECAST TABLE, which show a slow, multi-year grind rather than a bounce]`.
- **The U-shaped (or "soft landing / long correction runway") camp** argues a market adjustment IS
  necessary but that risks are tilted toward a soft landing — sluggish prices and activity persist
  for an extended period, but China's earlier stage of urbanization (versus Japan in 1990) provides
  a structural demand floor that supports an eventual, if moderate-paced, recovery rather than a
  permanent plateau `[1-SOURCE]`. CITIC SEC's "stabilize by 2026" call and the various bank notes
  projecting a 2027 floor followed by modest 2028 gains (Reuters poll: +0.5%) are the closest this
  session found to an explicit U-shape (a genuine trough followed by a recovery leg), as distinct
  from an L-shape (a fall followed by a flat, non-recovering plateau).
- **Both sides' own reasoning, stated fairly**: L-shape advocates point to the SAME structural
  facts as the bear case in Q2 — demographics, an already-built stock, and a local-government fiscal
  model that cannot easily reflate land sales — to argue the "recovery leg" bulls expect is not
  coming at all, only an indefinite plateau at a lower level. U-shape/soft-landing advocates counter
  that China's urbanization rate (still meaningfully below Japan's or South Korea's at equivalent
  income levels, on this argument) provides genuine underlying demand that an L-shape thesis
  under-weights, and that the SPEED of the adjustment (this desk's own CN-D1/CN-D4 base rate: a
  bigger boom buys a faster unwind, not merely a deeper one, at roughly the historical median 5-7
  year duration) argues for a real trough-and-recovery shape arriving on a more compressed timeline
  than Japan's multi-decade L `[DESK, CN-D1/CN-D4, not part of this dossier's web search — cited for
  contrast only]`.

## Q8. The ghost cities, settled properly (see GHOST CITY STATUS table above)

The table and its verdict paragraph above are the full answer. Restated once, briefly: the four
best-documented cases split 1 clear success (Zhengdong), 2 partial/real-but-incomplete recoveries
(Kangbashi, Chenggong), and 1 still-failing case on the latest evidence (Yujiapu specifically, as
distinct from the broader Tianjin Binhai area that surrounds it, which DID gain population). Both
popular reflexes — "it was all overblown Western media sensationalism" and "China is permanently
full of empty cities" — are wrong; the correct statement is city- and even district-specific, and
the mechanism that distinguishes success from failure (an organic adjacent demand source vs. a bet
on a wholly new function) is visible in the four cases themselves.

## Q9. The academic satellite/mobile-data literature on Chinese urban vacancy

Two genuinely distinct methodologies were sourced, and their findings and limits should not be
conflated:

- **Nighttime-lights + daytime satellite imagery**: Lu, Zhang, Liu, Ye & Miao, "Mapping China's
  Ghost Cities through the Combination of Nighttime Satellite Data and Daytime Satellite Data,"
  *Remote Sensing* 10(7):1037 (2018) `[1-SOURCE, but the paper itself and its abstract are
  independently indexed/mirrored across ResearchGate, MDPI and DOAJ, so the core finding is treated
  as solid despite the single-study tag]`. Method: DMSP/OLS nighttime brightness combined with a
  Normalized Difference Built-up Index (daytime) to track whether newly-built areas' nighttime
  brightness follows the expected inverted-U curve of occupation, or instead declines/never rises —
  the signature of a genuinely vacant district. **Finding: the ghost-city problem is real but, as of
  the study's data, confined to 22 smaller cities** — i.e., an explicit, bounded number rather than
  either "a myth" or "nationwide." **Limitation, stated by this literature itself and independently
  by other papers in this search**: nighttime-lights resolution is too coarse to detect vacancy at
  the individual-building or individual-unit level, is confounded by non-residential lighting
  (streetlights, commercial signage installed regardless of occupancy), and measures whether a WHOLE
  DISTRICT got occupied — not whether any given apartment inside an otherwise-lit district is empty.
- **Social-media / POI (point-of-interest) proximity data**: Williams, Xu, Tan, Foster & Chen,
  "Ghost cities of China: Identifying urban vacancy through social media data," *Cities* 94:275-285
  `[1-SOURCE]`. Method: uses proximity to amenities scraped from social/mapping platforms
  (restaurants, schools, grocery stores, banks, malls, KTVs, beauty salons, medical facilities) as a
  proxy for whether an area functions as a live neighborhood — the underlying logic being that a
  vacant district under-supports amenity businesses, which is independently observable even where
  building-level occupancy isn't. **Finding**: eight "underload" (low-occupancy) cities identified
  with computed load ratios, e.g. Zhuzhou 67.4%, Dongying 65.4%, Haikou 57.9%, Qinhuangdao 54.1%,
  Taizhou (Jiangsu) 52.4%, Jiaxing 47.3%, Ma'anshan 46.2%, Zhuhai 16.9% `[1-SOURCE]` — a DIFFERENT
  city list from the nighttime-lights study, which is itself informative (the two methods are not
  simply cross-validating the same list; they are triangulating from different angles and land on
  overlapping but non-identical sets of "at-risk" cities). **Limitation**: amenity density is an
  indirect proxy — a genuinely poor or newly-built area can under-supply amenities without being
  vacant, and the method cannot distinguish "vacant" from "occupied but under-served."
- **Neither methodology supports a single national vacancy rate or unit count.** Both explicitly
  contradict the more extreme viral framings (tens of millions to "1.4 billion people probably
  can't fill them all" estimates sourced elsewhere in this programme's `c-datatrust.md`) by finding
  a bounded, specific, named set of affected cities rather than a national crisis — this is the
  single most important methodological takeaway for how to read ANY China vacancy headline: ask
  whether it is a survey-based estimate (like Gan Li's CHFS, a different and more credible
  methodology entirely, already covered in `c-datatrust.md`), a disputed electricity-meter estimate,
  or one of these two remote-sensing/digital-trace methods, because they measure different things
  and are not interchangeable.

## Q10. The most common analytical mistake about Chinese property

**Premature and repeated bottom-calling (and, on the bear side, premature top/collapse-calling) —
treating a multi-month data print or a policy announcement as a regime change rather than a point in
a distribution — is the most concrete, best-attested pattern found this session, on both sides of
the debate:**

- **On the bull/turnaround side**: China's second-biggest developer declared the property downturn's
  worst was over within months of it starting; a year later its own chairman walked that call back;
  by 2025 the same company was reported teetering on the brink of default `[2-SOURCE]`. **UBS itself
  scrapped a prediction that a turnaround was imminent as recently as November 2025** `[1-SOURCE]` —
  a specific, dated instance of a top-tier house making and then retracting exactly this error
  within a single year. Fitch and S&P both revised their OWN 2026 sales forecasts more negative
  TWICE within the same year (Fitch: an original ~-7%/-8% call widened to -11%/-13%; S&P: an earlier
  -5%/-8% call widened to -10%/-14% by its Feb 2026 report) `[2-SOURCE]` — each revision was, in its
  moment, presented with the same confidence as the one it replaced.
- **On the bear/collapse side**: Jim Chanos called a "world-class property bubble" bursting by late
  2010 or early 2011; Chinese property prices were instead up roughly 70% in the aggregate by the
  time that timeline had elapsed `[1-SOURCE]` — directionally vindicated only a decade-plus later,
  which is functionally the same error (mistaking a correct STRUCTURAL diagnosis for a correct
  TIMING call) pointed the other way.
- **This desk's own quantified version of the same error (Half A, not a web source, cited for
  context)**: CN-D3 found that rapid 5-year mortgage-credit growth — the best-documented early
  warning indicator in the international housing-bust literature — lifts the odds of a crash peak
  within 3 years by 1.72x over the base rate, but is still WRONG 87.2% of the time at that horizon.
  The general lesson CN-D3 states for this desk applies directly to every bottom-call and top-call
  catalogued above: **a real, structural signal (credit is elevated; sentiment/data has improved)
  is not the same statement as a timing call ("therefore the turn is now"), and the base rate for
  turning a structural signal into a correctly-timed call is poor even for the single best-attested
  indicator in the literature** `[DESK, CN-D3]`. Every named miss above — Country Garden's 2022 call,
  UBS's November 2025 retraction, Chanos's decade-early timing, and the market-wide pattern of
  forecasts being revised in the SAME direction repeatedly through 2026 rather than settling — is a
  specific instance of exactly this error, made by analysts who were not naive about China and had
  real data in front of them at the time.
