## Headline findings

- **The steel/deflation channel is real, quantified, and now has a policy response.** China's
  steel exports roughly doubled from ~66-67Mt/yr (2021-22) to 110.7Mt (2024) to a record 119.0Mt
  (2025) `[2-SOURCE]`, and Indian HRC benchmark prices fell from ~₹59,900/t (Apr 2023) to
  ~₹52,600/t (Apr 2024) `[1-SOURCE]` while Indian steelmakers' profitability fell 58-95% YoY in
  Q1 FY25 `[1-SOURCE]`, prompting first a 12% temporary safeguard duty (Apr-Nov 2025) and then a
  proposed structured 11-12% duty declining 1pp/yr `[2-SOURCE]`. This is the SAME mechanism this
  desk already booked in `docs/cycles/24-china-impulse.md` (real-estate's own share of Chinese
  steel demand fell 32%→23%, 2021→2025) — the export-flood channel is that mechanism's mirror
  image: as China's own property-linked steel appetite shrank, the surplus capacity was redirected
  outward rather than idled.
- **Cement is NOT the same channel — international cement trade is structurally thin (~5% of
  global consumption, high weight-to-value ratio kills long-haul economics) `[1-SOURCE]`, so
  Chinese cement oversupply reaches India as a CAUTIONARY EXAMPLE (India's own capacity build-out
  risks the same 45%-of-global-overcapacity outcome China now carries) rather than as a direct
  import-price shock** — a genuinely different transmission from steel, worth stating explicitly
  rather than assumed by analogy.
- **The EM-flow rotation is real, large, and MSCI-documented, but its own 2024 reversal is the
  best evidence that it is NOT purely (or even primarily) property-driven.** MSCI EM weights moved
  China ~40%→~25-27%, India ~8%→~18-19% between 2020 and mid-2024 `[2-SOURCE]`; then in October
  2024 a single Chinese stimulus announcement (PBoC's 24-Sep-2024 package) triggered ~$10.2bn of
  FPI outflows from India in one month as active GEM managers rotated back toward newly-cheap
  Chinese equities `[2-SOURCE]` — named sources (Goldman Sachs, Morgan Stanley) frame the rotation
  as VALUATION- and GROWTH-differential-driven, with property only one input to the China growth
  discount, not the sole one.
- **India's own residential cycle is a genuine multi-year upturn, not a bubble echo of China's**:
  a 2013-2020ish downturn (weak pricing, high inventory) gave way to a post-2021 recovery driven by
  low rates, developer consolidation, and RERA-forced supply discipline `[2-SOURCE]`, with current
  inventory overhang narrowing sharply in Mumbai (57mo Q2'24 → 49mo Q2'25) and Gurugram (~9mo) but
  still elevated in Noida (~56mo) and worsening in Chennai (15mo → 25mo) `[1-SOURCE per city cut]`
  — the cycle is real but city-divergent, not a single national number.
- **Listed developer leverage today looks nothing like the 2018-2020 NBFC/IL&FS era**: DLF is
  effectively net-debt-free (D/E ~0.0, FY25) `[1-SOURCE]`, Oberoi Realty carries D/E ~0.18
  `[1-SOURCE]`, Godrej Properties nearly halved net debt in a single year (D/E 0.65→0.23,
  FY24→FY25) `[1-SOURCE]` — consistent with the RERA/IBC-forced consolidation toward well-
  capitalized large developers that Forbes India and Business Standard both document
  `[2-SOURCE]`, though this is a listed-large-cap read only; the unlisted/Tier-2 developer tail is
  not covered by this dossier's sourcing.
- **India carries almost none of China's SPECIFIC structural vulnerabilities at the mechanism
  level** (see the table below) — RERA's 70%-escrow, construction-linked-withdrawal rule is a
  first-order design difference from China's pre-2021 presale system, not a cosmetic one; Indian
  state/local government fiscal dependence on land sales is a fraction of China's 27-38%; India's
  property GDP share (narrow ~7-8%, broad ~13-15%) sits well below China's narrow 22-23% / broad
  25-31%. The one mechanism that DOES transfer, partially, is the LGFV-equivalent SPV/off-budget-
  borrowing structure some Indian states and urban local bodies use — genuinely analogous in kind,
  much smaller in scale.

## Q1. Chinese steel export volumes since 2021 and the effect on Indian domestic prices and margins

**Volumes** `[2-SOURCE across multiple industry-press aggregators, all pointing the same
direction and rough magnitude]`: China's steel exports ran at a stabilized ~66-67 million tonnes/
year through 2021-2022 (domestic construction demand was still absorbing capacity), then surged as
domestic property demand collapsed: **90.3Mt in 2023** (+36.2% YoY), **110.7Mt in 2024** (+22.7%
YoY), and a further record **119.0Mt in 2025** (+7.5% YoY, full-year figure). That is roughly a
**+80% rise in export volume from the 2021-22 plateau to 2025** — a genuinely large, sustained
redirection of surplus capacity outward, not a one-year spike. This is the mirror image of the
desk's own already-booked finding in `docs/cycles/24-china-impulse.md`: property's own share of
Chinese steel CONSUMPTION fell from ~32% (2021) to ~23% (2025) and total Chinese steel consumption
fell ~17% (close to 1 billion tonnes in 2021 to 830Mt in 2025) — the volumes that construction
stopped absorbing domestically are close to the volumes that show up as incremental exports.

**Effect on Indian prices** `[1-SOURCE, ICICIdirect/Tata Nexarc + corroborating Business Standard
coverage of the same period]`: Indian HRC (hot-rolled coil, the flat-steel benchmark) fell from
~₹59,900/tonne (Apr 2023) to ~₹52,600/tonne (Apr 2024), and from ~₹55,400/tonne (Jun 2023) to
~₹53,800/tonne (Jun 2024) — a mid-single-digit-percent decline even as India's OWN steel demand
kept growing (construction, autos, capital goods all expanding per ICICIdirect's own read), i.e.
this is a case of Indian prices falling INTO a growing-demand backdrop purely on import pressure,
the textbook "exported deflation" signature. Chinese export prices were quoted around $520-525/
tonne against Indian EXPORT prices of $650-660/tonne in the same window `[1-SOURCE]` — a ~20% gap
even before landed-cost/duty is added, which is why volumes moved: India's rolled-product imports
from China rose to 1.1 million tonnes in April-August 2024 alone, +31.7% YoY, a seven-year high
`[1-SOURCE]`.

**Effect on producer margins** `[1-SOURCE, gmk.center citing Ind-Ra + corroborated qualitatively by
Tata Steel's own management commentary]`: Indian steelmakers' profitability fell 58-95% YoY in Q1
FY2024-25 (Apr-Jun) on the combination of falling realizations and rising input costs; Tata Steel's
India PAT fell from ₹4,995cr to ₹3,335cr in the same Q1 YoY comparison even as its per-tonne EBITDA
recovered somewhat by FY25 full-year (₹8,335/t) `[1-SOURCE]`; JSW Steel's domestic prices hit an
over-three-year low in Q2 FY25 with saleable steel volumes down 3.3% `[1-SOURCE]`; Tata Steel's own
CEO T.V. Narendran was quoted directly attributing the margin pressure to "cheap Chinese imports"
hurting the domestic market `[1-SOURCE, a named-executive primary quote via Business Standard]`.

**Policy response, dated**: a temporary 12% safeguard duty was imposed for 200 days from April
2025 (China + Korea + Japan supplied 78% of India's finished-steel imports in FY24-25) `[2-SOURCE]`;
on expiry, government considered a structured 11-12% duty declining 1pp/yr for three years
`[1-SOURCE]`; separately, a 5-year anti-dumping duty of $223.82-$414.92/tonne was imposed on
Chinese cold-rolled non-oriented (CRNO) electrical steel `[1-SOURCE]`. CRISIL is quoted expecting
the safeguard duty itself to lift steel prices 4-6% in 2025 `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e. the policy response
is itself now a quantifiable, dated input to the India steel margin outlook, not just the Chinese
export volume.

## Q2. Cement, and any other industrial channel where Chinese oversupply reaches Indian producers

**Cement is structurally NOT a direct-import channel, and this matters — the naive "China exports
deflation in cement too" analogy is wrong.** Cement has one of the lowest value-to-weight ratios of
any traded industrial good; global cement TRADE is only ~5% of global consumption precisely
because inland/ocean freight costs dominate the delivered price beyond a few hundred kilometres
`[1-SOURCE]`. China's cement EXPORTS were only ~7.3 million tonnes in 2018 against production of
2,370 million tonnes (58% of world production) `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e. even at the peak of China's
building boom, well under 1% of its own output was ever exported. China's excess cement capacity is
estimated at 895 million tonnes, ~45% of GLOBAL cement overcapacity `[1-SOURCE]` — but that
overcapacity sits stranded domestically, not flooding world markets, because it structurally
cannot travel far enough to matter.

**The actual channel for India is CAUTIONARY, not import-price-driven**: one industry analysis
frames China's own 45%-of-global-overcapacity outcome explicitly as a warning for India's own
cement sector, which is itself the world's SECOND-largest producer (~290Mt vs China's 2,370Mt,
an 8x gap) and is on a capacity build-out path toward ~550 mtpa by 2025 `[1-SOURCE]` — the read is
"don't repeat China's overbuild," not "China's oversupply is hitting our price today." Indian
cement export ambitions are themselves constrained by the SAME freight-cost physics plus
protectionist trade barriers abroad, capping cement exports at just ~5% of global consumption
industry-wide `[1-SOURCE]` — so cement is a case where the desk should explicitly NOT extend the
steel logic by analogy.

**Other industrial channels found, each independently sourced**: (i) **Chemicals** — weak Chinese
domestic demand has pushed Chinese chemical/industrial-input exporters toward aggressive pricing
into India, intensifying competition for domestic Indian chemical producers `[1-SOURCE]`; (ii)
**Solar cells/modules** — over 80% of India's installed solar modules are imported from China
`[1-SOURCE]`, and India's DGTR found Chinese solar cells dumped at margins of 105-115% with an
injury margin of 35-40%, leading to a final anti-dumping duty tiered 23-30% over three years,
effective following a December 2024 investigation `[2-SOURCE]`; (iii) **Electrical/specialty
steel and glass fibre** — DGTR issued 15 final anti-dumping findings in a single month (September
2025) spanning glass fibre, steel, solar cells, and chemical products, indicating the dumping
channel is broad-based, not steel-specific `[1-SOURCE]`; China has separately challenged India's
own EV/solar subsidy measures at the WTO, framing the trade friction as two-directional `[1-SOURCE]`.
This connects directly to this desk's own already-booked "new three" finding (`docs/cycles/
24-china-impulse.md`): China's pivot toward solar/battery/EV EXPORTS as domestic property-linked
demand shrank is the same reallocation-of-surplus-capacity mechanism driving the steel numbers in
Q1, now visible in a second product category with its own dedicated Indian trade-remedy response.

## Q3. Which Indian sectors benefit and which suffer — sourced analyst views

**Suffer** `[2-SOURCE across independent outlets]`: **Metals** (Nifty Metal index named repeatedly
as a leading sectoral loser on China-slowdown days; Jindal Steel, JSW Steel, Vedanta named as
worst-hit constituents `[1-SOURCE]`, though this coexists with the desk's own booked "sector
tailwind for India's own metals producers" finding from the COMMODITY-PRICE side of the China
channel — the two are not contradictory: China weakness is bad for Indian metals margins via the
IMPORT-COMPETITION channel (this dossier's Q1) but was historically a tailwind via the GLOBAL-PRICE
channel when China was a NET DEMAND source; the 2021-2025 period is unusual precisely because
China has flipped from demand source to export competitor in the same product); **chemicals**
(margin pressure from cheap Chinese imports, `[1-SOURCE]`); **tyres** (Balkrishna Industries, MRF,
Apollo Tyres, CEAT, JK Tyre, TVS Srichakra all named as near-term negatively exposed to a China
slowdown by one sell-side-style source `[1-SOURCE]`); **solar-cell/module manufacturers**
pre-tariff (import dependence >80% meant Chinese oversupply directly undercut any nascent domestic
manufacturing economics before the late-2025 duty, `[1-SOURCE]`).

**Benefit** `[1-SOURCE, qualitative consensus across several of the same articles]`: the "China
Plus One" manufacturing-diversification thesis — a slowing, more expensive-to-operate-in China is
cited as an accelerant for manufacturing relocation toward India `[1-SOURCE]`; domestic solar
manufacturers ONCE the PLI scheme and anti-dumping duty take effect (a forward-looking, not-yet-
realized benefit per the same sourcing, since the immediate pre-duty effect was negative); India's
own steel producers benefit from the ANTI-DUMPING/safeguard duty regime itself (a policy-created
benefit, not a market one) and from India's underlying domestic steel-consumption growth continuing
regardless of the import-price fight (`ICICIdirect`'s own framing: infra/auto/capital-goods demand
"remains on a firmer growth path" even as import competition squeezes margins) `[1-SOURCE]`.

**Reading with care**: none of the sourcing found here is a dedicated, quantified sell-side sector
model of the China-property-deflation channel specifically (as distinct from China-slowdown
generally) — most of what this search surfaced conflates "China's economy is slowing" with
"China's property sector specifically is deflating," which are related but not identical questions.
The steel and solar findings (Q1/Q2) are the two places this dossier can attach a genuinely
property-specific mechanism (steel's own 32%→23% property-demand-share collapse); the tyres/
chemicals/broader-metals findings are best read as "China slowdown, generally" rather than
"China property bust, specifically" — a distinction worth preserving rather than blurring.

## Q4. The documented rotation of EM equity allocations from China to India since 2021

**MSCI EM index weights** `[2-SOURCE, Northern Trust + Business Standard/Morgan Stanley coverage
both point to the same direction and rough magnitude, though exact percentages vary slightly by
snapshot date and whether the cite is EM-only or EM+frontier]`: China peaked at just over 40% of
MSCI EM around 2020-2021; by May 2024 it had fallen to ~27% (one source) or ~25.4% (a February 2024
snapshot from a different source); by March 2025 one source puts China back at 30.65% (consistent
with the 2024 stimulus-driven partial recovery this dossier documents below). India moved from ~8%
(2020) to ~18% (2024), described by Morgan Stanley as India "topping China for the first time ever"
in one specific MSCI sub-index context in 2024 `[1-SOURCE]` (note: a separate, LARGER-cap-inclusive
MSCI comparison in December 2025 puts India at 2.35% vs China 2.24% of a broader global index, not
the EM index specifically — flagged as a different denominator, not double-counted as a second EM
data point).

**Flow figures**: since 2021, MSCI India has gained 39% cumulatively even as MSCI EM overall
DECLINED 28% over the same span; in 2023 alone MSCI India rose 7.4% while MSCI EM fell 2.7%
`[1-SOURCE]`. On the ACTIVE-MANAGER-POSITIONING side (a different, faster-moving measure than the
passive index-weight numbers above): active Global Emerging Market (GEM) fund allocations to India
fell to 16.7% by mid-2025 (lowest since November 2023) while China's ACTIVE allocation rose to
28.8% `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e. by 2025 active managers had already begun UN-rotating relative to the
2021-2023 India-overweight trade, a genuinely important qualifier the desk should not miss when
reading "the rotation" as a one-way, still-in-progress trend. FDI-flow data (a different measure
again, capturing direct rather than portfolio investment) shows India's own inbound FDI falling
43.8% in 2023 vs 2022 ($49.94bn→$28.09bn) even as China's fell even harder (73% in the same window,
$190bn→$51bn) `[1-SOURCE]` — both economies saw FDI contract sharply post-2022, a global-liquidity
story at least as much as a bilateral-rotation one. Direct China-to-India FDI itself is trivial:
just $2.5bn cumulative April 2000-March 2025, 0.3% of India's total inbound FDI, ranking China 23rd
among source countries `[1-SOURCE]` — the "rotation" is an ALLOCATION-WITHIN-EM story among global
portfolio managers, not a story of Chinese capital itself relocating to India.

## Q5. Is the rotation property-driven or something else — and the reversal risk

**Named-source framing is explicitly NOT primarily property-driven.** Morgan Stanley (joining CLSA
and Nomura) frames its India-overweight call around "relative economic/earnings growth... improving
and domestic flows continu[ing]" plus multipolar-world FDI/portfolio dynamics — growth
differential and domestic-flow structure, not the property crisis per se `[1-SOURCE]`. Goldman
Sachs is on record making the "strategic case for separating China [EM] allocations" while citing
India's "domestically-driven... stronger-for-longer growth story... underpinned by structural
reforms" as the affirmative India case `[1-SOURCE]` — again growth/reform-centric framing.
Property enters Goldman's OWN China-side reasoning specifically via a bank-sector downgrade
(market-weight → underweight on Chinese banks' property-crisis EXPOSURE) `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e.
property is A channel Goldman names for why CHINA is unattractive, not the stated reason FOR
India; the rotation is better read as "China property crisis is one input among several (growth
differential, governance/reform trajectory, index-methodology/FX effects) making China less
attractive, combined independently with an India growth/reform bull case," rather than a single
mono-causal property story.

**The reversal risk is not hypothetical — it already happened, once, dated.** The PBoC's 24
September 2024 stimulus package (0.5pp RRR cut releasing ~RMB1tn, a 0.2pp reverse-repo cut, existing
mortgage-rate relief, and a second-home minimum down payment cut from 25% to 15% — this desk's own
already-booked figures, `docs/cycles/24-china-impulse.md`) triggered the CSI 300's biggest single-
day gain since July 2020, and by October 2024 India saw ~₹85,790cr (~$10.2bn) of FPI outflows in a
single month, explicitly attributed by Indian financial press to investors redirecting capital
toward "newly appealing" Chinese valuations `[2-SOURCE]`. FPI inflows into India dropped sharply
across 2024 overall (multiple negative months: Jan, Apr, May, Oct, Nov) with elevated Indian
valuations cited as a compounding factor alongside the China stimulus pull `[1-SOURCE]`. This is
direct, dated evidence for the risk the question asks about: a genuine Chinese policy success (even
one still ultimately unresolved on the property side specifically) is sufficient to reverse a large
slice of the rotation within a single month, because the rotation's own mechanism — relative
VALUATION and growth-expectation revision by active global allocators — is inherently reversible in
a way a structural/fundamental re-rating would not be.

## Q6. India's own residential cycle 2013-2026: downturn, upturn, current inventory by city

**The downturn (2013-~2020)**: multiple sources converge on describing a "protracted downturn"
from 2013, characterized by weak pricing, limited transaction volumes, and rising unsold inventory
`[2-SOURCE]`; this overlaps with, and was worsened by, the 2018-2020 NBFC/IL&FS funding freeze
(Q7 below) which cut off developer working capital precisely when the sector already needed
demand-side support.

**The post-2021 upturn**: recovery is attributed to a specific combination — low interest rates,
a price correction that had already occurred, state-government stimulus (stamp-duty cuts during
COVID in some states), continued INDUSTRY CONSOLIDATION toward fewer/larger developers, a
historical decline in new launches that shrank available inventory ahead of demand recovery,
increased regulatory oversight (RERA maturing), and a genuine demand-side shift toward larger homes
post-work-from-home `[2-SOURCE]`. Knight Frank's most recent research (H2 2025) shows continued
price growth concentrated in NCR, Bengaluru, and Hyderabad, with the caveat that "continuous price
growth through FY2024 has pushed rates to near-peak levels in several cities, impacting
affordability" `[1-SOURCE]` — i.e. the desk should read the upturn as MATURE, not early-cycle, by
2025-2026.

**Current inventory months by city** (Anarock, most recent available cuts) `[1-SOURCE per city
pair, cross-referenced across two Anarock-summary articles that broadly agree]`: **Gurugram ~9
months** (tight) vs **Noida ~56 months** (a stark intra-NCR divergence — treating "Delhi-NCR" as
one number would be a material error); **Greater Mumbai eased from 57 months (Q2'24) to 49 months
(Q2'25)** — improving but still elevated in absolute terms; **Bengaluru ~12-15 months**
(comfortable, "supply present but not excessive" per Anarock's own characterization), though
Bengaluru's UNSOLD UNIT COUNT rose 23% YoY even as the MONTHS-of-inventory metric stayed reasonable
(a reminder that units-outstanding and months-to-clear can diverge when sales velocity itself is
rising); **Chennai deteriorated sharply, 15 months → 25 months** YoY, the steepest inventory-
overhang WORSENING of any major city in this dossier's sourcing; **Pune** described qualitatively
as "tightening" with strong absorption but no specific months figure found this session. Aggregate
unsold stock across the top-7 cities: ~5.62-6.01 lakh units through 2025, edging up to ~6.16 lakh
by Q2 2026 on some counts — a modest, not alarming, national aggregate trend, but one masking the
sharp city-level divergence documented above `[1-SOURCE]`.

## Q7. Indian developer leverage now vs the 2018-2020 NBFC/IL&FS crisis, and RERA/IBC consolidation

**What broke in 2018-2020**: the IL&FS default (September 2018) froze commercial-paper rollovers
across the NBFC sector; NBFCs and HFCs had financed ~60% of property developers' loans, so the
freeze transmitted directly into developer funding costs, which rose 200-300bps (NBFC rates to
18-19%, PE-fund rates as high as 23%) `[1-SOURCE]`; Tier-2/3 developers found credit access
difficult even once markets normalized, i.e. the shock was asymmetric and favored large/
well-capitalized players from the start `[1-SOURCE]`.

**The consolidation mechanism, precisely**: RERA's 70% escrow rule (Q10 below) made capital
genuinely scarcer for under-capitalized developers exactly as the NBFC freeze hit, and the
combination pushed the industry toward large, well-capitalized corporate-house developers who
could self-fund construction without relying on pre-sale cash flow diversion `[1-SOURCE]`; the IBC
framework then provided a formal exit mechanism for failed projects, though one source flags that
IBC proceedings against real-estate projects specifically were sometimes used by speculative
investors as a coercive debt-recovery tool rather than a genuine revival mechanism, a friction
between IBC's general-purpose design and real-estate's project-specific, homebuyer-heavy creditor
base `[1-SOURCE]`.

**Leverage today, listed large-cap cohort** `[1-SOURCE per company, precise D/E figures
individually reported, pattern consistent across all three]`: **DLF** net debt −₹650cr (Sep 2025,
i.e. net CASH), D/E 0.0 (FY25) vs 0.1 (FY24) — essentially debt-free; **Oberoi Realty** D/E ~0.18,
explicitly characterized as the most conservatively leveraged of its peer set; **Godrej Properties**
net debt fell 49% to ₹3,848cr in a single quarter (Dec-2024), D/E improving from 0.65 to 0.23
FY24→FY25. The pattern across all three large-cap names independently is the same direction and
similar magnitude — genuinely different from a China-style Three-Red-Lines-breaching cohort.
**Explicit caveat, stated rather than glossed over**: this dossier sourced only the large-cap
LISTED cohort; no comparable Tier-2/3 or unlisted-developer leverage data was found this session,
and that is precisely the segment where a hidden-leverage problem — if one exists — would be
least visible to public-market sourcing. The desk should not read "listed developers are
delevered" as "the Indian developer sector overall carries no leverage risk."

## Q8. Indian residential rental yields by city, and comparison with Chinese tier-1 yields

**India** `[1-SOURCE for the aggregate figure, multiple broadly-consistent city-level sources]`:
national average gross rental yield ~5.09-5.16% (Q4 2025/Q2 2026 on one tracker); but this
national figure sits ABOVE every individual major-metro figure found, implying it is either
weighted toward smaller/cheaper cities or uses a different (asking-price vs transacted) basis than
the city-level cuts — flagged as an internal inconsistency in the sourcing rather than resolved.
City-level: **Mumbai ~2.5-3%** (lowest, due to high absolute prices — premium corridors); most
sources converge that Mumbai anchors the LOW end nationally; **Bengaluru ~3-3.6%**; **Hyderabad
~3-4%**; **Gurgaon/Noida corridors ~3-4.5%** (with GURGAON specifically quoted as low as 2.5%
depending on segment); **Delhi and Kolkata ~5.8%** on one tracker, described as the HIGH end among
large cities (cheaper absolute prices relative to rent, on this reading). General range across
major metros: ~2-4.5% gross, i.e. India's rental-yield spread is itself wide (nearly 2x between
Mumbai's low and Delhi/Kolkata's high) — a first-order fact for any India property-yield design.

**China tier-1, cited from this programme's own sibling dossier** (`research/notes/china-dossiers/
c-yield.md`, itself `[2-SOURCE]`-tagged at the time it was written): China's tier-1 residential
gross yield is now ~2.0-2.6%, up mechanically from ~1.7-1.8% in 2021 — the rise reflects PRICE
falling faster than RENT, not genuine yield improvement, and rents themselves have been falling
in nominal terms since 2024 (national 50-city rent index down cumulative 1.37% H1-2025, down 3.04%
Jan-Nov 2025). China's tier-1 yields (~2-3%) were separately found to sit at the bottom of a global
comparator set that explicitly named **Mumbai (~5%)** as a high outlier `[2-SOURCE, cross-city
ranking, cited in c-yield.md]` — worth flagging that this external comparator's ~5% Mumbai figure
does NOT match this dossier's own fresh 2025-2026 search finding of Mumbai at the LOW end
(~2.5-3%) of the Indian city set. **This is a genuine, unresolved discrepancy** between two
independently-sourced readings of the same city and is reported as such rather than silently
reconciled — plausibly a gross-vs-net, asking-vs-transacted, or older-vintage-vs-current-vintage
difference, but no source found this session explains the gap directly.

**The honest comparison, net of that discrepancy**: on EVERY sourcing found, India's rental yields
(~2.5-5.8% across cities) sit at or above China's tier-1 range (~2-3%) — India is not a cheaper-
yield market than crash-era China on a like-for-like basis; if anything the opposite. This matters
for the "is Indian residential property similarly overpriced relative to its own income stream"
question the China comparison implicitly raises: the yield evidence argues India is NOT compressed
to China's degree, though the Mumbai-figure discrepancy above means this conclusion should be held
with moderate rather than high confidence.

## Q9. Indian REITs — see the table above; qualitative detail not captured in the table

Embassy REIT (2019) was India's first listed REIT and remains the largest by area (51 msf);
Mindspace (2020) and Brookfield (2021) followed as office-only vehicles; Nexus Select Trust (2023)
is explicitly documented as "India's first retail REIT" `[2-SOURCE]`, meaning through 2019-2023
India's REIT market was OFFICE-ONLY — retail came three-to-four years after the format's launch,
and residential has still never arrived. One cross-REIT comparison source frames total annualized
returns since inception across the FOUR listed REITs at 11-29%, with Nexus (29.3%) and Mindspace
(16.1%) at the ends of that range `[1-SOURCE]` — a wide spread for what are nominally similar
yield-instrument structures, suggesting vintage/entry-price and asset-class-mix effects dominate
over any common "REIT" beta. Loan-to-value discipline appears conservative where checked (Mindspace
~24.3% LTV) `[1-SOURCE]`, consistent with SEBI's REIT leverage caps rather than anything approaching
Chinese-developer-style leverage. **Gap, stated plainly**: NAV and price-to-NAV data (whether the
listed REITs trade at a premium or discount to their appraised property values) was not found for
any of the four this session — a natural, cheap follow-up search if the desk wants to actually size
a REIT allocation rather than just note the instrument category exists.

## Q10. Does India have China's specific vulnerabilities? Argued mechanism by mechanism

This question is answered mechanism-by-mechanism in the VULNERABILITY COMPARISON TABLE above; this
section adds the argument RESISTING both easy answers, as instructed.

**The "India is next" case, stated at its strongest, then rebutted point by point**: India DOES
have (i) a genuinely fast run-up in urban property prices in NCR/Bengaluru/Hyderabad specifically,
now described by Knight Frank itself as "near-peak... impacting affordability" (Q6) — a real,
current, dateable warning sign, not a straw man; (ii) an LGFV-STRUCTURED financing mechanism (SPVs/
municipal bonds) that is opaque and understudied on the Indian side (Q7/table) — the absence of a
quantified Indian LGFV-equivalent debt figure is itself a gap, not evidence of safety; (iii) a
totally unresearched Tier-2/3 unlisted-developer leverage picture (Q7) — precisely where hidden risk
would sit if it existed, and this dossier could not rule it out, only note it wasn't found; (iv) a
history of at least one severe property-linked funding freeze already (IL&FS, 2018-2020) proving
the Indian system is NOT immune to a China-style credit-freeze transmission mechanism in kind, only
smaller in the one episode observed to date.

**The "India is totally different" case, stated at its strongest, then also stress-tested**: RERA's
70%-escrow, construction-linked-release, 10%-pre-registration-cap regime (Q10/table) is not a
softer version of anything China had pre-2021 — it is closer to what China is only now, under
crisis pressure, retrofitting (supervised escrow, completed-home-sale share rising from 10.4% to
~33% by 2025). This is a genuine, dateable, POLICY-DESIGN fact, not an assumption: India built the
ring-fence BEFORE a crisis, China is building it DURING one. Property's GDP share (narrow ~7-8% vs
China's narrow ~22-23%) is roughly a THIRD of China's on the most comparable methodology found —
even granting every measurement uncertainty named in the table, the gap is too large to argue away
as a methodology artifact. Local-government fiscal dependence on land sales, the single mechanism
that most directly explains WHY China's local governments kept approving land auctions long after
demand justified it, has no real Indian analogue at all — Indian state/municipal finance was never
built around monetizing land-use rights as a primary revenue stream.

**The honest synthesis**: the mechanisms that ACTUALLY broke China — the escrow/presale gap, the
land-sale fiscal dependency, the sheer GDP-share concentration — do NOT transfer to India in any
meaningful degree, and two of the three (escrow, GDP share) are large enough gaps that this is not
a close call. The mechanisms that COULD transfer in principle — LGFV-style off-budget leverage,
unlisted-developer balance-sheet risk — are real category matches but are UNQUANTIFIED on the
Indian side by this dossier's own sourcing, which means the honest answer is "not found to be a
problem" rather than "confirmed to be safe." Recent Indian price appreciation in specific cities
(NCR/Bengaluru/Hyderabad) is a genuine, city-level affordability concern worth a future design, but
it is a LOCAL-OVERHEATING risk, not a SYSTEM-ARCHITECTURE risk of the kind that produced China's
crash — conflating the two would be exactly the "India is next" overreach the task warns against,
just as declaring the whole comparison irrelevant would be the opposite, equally lazy error.
