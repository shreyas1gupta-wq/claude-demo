## Headline findings

- **The single most severe, best-documented collapse in this entire dossier is POLICY-driven,
  not cycle-driven**: Beijing's 商住房 (commercial-titled "quasi-residential") apartments, after
  the March 2017 "3·26" ban on individual buyers and mortgage lending, saw transaction volume
  fall from 89,690 units (Aug 2015–Mar 2017, 20 months) to 5,409 units (Apr 2017–Nov 2018, 20
  months) — a **-94% volume collapse** — with a flagship complex (Beijing SOHO/"北京像素") quoted
  price falling from ~¥70,000/sqm to ~¥40,000/sqm (**-~43%**), and a later (2020) wave of project
  liquidations discounting a further **>40%** off remaining unsold stock. `[2-SOURCE]` (focus.cn
  + sina.com.cn/blog independently report the same order of magnitude on volume and price).
- **Strata-titled community/street shops (社区商铺/街铺) sold to retail investors are the worst-
  performing MARKET-cycle (non-policy) asset class found**: peak prices around ¥50,000/sqm now
  quoted at ¥20,000–30,000/sqm, a **-40% to -60%** decline, with investors described as routinely
  unable to exit. `[1-SOURCE]` — a single jiemian.com-sourced cluster; not independently
  corroborated by a second outlet, flagged for further pull.
- **Ultra-prime/luxury housing has NOT fallen — it has risen, and the divergence is widening**:
  Shanghai core-area (Xuhui Binjiang) resale luxury prices +20% YoY in 2025, with a flagship
  project (滨江凯旋门) up >25% over four years (¥138,000→¥172,800/sqm); Shenzhen set fresh price
  records in late 2025 (CITIC Sino Bay ¥380,000/sqm), even as Shenzhen's CITYWIDE new-home price
  fell 25–30% YTD over the same period. `[2-SOURCE]` for the direction (multiple independent
  Chinese-language outlets + Caixin/SCMP English coverage); the exact magnitude varies by source.
- **COMPOSITION-EFFECT WARNING, flagged per the desk's standing caution**: at least two of the
  "resilience" findings here are partly compositional, not purely price-level facts. (1) Shanghai
  "old apartments outperforming new" is concentrated in CORE districts slated for demolition/
  redevelopment (老破小 in Xuhui trading near luxury-tower prices) — this is a redevelopment-
  optionality premium on a specific old-stock subset, not old stock in general, which elsewhere
  (lower-tier cities, non-core districts) is discounted the normal 20–30%+ for age and location.
  (2) The "improvement-housing outperforming first-home" read (below) compares NATIONAL averages
  of two different community mixes and could partly reflect which cities/districts each label is
  concentrated in, not a pure preference shift — flagged, not resolved, with the data available.
- **Malls and high streets have NOT moved in lockstep, and which is "worse" flips by quarter/
  source**: Q1 2025 CBRE-family data had shopping-centre vacancy (16.8%) running WORSE than high-
  street vacancy (13.7%); by Q2 2025 the reported high-street vacancy (9.7%) had risen close to
  the mid/high-end retail average (9.5%). Rents on 100 major commercial streets fell to their
  lowest level since H2 2018 by H2 2025. Retail cap rates are EXPANDING (yields rising, prices
  falling) in China even as cap rates compress elsewhere in Asia-Pacific — a genuine outlier.
  `[2-SOURCE]` on the direction of both vacancy and cap-rate movement.
- **The retail REIT market gives the cleanest institutional mark on true retail value**: China
  Merchants Commercial REIT (mall-anchored) traded at a **-57.9% discount to NAV** as of 2025-06-
  30, with interim rental income down -17.9% YoY and revenue down -15.5% YoY — a rare
  institutionally-priced (not strata-titled) confirmation that the retail repricing is real and
  large, not just a strata/retail-investor phenomenon. `[2-SOURCE]` (PR Newswire + Stockopedia/
  iTiger both report the NAV discount and the income figures independently).

## Q1. Villas / low-density housing (别墅, 洋房) vs high-rise apartments

Evidence is thinner here than for any other question in this dossier — the desk should treat
this as a genuine gap, not a settled "villas win" story.

- **Supply is deliberately scarce and getting scarcer**: Beijing approved only 12 new villa
  projects in 2024, a policy-driven land-scarcity constraint (villas use far more land per unit
  than high-rise) that structurally supports villa land/unit values regardless of the demand
  cycle. `[1-SOURCE]` (mordorintelligence aggregation).
- **Transaction-value share**: apartments/condos were ~85.9% of 2025 transaction VALUE nationally,
  villas/landed housing ~14.1%, but the villa segment is forecast to grow slightly faster (1.36%
  CAGR to 2031) than the market overall — consistent with a "small, resilient niche" story rather
  than a mass-market comparison. `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **The one directly relevant Chinese-language finding**: for **first-residence-type villas**
  (第一居所型别墅, i.e. villas bought as a primary home rather than a second/investment home),
  one industry blog (我爱我家集团/Sina) reports **"量领跌、价领涨"** — volume leads the market DOWN
  (fell fastest) while PRICE leads the market UP (held up or rose fastest). This is a genuinely
  interesting and counter-intuitive shape — buyers who remain are paying up for scarce good
  stock even as overall deal counts collapse — but it comes from a single blog source of
  uncertain vintage and was not corroborated by a second outlet. `[1-SOURCE — unverified date]`.
- **What is NOT found**: no direct, dated, magnitude-quantified comparison of "villa price index"
  vs "apartment price index" over the same window from an NBS-adjacent or major agency (CBRE/
  Savills/JLL/Cushman) source. Several report titles (gelonghui, chyxx, chinabgao industry
  reports) look like they would contain exactly this, but only table-of-contents-level search
  snippets were retrievable — a direct WebFetch-based extraction is the fix, currently blocked.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: villas appear to hold value AT LEAST as well as apartments, likely
better, on the thin evidence available — driven by supply scarcity and a "flight to scarce
stock" pattern — but this is `[1-SOURCE]` throughout and should not be treated as desk-grade
until a dedicated principal-machine or WebFetch-enabled pull corroborates the price-index
comparison directly.

## Q2. "Improvement housing" (改善型) vs first-home (刚需): which fell harder?

- **First-home fell harder / improvement held up better, as of the most recent read (Dec 2024)**:
  52.0% of "improvement"-type communities saw month-on-month price INCREASES in Dec 2024 (up 4.9
  points from Nov), vs 45.0% of "first-home"-type communities (down 3.7 points from Nov) — i.e.
  the improvement segment was not just doing better in level, it was ACCELERATING while the
  first-home segment was decelerating in the same month. `[1-SOURCE]` (199it.com aggregation,
  likely sourcing a China Index Academy or Beike-style community-level survey; source methodology
  for the underlying survey was not independently re-verified).
- **Consistent evidence from bargaining room**: December 2024 average price-negotiation space
  (议价空间) was LOWEST for improvement-type communities at 16.5% (i.e., sellers conceded least),
  vs 20% for first-home and 17% for high-end — the improvement segment needed the smallest
  discount to transact, a second, independent metric pointing the same direction as the MoM-price
  share figure above (both from the same underlying report, so this is corroboration WITHIN one
  source, not a second independent source — flagged accordingly). `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **This is the opposite of the naive prior**: one might expect "upgrade" buyers (discretionary,
  more exposed to wealth-effect and confidence) to pull back harder than "essential need" buyers
  in a downturn. The data says the reverse, at least by this measure and this month. A plausible
  mechanism (not confirmed by any source found): improvement-type communities in China are
  disproportionately NEWER, better-located, and better-amenitized than first-home stock (which
  skews older/smaller/more peripheral) — meaning this split may be partly re-capturing the
  age/location effect from Q4 below rather than a pure preference-type effect. **Flagged as a
  possible confound, not resolved.**

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: first-home (刚需) fell harder / recovered more slowly than
improvement (改善型) on the one comparable survey found (Dec 2024 snapshot), `[1-SOURCE]`,
with a plausible but unconfirmed confound (improvement stock is systematically newer/better
located).

## Q3. Luxury / ultra-prime segment: has it fallen at all? Named projects and figures

**No — the ultra-prime segment in China's two strongest cities has RISEN through the bust,
and the gap to the mass market has widened, not narrowed.**

- **Shanghai**: core-area (Xuhui Binjiang) resale luxury home prices were up **~20% YoY** in
  2025 per one Chinese-language market report (huxiu.com), and a flagship project, **滨江凯旋门
  (Riverside Arc de Triomphe)**, rose from ¥138,000/sqm (2020) to ¥172,800/sqm (2024) — **>25%
  over 4 years**. A separate, less-corroborated figure circulating (capstone72.com, appearing
  twice in search but from what looks like the same underlying source) claims Shanghai luxury
  +30% / suburban -40% — directionally consistent with the Chinese-language figures above but
  the exact magnitude is `[1-SOURCE]` and should be read as an upper-bound/illustrative claim
  rather than a confirmed print. Meanwhile, more than 60% of listings in Shanghai's outer-ring
  suburbs had been discounted 3+ times with no buyer, and some outer-ring new projects are
  selling 15%+ below their filed reference price. `[2-SOURCE]` on the CORE-vs-PERIPHERY
  divergence direction (huxiu.com + sina.com.cn/finance independently report it); magnitudes
  differ by source (20% vs 30%, and -20% vs -40% on the down leg) — **use the direction, treat
  the exact numbers as a range, not a point estimate.**
- **Shenzhen**: named luxury projects with 2025 figures — **China Merchants Shekou's Houhai
  Merchants Legend** sold out 40 units (195-432 sqm, ¥24m-¥68m+ each) by end-Dec 2025; **CITIC's
  Sino Bay (中信新悦湾)** sold over ¥10 billion in two hours, with prices up to **¥380,000/sqm**
  — a fresh record. In the first four months of 2026, Shenzhen recorded 162 transactions above
  ¥50 million (18x all of 2025's volume for that price band), at an average ¥205,000/sqm,
  reportedly SURPASSING Beijing/Shanghai/Guangzhou in that ultra-luxury band. `[2-SOURCE]`
  (Caixin + multiple Chinese-language outlets on Sino Bay/Houhai Merchants Legend).
- **The mass-market contrast, same city, same period**: Shenzhen's CITYWIDE new-home price fell
  **25-30% year-to-date** over the same 2025 window the luxury records were being set, with most
  ordinary projects needing discounts of up to 25% to sell. `[2-SOURCE]`. This is as clean a
  bifurcation as exists anywhere in this dossier: one city, one year, luxury +record / mass -25-30%.

**Composition-effect note**: none of the "average price is rising" claims above are NBS or
CREIS-style broad indices — they are luxury-segment-specific reports, so this is NOT the same
composition trap as the "average land price rose because mix shifted to tier-1" precedent. It is
a genuine, segment-level bifurcation, not an artifact of averaging. Still, the Shanghai "core
+20-30%" figures are themselves an average of core-district transactions, which could shift
composition WITHIN the core (larger units, more redevelopment-driven old-stock trades — see Q4)
— flagged, not fully resolved.

## Q4. OLD vs NEW apartments — the age-depreciation problem and second-hand prices

- **The general premium/discount**: in China's largest cities, new-build homes command roughly
  **15-30% more** than comparable resale homes in the same district; in weaker interior/smaller
  cities the resale discount for older, less desirable stock can run **20-30%+ or more**.
  `[1-SOURCE]`, though directionally this matches the well-known "developer price floor rigidity
  vs individual seller flexibility" mechanism (developers protect collateral values on unsold
  new stock; individual owners cut price to clear) cited independently.
- **International reference point for the underlying depreciation curve** (not China-specific,
  used here only as a structural prior): building structure value can lose as much as 50% in the
  first 10 years post-construction, with depreciation continuing on a decaying curve out to
  roughly 30 years. `[1-SOURCE]`, NOT a China-specific measurement — flagged as a general-pattern
  reference, not a China print.
- **COMPOSITION-EFFECT FLAG — the Shanghai counter-example**: in Xuhui district, a 35-sqm "老破小"
  (old/run-down/small) unit was listed at ¥113,000/sqm, essentially matching the ¥110,000/sqm
  transaction price of a NEW luxury tower (尚海湾) directly across the street — an inverted
  price relationship (old > or ≈ new) driven entirely by demolition/redevelopment speculation in
  a specific hyper-core location. `[1-SOURCE]` (huxiu.com). This is exactly the kind of
  composition trap the desk has been warned about: it does NOT mean "old apartments hold value" —
  it means a narrow subset of old apartments in specific redevelopment-eligible core locations
  trade on a completely different (option-value) basis than old apartments generally, which
  elsewhere face the normal double discount of age AND (often) peripheral location.
- **Aggregate context**: the 100-city secondhand price index fell -13% June 2023 → June 2025
  nationally (mix of all ages/locations); by end-2024, listing-price index cumulative declines
  were tier-graded — tier-1 -15.2%, tier-2 -22.7%, tier-3 -24.4%, tier-4 -18.3% — with tier-2
  the WORST of the four, a genuine surprise worth flagging (not monotone with tier rank).
  `[1-SOURCE]` (a China Index Academy-style aggregation via a Chinese portal), consistent in
  direction with the b1-t1-overview dossier's independently-sourced NBS tier gap.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: the textbook age-depreciation effect (15-30%+ new-build premium)
holds as a general rule, but it is NOT universal — a specific and economically important
exception exists in hyper-core, redevelopment-eligible old stock (Shanghai Xuhui example), where
the age discount inverts into a redevelopment-option premium. Treat "old apartments are cheap" as
true almost everywhere except this narrow, genuinely valuable exception.

## Q5. 类住宅 / 商住房 (commercial-titled apartments): the 2017 Beijing ban and price collapse

This is the most severe, most specific, and best-corroborated collapse in the entire dossier —
a POLICY shock, not a market-cycle event, and should be read as a distinct causal category from
everything else here.

- **The policy**: Beijing's "3·26" measures (26 March 2017) banned individual (non-corporate)
  buyers from purchasing 商住房 ("quasi-residential"/commercial-titled apartments, built on
  commercially-zoned land but marketed and lived in as apartments) and banned bank mortgage
  lending for such purchases; a minimum 500-sqm floor-area-per-unit rule was also imposed,
  effectively killing the small-unit product entirely. `[2-SOURCE]` (mingtiandi + caixinglobal +
  contemporaneous Borneo Post/pressreader wire coverage, all describing the same March 2017
  measures).
- **Volume collapse**: in the 20 months before the ban (Aug 2015-Mar 2017), Beijing 商住房
  transaction volume was **89,690 units**; in the ~20 months after (Apr 2017-Nov 2018), it was
  **5,409 units** — a **-94% collapse**, `[2-SOURCE]` (house.focus.cn's "317新政一周年" retrospective
  + blog.sina.com.cn independently citing the same order-of-magnitude collapse). New SUPPLY
  collapsed even further: two years pre-ban saw 83,023 new units added and 94,551 sold; two years
  post-ban saw only **1,966 new units** added.
  `[1-SOURCE]` for the supply-side figure specifically (sina.com.cn), consistent in direction
  with the volume collapse above.
- **Price collapse**: the flagship complex 北京像素 ("Beijing SOHO/Pixel"), a well-known duplex-
  unit 商住房 development, saw quoted per-sqm prices fall from **~¥70,000/sqm pre-policy to
  ~¥40,000/sqm** — roughly **-43%** — and total transaction prices for individual duplex units
  fall from a peak above ¥3 million to around ¥1.9 million (a drop of "100万+ yuan", ~40%).
  `[1-SOURCE]` (sina.com.cn/blog specifically for the per-unit total-price figure; the per-sqm
  figure from the same article cluster) — flagged as needing a second independent source at
  this specific magnitude, though the direction and rough scale (~40%) is corroborated by the
  separate "beyond 40% off" liquidation report below.
- **A second, later wave**: in 2020, distressed 商住房 PROJECTS (not just secondary listings)
  sought to liquidate remaining unsold inventory at discounts **exceeding 40%**, per Sina Finance
  reporting — a second, independent confirmation that the ~40% order of magnitude is real and
  persisted (rather than being a one-time 2017-18 shock that then stabilized). `[2-SOURCE]`
  jointly with the Pixel-complex figure above (same rough magnitude, different point in time,
  different specific project/mechanism).
- **The human cost, cited but not itself a price figure**: an estimated 17 million sqm of
  existing 商住房 stock (≈170,000 people) was affected; owners who had already moved in were
  banned from reselling, and some buyers who had purchased pre-completion were left unable to
  obtain a property ownership certificate at all — an illiquidity/legal-limbo dimension that a
  pure price-decline figure understates. `[1-SOURCE]` (mingtiandi).

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: a genuinely severe, well-corroborated, POLICY-caused (not
cycle-caused) collapse: **-94% transaction volume, roughly -40% to -43% price** on the
best-documented flagship project, with a second liquidation wave in 2020 confirming the
discount persisted at a similar (>40%) magnitude. This is the single most extreme number in
this dossier and, unlike almost everything else here, its cause (an abrupt, near-total demand-
side ban) is not really comparable to a normal price-cycle bust — it is closer to an asset class
being regulated out of existence for individual buyers.

## Q6. High-street retail rent decline: Wangfujing, Nanjing Rd/Huaihai Rd, Shenzhen/Guangzhou

- **National context (the baseline against which each street sits)**: average rents across 100
  major commercial streets in 15 key mainland cities fell to **¥24/sqm/day in H2 2025** — the
  lowest level since H2 2018 — with the pace of decline ACCELERATING through 2025 (full-year 2025
  down 0.8%, a steeper fall than 2024). Prime ground-floor rents across 21 cities fell 2.8% in
  H1 2025 alone. `[2-SOURCE]` (Cushman & Wakefield-family reporting + Savills/JLL corroborating
  the direction and rough scale).
- **Beijing / Wangfujing**: Beijing citywide retail rents fell **-3% qoq / -10.4% YoY in Q1
  2026**, with vacancy roughly stable (7% urban-wide, 3% core areas). No Wangfujing-specific rent
  FIGURE was retrievable (only tenant-activity anecdotes — Unitree Robotics' 2026 store opening
  at Wangfujing Yintai in88) — the street-specific number remains a genuine gap. `[1-SOURCE]`
  for the citywide figure (Caixin); Wangfujing itself `[RECALL — unverified]` beyond the
  anecdote.
- **Shanghai / Nanjing Rd & Huaihai Rd**: no street-specific rent-decline figure was retrievable
  for either named street; only confirmation that both sit within the core Huangpu/Xuhui/Jing'an
  districts referenced generically in market reports, and that Shanghai's broader retail rental
  index fell from >¥800/sqm/month (2020) to **¥713.01/sqm/month in Q4 2025** (roughly **-11%**
  cumulative over the period, though this is a citywide/shopping-centre-context figure, not
  street-specific). `[1-SOURCE]`. A genuine gap: street-level Nanjing Rd/Huaihai Rd figures need
  a dedicated pull (ideally via a source that permits WebFetch, or a principal-machine Savills/
  CBRE PDF pull).
- **Guangzhou**: citywide prime retail rent fell **-2.4% to -3.1% qoq** across several 2024-25
  quarters (Cushman & Wakefield MarketBeat), and full-year 2024 -2.3% (Savills). A separately-
  seen claim of Guangzhou "-15%+" or "steepest first-tier fall" from an earlier search summary
  could NOT be re-confirmed at that magnitude in follow-up searches — **this number is
  downgraded to `[1-SOURCE], not reconfirmed`, and should not be treated as established.** The
  DIRECTION (Guangzhou among the weaker first-tier retail markets) is `[2-SOURCE]`.
- **Shenzhen**: no rent-decline figure specific to a named high-street district (e.g. Dongmen)
  was retrievable — only generic tourism/history content on Dongmen Pedestrian Street. A genuine
  gap.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: the multi-year, multi-city DIRECTION (high-street rents falling to
their lowest level since 2018, accelerating through 2025) is well corroborated `[2-SOURCE]`, but
STREET-SPECIFIC figures for the named streets (Wangfujing, Nanjing Rd, Huaihai Rd, a named
Shenzhen/Guangzhou strip) were largely NOT retrievable via WebSearch snippets — this section is
the thinnest in the dossier on the exact question asked, and is named explicitly as a gap below.

## Q7. Shopping mall rents/vacancy vs high street — which held up better; e-commerce interaction

- **The comparison flips by quarter and by data house — do not read this as one clean story**:
  - Q1 2025 (Savills-family read): shopping-centre vacancy **16.8%**, high-street vacancy
    **13.7%** — high street clearly BETTER (lower vacancy) that quarter.
  - Q2 2025 (a different aggregation): average high-street vacancy risen to **9.7%**, "mid-to-
    high-end retail" overall vacancy **9.5%** — high street now roughly IN LINE with, if
    marginally worse than, the broader (largely mall-inclusive) average.
  - Separately, national prime-retail vacancy across 15 major cities was reported at **11.1%**
    in Q2 2025 (a third, still-different aggregation/definition).
  These three reads are NOT reconcilable into one number with the sources at hand — different
  city samples, different asset definitions (prime vs all, shopping-centre vs mall vs mixed) are
  in play. `[2-SOURCE]` only on the qualitative point that BOTH formats carry meaningfully
  elevated (high-single/low-double-digit) vacancy through 2025, not on any single vacancy level.
- **Rents**: shopping-centre rents were comparatively STABLE to slightly down (Shanghai
  shopping-centre rent ~-0.2% qoq readings in 2025; Q2 2025 Chinese mall vacancy edged up only
  +0.3pp to 7.4% with rents -0.2% qoq) — i.e., MALLS may be managing occupancy better via active
  re-tenanting even as high-street rents fell more sharply in percentage terms nationally
  (the -0.8% 2025 full-year high-street figure vs mall's more modest quarterly moves). `[1-SOURCE]`
  each, not cross-corroborated against each other in the same report.
- **The e-commerce interaction, structurally**: tenant demand is shifting from high-rent to
  low-rent categories (structural rent decline pressure on malls), new supply in 21 major cities
  fell 27.5% YoY in H1 2025 (developers pulling back), and — the clearest sign of the format war —
  **luxury brands (Dior, Louis Vuitton, Tiffany) are moving OUT of enclosed malls and INTO
  standalone street-facing flagships**, explicitly to replicate Ginza-style prestige retail.
  `[1-SOURCE]` (SCMP), a qualitative but striking data point: at the very top of the market,
  street-facing formats are winning share back from malls, even as malls broadly out-manage
  street retail on vacancy in some quarters. E-commerce penetration itself sits at **24.9%** of
  retail sales — meaningful but not dominant, i.e. the mall/street rent story is not purely an
  e-commerce-substitution story; oversupply (local governments mandating attached commercial
  space in every residential land parcel) is repeatedly cited as the deeper structural driver.
  `[1-SOURCE]` each.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: no clean, single "malls beat high street" or "high street beats
malls" verdict is supportable from the sources found — the comparison genuinely flips by quarter
and definition. The one robust finding is a BIFURCATION AT THE TOP: prestige/luxury retail is
migrating from malls back to street-facing flagships, even as both formats broadly carry
elevated, roughly comparable vacancy in the mass market.

## Q8. Strata-titled SHOPS (商铺) sold to retail investors — the price collapse

- **The core figure**: community/street-level strata shops (社区商铺/街铺), once trading at peaks
  around **¥50,000/sqm**, are now typically quoted at **¥20,000-30,000/sqm** — a **-40% to -60%**
  decline from peak. `[1-SOURCE]` (jiemian.com), NOT independently re-confirmed by a second
  outlet at this specific magnitude in this session's searches — **this is the single most
  important figure the principal asked for in this dossier, and it is currently only
  single-sourced; flagged as the top priority for a follow-up, dedicated pull.**
- **Mechanism cited**: value erosion is linked to population/wealth migration out of a shop's
  catchment (a named pattern of affluent residents relocating and community-shop catchments
  hollowing out over subsequent years), plus a long multi-year "ramp-up" period (2-3 years
  minimum, up to 7-8 years) before a new community shop even reaches stabilized trade — meaning
  a shop bought at the wrong point in a neighbourhood's life cycle can be structurally
  underwater for years even before any citywide downturn. `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Corroborating (but not itself quantifying) evidence from the broader commercial-property
  data**: national commercial-real-estate sales area fell -17.7% YoY in the first 8 months of
  2024, and average street-shop (百街商铺) rents fell to ¥24.05/sqm/day in H2 2025, with the
  DECLINE RATE ITSELF widening versus H1 2025 — consistent with, but not a direct confirmation
  of, the -40-60% CAPITAL price-level collapse claimed above (rent decline of a few percent per
  half-year is a very different animal from a 40-60% capital-value collapse; the two can both be
  true if cap rates are simultaneously expanding sharply, which section Q9 below supports).
  `[2-SOURCE]` on the rent trend; the capital-value collapse remains `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Investor-outcome framing**: strata shops are explicitly described in the sourced material as
  "high-risk investment products" with investors routinely left holding unsold/untenanted units
  — language consistent with the "reportedly among the worst-hit categories of all" framing in
  the task brief, but again on `[1-SOURCE]` evidence.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: the data available supports the principal's framing — strata shops
sold to retail investors are a genuinely bad-performing category, plausibly the worst
MARKET-CYCLE (as opposed to policy-driven, see Q5) real-estate sub-class in this dossier — but
the headline **-40% to -60%** figure rests on one source cluster and should be treated as
directionally right, magnitude-uncertain, pending a second independent pull.

## Q9. Retail property cap rates / yields, now vs pre-crash

- **The direction is clear and somewhat counter-intuitive relative to the rest of Asia-Pacific**:
  Greater China cap rates are on an UPWARD trend (yields rising, capital values falling) even as
  cap rates are COMPRESSING in most other Asia-Pacific markets — a genuine regional outlier,
  consistent with China's still-unresolved property downturn versus a recovering regional cycle
  elsewhere. `[2-SOURCE]` (CBRE Asia-Pacific cap-rate survey language + a separate Chinese-
  language capitalization-rate survey both describing sellers' price expectations falling / cap
  rates rising).
- **Current levels (Sept 2024 reading, the most specific figure found)**: across major Chinese
  cities, retail-format cap rates run **5.2%-7.7%**, ranked from most-expensive (lowest cap rate)
  to cheapest: shopping centres > community commercial > outlets (i.e. outlets are priced
  cheapest/highest-yielding of the three retail sub-formats). `[1-SOURCE]` (a China REITs-forum-
  adjacent capitalization-rate survey, via 用益信托网/MBAChina aggregation) — a genuinely useful,
  fairly granular figure, though not independently cross-checked against a second house's number
  for the same date.
- **A related, adjacent data point (office, not retail, but same survey universe, useful as a
  cross-check on methodology)**: Beijing/Shanghai CORE Grade-A office cap rates sit at 4.2-4.8%,
  with NON-core projects in the same cities running 30-50bps HIGHER (cheaper) — confirming the
  broader pattern that "core" and "non-core" are being priced on visibly different cap-rate
  bases across the market, not just in residential (see Q3/Q4 core-vs-periphery bifurcation).
  `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **What could NOT be found**: a clean, single "2015 (or pre-2021 peak) cap rate X% -> 2024/25
  cap rate Y%" time-series for retail specifically. Several searches (English and Chinese) failed
  to surface a direct historical comparison; only the CURRENT level and the DIRECTION of change
  were retrievable. This is a named gap.
- **Private-market vs public-market yield split (an important, distinct data point)**: private-
  placement C-REITs are pricing new retail deals at **5-6% yields**, vs listed/public C-REIT
  retail assets around **3-4%** forecast distribution yields (e.g. CapitaLand's Shanghai retail
  C-REIT: 4.40% FY2025 / 4.53% FY2026 forecast) — i.e. the PUBLIC market is still pricing retail
  more richly (lower yield) than private negotiated deals, even as secondary trading of existing
  listed retail REITs shows steep NAV discounts (see Q10) — an apparent tension between
  primary-listing pricing and secondary-market pricing that is itself worth flagging. `[2-SOURCE]`.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: retail cap rates are expanding (5.2-7.7% current range, direction
clearly upward `[2-SOURCE]`), but a clean pre-crash-vs-now numerical comparison could not be
sourced — named as a gap, not filled with a guessed number.

## Q10. Named distressed mall sales / REIT-level retail valuations that reveal the actual mark

- **China Merchants Commercial REIT (HKG:1503)** — the cleanest institutional mark found:
  closing unit price at a **-57.9% discount to NAV per unit** as of 2025-06-30; interim rental
  income down **-17.9%** YoY, revenue down **-15.5%** YoY, over the same period. `[2-SOURCE]`
  (PRNewswire interim-results release + Stockopedia/iTiger independently reporting both the NAV
  discount and the income decline figures).
- **CapitaLand China Trust (CLCT)** — a Singapore-listed, China-retail-exposed REIT, also
  trading at a large discount to book/NAV, with FY2024 distribution-per-unit down **-16.2%** YoY
  even as the underlying MALL portfolio's OPERATING performance was described as "resilient" —
  i.e. the unit-price/NAV discount here is arguably more a capital-markets/sentiment/funding-cost
  story than a pure asset-cash-flow story, a genuinely useful nuance versus CMC REIT where BOTH
  the valuation mark AND the operating cash flow (rental income) deteriorated together. `[1-SOURCE]`.
- **Shanghai's Temple Square Mall** — a named distressed-asset sale: once sought by foreign funds
  including CarVal, the complex was abandoned since 2017 after its owner's debt crisis, and sold
  BELOW VALUATION at auction in February 2025 — no specific discount percentage was retrievable,
  but the qualitative fact (a foreign-fund-target asset, abandoned 8 years, finally sold below
  its own appraised valuation) is itself informative about the depth of the retail-asset
  repricing. `[1-SOURCE]` (SCMP).
- **The broader distressed-auction context** (residential, cited here only as a cross-check on
  method, not itself a retail figure): nationwide auctioned homes sold at ~73% of appraised value
  on average in 2026 (a -27% discount) — useful as a general "how much does China's judicial/
  distressed-sale channel discount appraised value" benchmark against which the mall-specific
  figures above can be read. `[1-SOURCE]`.

**Answer as of 2026-09-11**: the REIT-level mark is unambiguous and reasonably well corroborated
— CMC REIT's -57.9% NAV discount alongside a -17.9% rental-income decline is about as clean a
"the market believes China retail property is worth roughly half its book value, and the cash
flow backing that book value is also falling" statement as exists in any of this dossier's
sources.
