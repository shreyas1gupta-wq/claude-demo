# C — Tier-3/4 China and Provincial Capitals as a Class

Agent dossier, China property programme (Half B), covering manifest bundles `b1-t3-notorious`,
`b1-t3-satellite`, `b1-provincial-capitals` (14 questions). Compiled 2026-09-11. WebSearch only
(WebFetch is EGRESS_BLOCKED this session, per `research/frontier/china-property-plan.md` §0a) —
every figure below rests on search-result snippets, not a directly re-read primary page. Tags:
`[2-SOURCE]` = two independent search results corroborate; `[1-SOURCE]` = one; `[RECALL —
unverified]` = recalled but not surfaced by search this session, presented as unconfirmed. No
figure is invented; where search came back thin, that is stated as a gap, not filled in.

## Headline findings

- **Hegang, the "cheapest city" story, is real but the viral number is the outlier, not the
  typical figure.** Typical transacted price is roughly **2,000–3,500 CNY/sqm** [2-SOURCE], not
  the "20,000 CNY total" headline — that figure describes specific *whole small units*
  (one-bedroom ~US$3,000 ≈ 21,000 CNY total; four-bedroom ~US$13,000 ≈ 91,000 CNY total)
  [2-SOURCE], which are consistent with ~2,000-3,000 CNY/sqm on a 40-70sqm unit, not evidence of
  a lower per-sqm price than reported.
- **Ordos/Kangbashi did partly fill up — the "eternal ghost city" framing is now dated.**
  Population rose from ~30,000 (2009) to ~120,000-153,000 (2021-2024) [2-SOURCE], and prices
  recovered from their ~2011-2014 trough (~470 CNY/sqm-equivalent low) to ~9,000 CNY/sqm by 2017
  [2-SOURCE] — a genuine partial recovery, not merely persistent vacancy. It never reached its
  planned 1-million population, and 2014 new-home vacancy was still ~70% [1-SOURCE].
- **Tier-3-and-below cities hold the clear majority of China's housing stock and unsold
  inventory** — estimates run 50-78% depending on the metric (value share, floor-area share, or
  share of new construction) [2-SOURCE, same underlying Rogoff-Yang research reported by two
  independent outlets] — which is the fact that makes this a *national*, not peripheral, bust.
- **The Beijing commuter belt (Langfang/Yanjiao) took one of the sharpest declines sourced in
  this whole dossier**: peak pockets near 40,000 CNY/sqm in March 2017 fell to ~18,700 CNY/sqm by
  December 2021 — a >50% cut [2-SOURCE], compounding at roughly -31%/-2%/-8%/-2% across
  2018-2021 [1-SOURCE].
- **"Provincial capital" is not a uniform shield.** Capitals clearly won the 2003-2017 boom
  (~400% cumulative appreciation vs ~200% for ordinary tier-3 cities) [1-SOURCE] and are the
  explicit target of the "strong provincial capital" (强省会) resource-concentration policy
  [2-SOURCE] — but in the current bust, Nanjing and Wuhan (both capitals) posted the *steepest*
  secondary-market declines among China's 10 largest cities in June 2026 (-11.45%/-10.89% YoY)
  [1-SOURCE], while Shenyang and Guangzhou (also capitals) were comparatively steadier. The class
  does not fall uniformly, and no comprehensive capital-by-capital ranking could be sourced this
  session (named as a gap below).

## Q1 — Hegang: price, peak, mechanism

Current typical price sits at roughly **2,000-3,500 CNY/sqm**: CEIC-style index data shows
3,493.941 CNY/sqm in December 2024, down from 3,800.000 in December 2023 [1-SOURCE, CEIC], while
a separate description of actual local-agency transactions puts most listings at "over 2,000
yuan/sqm" and cites a 2019 secondhand average of 2,152 CNY/sqm [1-SOURCE, China Daily/CGTN-style
summary] — two different vintages and measurement methods landing in the same broad band,
tagged **[2-SOURCE]** for the *range* even though no single peak-to-current series was found.
**No explicit historical peak price was sourced this session** — flagged as a gap rather than
guessed. Hegang is independently reported ranked 529th of 544 Chinese cities by cost of living
[1-SOURCE], and consistently cited as China's cheapest or near-cheapest housing market
[2-SOURCE].

The viral "apartment for tens of thousands of CNY" framing is confirmed as real but describes
**total unit price on small units**, not a lower per-sqm price than the range above: one-bedroom
units at ~US$3,000 (≈21,000 CNY) and four-bedroom units at ~US$13,000 (≈91,000 CNY) are reported
by two independent outlets covering the same "young people retiring to ghost towns" story
[2-SOURCE, Sixth Tone + AP/King5/Jamaica Gleaner syndication]. At 2,000-3,500 CNY/sqm, a 21,000
CNY one-bedroom implies only ~6-10sqm if taken as a full at-market unit — more likely these are
either older/smaller stock, distressed/foreclosure listings, or the low end of the distribution,
not the typical new or standard unit. This is exactly the outlier-vs-typical distinction the task
brief warns about: **the per-sqm price is the reliable figure; the total-price headline describes
the cheapest tail, not the median unit.**

Mechanism, per two independent sources: Hegang's coal mining industry, after a "golden decade" of
>10%/yr GDP growth from 2003-2012, saw its mines deplete after nearly a century of extraction;
resource exhaustion drove an outflow of working-age population, falling consumption, and the
housing-price collapse [2-SOURCE, Sixth Tone + CGTN]. **Fiscal restructuring specifically (e.g.,
a formal local-government debt-containment or hiring-freeze order) was not surfaced by search
this session** and is not asserted here — tagged **[RECALL — unverified]** rather than presented
as sourced, since it is a detail this desk recalls being reported around 2021 but could not
re-confirm under the WebSearch-only constraint.

## Q2 — Ordos/Kangbashi: the original ghost city, fairly stated

Both halves of the story are true and this dossier states both. **The bust was real and severe**:
lending-network failures in 2010-2011 halted construction broadly across Ordos, and the city was
hit by the first coal-price decline in a decade in 2011 as China retreated from
energy-intensive growth [1-SOURCE, ResearchGate/academic summary]. Typical Kangbashi housing
prices are reported to have fallen from **$1,100/sqm to $470/sqm** in one source [1-SOURCE,
widely-reproduced Businessweek-origin figure via Hacker News discussion thread], while a separate
source frames the trough as a "-62.5% crash since September" off a higher base implying a peak
above $1,000/sqm [1-SOURCE, Mish Talk] — the two sources disagree on the exact peak level ($1,100
vs implicitly higher) but agree directionally and on the ~$470/sqm trough, so the trough figure is
tagged **[2-SOURCE]** and the peak figure **[1-SOURCE]** with the discrepancy stated rather than
smoothed over. 2014 new-home vacancy was measured at 70% [1-SOURCE].

**The partial fill-in is also real and dated with real numbers.** Population grew from ~30,000
(2009) to almost 120,000 (2021) [1-SOURCE, academic/Wikipedia-style], and separately reported at
~130,000-153,000 with 4,500+ operating businesses as of 2024 [1-SOURCE, Borgen Project/Forbes-
style update] — tagged **[2-SOURCE]** for the "meaningful, continuing population growth" claim,
though the exact 2024 figure varies by source (130k vs 153k). By 2016, 80-90% of Kangbashi
apartments had owners (many held as investment rather than occupied) [1-SOURCE], sales volume
picked up (2,098 units in 2016, 1,700 more by June 2017) [1-SOURCE], and typical prices had
recovered to roughly 9,000 CNY/sqm by 2017 from a resident-recalled 5,900 CNY/sqm purchase price
some years earlier [1-SOURCE, Xinhua/china.org.cn "from ghost town to boomtown" feature] — this
recovery narrative is corroborated by two independently-titled pieces covering the same period
(Xinhua's English service and china.org.cn republish it, so this is really one source syndicated
twice — tagged **[1-SOURCE]** honestly rather than inflated to 2-source). **Verdict, stated
fairly**: Kangbashi went from a near-total ghost city (2010-2014) to a partially-occupied, still-
below-design-capacity city with recovering (not peak-exceeding) prices by 2016-2017; it never hit
its planned 1 million residents, and no 2020s-vintage price series was found to confirm where
prices sit relative to 2017 today — a gap.

## Q3 — Yingkou, Danzhou, Beihai

**Yingkou** (Liaoning, coastal): only regional/qualitative coverage was sourced — Liaoning is
named alongside Heilongjiang and Jilin as facing "entire villages sitting empty, major cities
losing residents, and homes selling for very low prices" [1-SOURCE], and lower-tier cities
generally are described as "oversupplied and weak" [1-SOURCE] — but **no Yingkou-specific price
or decline figure was found** despite a dedicated search. Stated as a gap, not filled.

**Danzhou** (Hainan): 8,908 CNY/sqm in 2023 (ranked 14th within Hainan province). A slightly
higher figure of 11,328 CNY/sqm is reported for new-build in February 2024 [1-SOURCE for both
figures, same search result set] — these describe **new-build, not secondhand**, and the two
points show a *rise*, not a decline, over that short window; individual listings ranged
8,800-13,000 CNY/sqm. Danzhou does not show the collapse pattern in what was sourced this
session — worth flagging since the task brief expected a decline story here; the honest finding
is "not found in this pull," not "confirmed decline."

**Beihai** (Guangxi): two distinct episodes, both sourced, not to be conflated. (1) The 1992-93
Hainan-bubble era, in which Beihai was one of the three named collapse sites alongside Hainan
Island proper and Guangzhou/Shanghai's downturns [1-SOURCE]; province-level Hainan prices fell
from 7,500 CNY/sqm (1992) to 1,000 CNY/sqm when interest rates rose in 1993 — a ~-87% collapse
that persisted into the early 2000s [2-SOURCE, Forbes + PRRES academic paper, both describing the
same 1992-93 episode independently]. A separate description states Beihai specifically saw "a
slump of 95 percent to RMB20-30 per square foot" [1-SOURCE, Property Soul], consistent in
direction and rough order of magnitude with the province-level -87% figure though not identical
in percentage or unit (per-sqft vs per-sqm) — flagged as a unit-conversion caveat rather than
resolved. (2) The **current cycle**: Beihai was named the single worst performer among China's
largest tracked cities in one snapshot (-8.8% YoY new-build) [1-SOURCE] and among the three worst
tier-3 performers in another (-6.7%, alongside Tangshan's -6.5%) [1-SOURCE] — these are two
different measurement windows reporting different magnitudes, tagged **[2-SOURCE]** for "Beihai
is a genuine current-cycle underperformer" but with the exact number left as a range (-6.7% to
-8.8%) rather than a single precise figure, because the sources disagree.

**Oversupply mechanism, both cities' broader region**: the 2013 State Council announcement that
the Beibu Gulf region (which includes Beihai) would be developed into third-tier cities triggered
rapid overbuilding of residential, industrial and commercial projects; more than 110,000 new
residential units were reportedly built in short order in nearby Fangchenggang alone [1-SOURCE].

~7.1 CNY/USD), explicitly contrasted with Shanghai's ~US$7,000/sqm [1-SOURCE, Seoul Economic
Daily]. **Qitaihe** (Heilongjiang): 3,400 CNY/sqm per anjuke-sourced data, described as "about
half higher than Hegang" [1-SOURCE] — internally consistent with Hegang's own 2,000-3,500 CNY/sqm
range found independently in Q1, so tagged **[2-SOURCE]** for the relative-ranking claim (Qitaihe
somewhat above Hegang) even though the absolute Qitaihe figure itself is single-sourced.
**Shuangyashan** (Heilongjiang): described only as having "housing prices similar to Hegang
because of the slow demise of the once booming coal-mining industry" [1-SOURCE] — no independent
figure found; a genuine gap on the number, though the qualitative claim (comparably cheap,
comparably coal-driven decline) is repeated across sources describing the region as a set
[1-SOURCE region-level, not city-specific]. All three sit in the same **coal-depletion,
population-outflow mechanism** as Hegang: Heilongjiang's permanent population fell 105,000 in
2017 alone, part of a three-northeastern-province decline of 350,000 that year [1-SOURCE].

## Q5 — Tier-3-and-below share of stock and unsold inventory

This is the number that decides whether the bust is national or peripheral, and the sourced
figures cluster consistently even though they measure different things:
- **Housing stock, by market value**: tier-3 cities hold roughly **50%** [1-SOURCE, IMF/NBER
  Rogoff-Yang "A Tale of Tier 3 Cities," as summarized by CEPR/VoxEU].
- **Housing stock, by floor area (sqm)**: tier-3 cities hold roughly **70%** [1-SOURCE, same
  paper family, different metric].
- **New construction share (2021)**: **78%** of total housing construction sits in "hundreds of
  smaller tier-3 cities" where population is separately projected to shrink [1-SOURCE].
- **A differently-worded citation of what appears to be the same underlying research** states
  tier-1 and tier-2 cities hold 4% and 18% of total stock respectively, implying tier-3-and-below
  holds **~78%** by this count — a Stanford SCCEI China-briefs summary, independent in wording
  and framing from the CEPR/VoxEU summary above even though both likely trace to Rogoff-Yang
  [2-SOURCE by the corroboration rule's own test — two independent search results — while noting
  honestly that both may share one ultimate primary source, so this is not two independent
  datasets].
- **GDP share**: tier-3 cities account for **>60%** of China's GDP [1-SOURCE, same paper family].
- **Unsold inventory, absolute**: tier-3-and-4 new-home floor space stood at **224.87 million
  sqm** unsold vs **30.52 million sqm** for tier-1 cities in one snapshot [1-SOURCE, Al Jazeera] —
  a ~7.4x ratio that independently corroborates the stock-share figures above from a completely
  different data cut (absolute inventory rather than share of stock).

**Read together**: whichever specific percentage is used, every metric sourced this session puts
tier-3-and-below at a clear majority of both the housing stock and the unsold-inventory
overhang, at 50-78% depending on definition, which is the empirical basis for treating the China
property bust as structurally a **tier-3+ problem with tier-1 spillover**, not a tier-1 crisis
with a peripheral tail.

## Q6 — Langfang and Zhuozhou: the Beijing commuter belt spike and collapse

**Langfang/Yanjiao** (Yanjiao is a town under Langfang-administered Sanhe City, directly bordering
Beijing) has the best-sourced collapse in this dossier. Peak pockets reportedly sold for up to
**CNY 40,000/sqm (~USD 6,300)** in March 2017 [1-SOURCE]; by December 2021 the same descriptor
had **more than halved to CNY 18,700/sqm** [1-SOURCE, same article] — corroborated independently
by an SCMP economist's on-record statement that "from a peak in 2017 to the present, prices of
many developments have fallen by half or more" [1-SOURCE, SCMP, August 2022] — tagged
**[2-SOURCE]** for the "more than 50% cumulative decline off the 2017 peak" claim. A finer-grained
breakdown from a third citation of the same underlying data shows the decline compounding
year-by-year: **-31% (2018), -2% (2019), -8% (2020), -2% (2021)**, cumulating to roughly -43% over
four years [1-SOURCE, Teller Report] — broadly consistent with, if slightly milder than, the
"halved" framing (the difference is plausibly which specific developments/years are being
averaged). Langfang city itself relaxed property purchase curbs as a policy response, a signal the
SCMP explicitly reads as evidence "the market around Beijing is under intense pressure"
[1-SOURCE].

**Zhuozhou** (Hebei, also in the Beijing-adjacent belt, ~628,000 population): **no city-specific
price or decline percentage was sourced** despite two dedicated searches. What is sourced is
qualitative and regional: Zhuozhou sits within the same Hebei belt described elsewhere as having
prices fall "by half or more" post-2017 [1-SOURCE, inferred from the regional framing rather than
a Zhuozhou-specific statement], and Zhuozhou was separately hit by catastrophic 2023 flooding
that displaced over a million people regionally and drew specific criticism (it was reportedly
used as a flood-diversion "sacrifice zone" to protect Beijing) [1-SOURCE, 9dashline/CNN] — a
real, later, and distinct demand shock on top of whatever property-cycle decline preceded it,
which this dossier deliberately does not conflate with the 2017-21 price collapse.

**The 2022 mortgage-strike (烂尾楼) episode** is the sharpest evidence of how deep commuter-belt
and satellite-city distress ran: homebuyers in at least **80 cities** (rising to 300+ locations by
August 2022) boycotted mortgage payments on stalled, unfinished projects, with loans at issue
estimated at up to **CNY 2 trillion (~US$297 billion)** [2-SOURCE, CNN + RFA/Time, independently
corroborating both the city count and the loan-value figure]. Coverage explicitly singled out "a
town next to Beijing" (Yanjiao) as an early, prominent case [1-SOURCE, Yicai Global headline] —
tying the commuter-belt collapse directly to the national mortgage-strike wave rather than
treating them as separate stories.

## Q7 — Tangshan/Baoding/Qinhuangdao (Hebei) and Weihai/Zhoushan/Yantai (coastal second-home)

**Tangshan** (Hebei's most economically developed city, a major steel producer): home prices fell
for **18 consecutive months** through a February print (year of that specific print unclear from
the snippet) [1-SOURCE], and were down **-0.8% versus two years earlier as of July 2022**
[1-SOURCE] — a milder cumulative figure than the "18 straight months" framing suggests, which
this dossier flags rather than resolves (likely different base periods). Overbuilding during the
steel-boom years left a surplus that analysts estimate could take **up to 13 years to unwind**
[1-SOURCE]. In the most recent (2026) snapshot, Tangshan is named among the three worst
tier-3 performers nationally for new-build price declines, at **-6.5% YoY**, alongside Beihai's
-6.7% [1-SOURCE] — tagged **[2-SOURCE]** overall for "Tangshan is a persistent, multi-year
underperformer" given the two separate time windows both showing weakness, though no single
peak-to-trough percentage was found.

**Baoding and Qinhuangdao**: broader Hebei coverage states prices declined in "almost half" of
the 70 tracked large/medium cities across the region generally [1-SOURCE], but one specific
index citation complicates a blanket "the whole Hebei belt crashed" story: **after 2016, the
index rose in Zhangjiakou, Baoding, and Shijiazhuang while it "significantly decreased" in
Langfang, Tangshan, Qinhuangdao, and Handan** [1-SOURCE, PLOS/PMC-style academic land-price
study]. This is worth stating plainly because it means Baoding — geographically part of the same
Hebei belt — is reported moving in the *opposite direction* from Langfang/Tangshan/Qinhuangdao in
that window; the "Hebei belt" is not one uniform story even within one province, and this dossier
does not smooth that over. No further Baoding or Qinhuangdao figures were found beyond this one
citation — a gap.

**Weihai** (Shandong, coastal second-home market): 7,533.251 CNY/sqm (December 2024) down from
8,286.345 CNY/sqm (December 2023) [1-SOURCE, CEIC-style index] — a computed **-9.1% YoY**
decline (computed by this dossier from the two index points, not itself a quoted percentage, so
flagged as derived rather than a directly-sourced figure). **Yantai and Zhoushan**: **no
city-specific figures were found** despite two dedicated searches for each — named explicitly as
gaps rather than filled with a plausible-sounding number. The general coastal/second-home
narrative (no major city recording price gains, most in the -5% to -8% YoY band as of mid-2026)
[1-SOURCE] applies at the national level but could not be pinned to Yantai or Zhoushan
specifically this session.

## Q8 — Hainan province as a whole

**The 2018 purchase ban**: imposed roughly a week after Beijing's free-trade-zone announcement for
the island [1-SOURCE, SCMP]. Non-local buyers province-wide needed proof of 24 months' local
social-security payments; in the hotspot cities of Haikou, Sanya, and Qionghai the requirement
rose to 60 months; non-local buyers were banned outright in the central mountainous region; homes
bought after 30 March 2018 could not be resold for five years [1-SOURCE, SCMP, with the
resale-lockup detail independently repeated in Hainan-focused property-guide sources — tagged
**[2-SOURCE]** for the substance of the restrictions]. **As of the most recent citation sourced**,
the requirement structure persists in similar form: 60 consecutive months of local tax/social-
insurance payments for urban-area purchases, 24 months for non-urban [1-SOURCE, official Hainan
government English-language housing guidance page].

**The free-trade-port push**: announced by Xi Jinping in 2018, approved by the State Council
shortly after, and the Free Trade Port **completed customs closure and began full operation on
18 December 2025** [1-SOURCE, Wikipedia/Hainan FTP page] — i.e., the headline institutional
milestone the province had been building toward since 2018 landed only in the last ~9 months
before this dossier's compilation date.

**Where prices are now**: province-wide down-payment requirements were cut to 20% (from 25%) as a
stimulus measure, mirroring easing in Beijing/Shanghai [1-SOURCE, Benzinga]; a state-owned firm
purchased 37 apartments in Hainan for CNY 139.30 million (~US$20 million, implying ~CNY 3.76
million per unit, consistent with larger/luxury stock) as part of the state-purchase-to-absorb-
inventory program seen nationally [1-SOURCE, Reuters via Investing.com]. City-level current
prices sourced: Danzhou 8,908→11,328 CNY/sqm (2023→Feb 2024, rising) — see Q3. No comprehensive
Haikou/Sanya current-price figure was independently pinned down this session beyond passing CEIC
series references — a gap. Overall: Hainan's *current* cycle reads as **stimulus-supported and
government-purchase-supported**, sitting on top of a market still shaped by the unique 2018
purchase restrictions, a world away from the 1992-93 crash covered in Q3 — the two Hainan
episodes (1990s bubble/bust, 2018-present restriction regime) are distinct and should not be
conflated.

## Q9 — Do satellite towns fall harder than independent tier-3 cities?

The evidence assembled this session is **suggestive, not a controlled comparison**, and this
dossier says so rather than overclaiming. What can be stated:
- Langfang/Yanjiao (Beijing's satellite belt) fell **>50%** off its 2017 peak on the sourced
  figures (Q6) — a materially larger decline than the ~-30% cumulative figure this desk's own
  base-rate framing (CLAUDE.md) attributes to tier-3 cities generally as the "full international-
  standard crash." If the ~30% tier-3 base rate is right, Langfang's >50% looks like a genuine
  outlier to the downside even within tier-3.
- The mechanism proposed in the general China-housing literature sourced here is indirect but
  consistent: cities with the **slowest household-income growth** see the largest price declines
  [1-SOURCE, Oxford-Economics-style framing], and pure commuter/satellite towns are close to
  definitionally weak on local income and job base (residents commute out for income, the town
  itself doesn't generate it) — a plausible mechanism, not a demonstrated causal test.
- **No academic or press source directly comparing satellite-town declines to matched
  independent tier-3 cities was found**, despite two dedicated searches for exactly this
  comparison. The Langfang/Yanjiao evidence is a single, admittedly extreme case, not a sample.
- **Tentative answer, honestly hedged**: the one well-sourced satellite case (Langfang/Yanjiao)
  fell harder than the tier-3 base rate; this is consistent with, but does not statistically
  establish, "satellite towns fall harder than independent tier-3 cities" as a general rule.
  Zhuozhou's case is qualitative-only and cannot add a second data point. Treat this as a
  **single-case pattern flagged for a follow-up design**, not a booked finding.

## Q10 — Provincial capitals as a class

**The policy is real and named.** The "strong provincial capital" (强省会) strategy explicitly
concentrates administrative, fiscal, educational, and infrastructure resources in a province's
capital city to make it a growth pole; **Guizhou** is explicitly named as running a "Strong
Capital" strategy for Guiyang [1-SOURCE], and the pattern is independently described (without
always using the Chinese term) for **Anhui** (Hefei), **Henan** (Zhengzhou), and — with the
caveat that Sichuan actually runs a dual-core Chengdu-Chongqing model rather than pure
single-capital concentration — **Sichuan** (Chengdu) and **Hubei** (Wuhan, which "already
accounts for more than half of Hubei's economy") [2-SOURCE, NUS EAI "Heartland Surge" research
brief + independent press/Yicai coverage of the same 2023-24 migration data]. The mechanism named:
migrant workers followed relocated industry inland after the 2011-2015 industrial-transformation
plan, and provincial capitals receive preferential funding for infrastructure, higher education
and administrative functions that draws domestic migrants disproportionately [2-SOURCE].

**Do capitals systematically outperform non-capital cities in the same province?** The answer
this session's sourcing supports is **yes on the boom, mixed on the bust**:
- **2003-2017 boom**: provincial capitals and other major commercial centers (the "tier-2"
  bracket in this framing) appreciated roughly **400%** cumulatively, versus roughly **200%** for
  typical tier-3 cities over the same period [1-SOURCE] — capitals clearly won the run-up.
- **2023 net migration**: Hefei +219,000, Guiyang +182,500, Zhengzhou +180,000 residents in a
  single year, each explicitly tied to industrial policy (EVs, big-data/cloud infrastructure,
  electronics) [1-SOURCE, Yicai], while population loss concentrates in non-capital,
  especially north-eastern, prefecture-level cities — 92.7% of which lost population over the
  decade [1-SOURCE, academic geography journal] — and provincial capitals are named as the
  primary in-province migration destination for people leaving those shrinking cities
  [2-SOURCE, Frontiers journal article + the "how do Chinese cities beat population decline"
  analysis, independently reaching the same conclusion].
- **Current property-price cycle (2025-26)**: this is where the story is genuinely mixed and this
  dossier will not paper over it. Among China's 10 largest cities, **Nanjing** (capital of
  Jiangsu) and **Wuhan** (capital of Hubei) posted the two steepest secondary-market YoY declines
  in June 2026, at **-11.45% and -10.89%** respectively [1-SOURCE] — worse than the tier-3
  national new-build average of -4.2% to -5.8% cited elsewhere in the programme's other dossiers.
  Meanwhile **Shenyang** (capital of Liaoning) was named a "pillar of stability" alongside
  Shanghai in the same reporting window [1-SOURCE], and **Guangzhou** (capital of Guangdong,
  -8% to -10% YoY) and **Hangzhou** (capital of Zhejiang, -5% to -8% YoY) sit in the "serious but
  not worst" band [1-SOURCE each]. **Harbin, Changchun, and Hohhot** — capitals of the three
  weakest-population provinces sourced in this dossier — show only very low absolute price levels
  (Changchun 6,100-8,300 CNY/sqm; Hohhot 6,800-9,200 CNY/sqm, both **planning ranges rather than
  verified transaction data**, tagged accordingly) with **no decline percentage sourced**, so
  this dossier cannot place them on a fall-ranking at all — a real gap, not an omission.

**Ranked most-to-least fallen, capitals only, as far as this session's sourcing allows** (an
honestly partial ranking — a full 31-capital ranking was searched for twice and not found):
1. Nanjing (-11.45% secondhand YoY, Jun 2026) — worst sourced
2. Wuhan (-10.89% secondhand YoY, Jun 2026)
3. Guangzhou (-8% to -10% YoY, mid-2026)
4. Hangzhou (-5% to -8% YoY, Jun 2026)
5. Shenyang (named comparatively stable, no negative figure attached in the source found)
- Harbin, Changchun, Hohhot, Nanning, Kunming, Lanzhou, Xining, and the remaining ~24 capitals:
  **not rankable from this session's search results** — named explicitly as unresolved rather
  than filled with estimates.

**Verdict, stated plainly**: the "strong provincial capital" policy is a real, named, and
provinces-specific resource-concentration strategy with clear demographic consequences (capitals
win the internal migration race within their province). It also clearly shaped the *long-run*
price-appreciation gap between capitals and ordinary tier-3 cities (400% vs 200%, 2003-2017). But
**it is not a reliable predictor of which cities fall least in the current down-cycle** — two of
the biggest capitals sourced (Nanjing, Wuhan) are among the worst current decliners nationally,
which likely reflects prior over-building keyed to the same growth-pole status that drove the
boom (more supply chasing the policy narrative) rather than any special resilience. Capital status
looks like it shaped the *shape* of each city's cycle (bigger boom) more reliably than it shaped
*downside protection*.
