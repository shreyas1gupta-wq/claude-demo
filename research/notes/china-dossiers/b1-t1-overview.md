## Q1. Official NBS tier-1/2/3 aggregates — new-home and second-hand, peak, latest, cumulative decline

### 1a. Definitions and data-quality caveat (read before the numbers)

The NBS "Sales Prices of Commercial Residential Buildings in 70 Medium and Large-sized Cities"
survey has published monthly since 2011 (35 cities from 1997, expanded to 70 from 2005/2011).
**NBS's own headline releases report month-on-month (MoM) and year-on-year (YoY) percent changes
— not a published rebased cumulative index level.** Any "cumulative % decline from peak" figure
in this dossier is therefore a THIRD PARTY reconstruction (chaining NBS's MoM prints, or a
separate BIS/CEIC/Dallas-Fed series, or a private data house's own index) — never NBS's own
stated number — and different reconstructions disagree with each other by construction (different
chaining conventions, different base periods, some real/deflated, some nominal). They are
reported separately below and must not be merged into one "the" number.
[China Briefing, tier system origin](https://www.china-briefing.com/news/chinas-city-tier-classification-defined/) ·
[NBER w21112, Fang et al., methodology](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21112/w21112.pdf)

The NBS methodology itself is criticized in the academic literature for relying on a matched
same/comparable-complex sample rather than a full transaction census, which several economists
argue smooths turning points and biases the index toward understating both the up-cycle and (more
relevantly here) the down-cycle. [CEPR VoxEU, "China's housing boom"](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/chinas-housing-boom) ·
[NBER w21112](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21112/w21112.pdf)

## Q2. Why has tier-1 held up better than tier-2/3? Mechanism, not just the number

Five distinct, sourced mechanisms, roughly in order of how directly they're evidenced:

1. **Population inflow vs outflow.** Tier-1 (and strong "new tier-1") cities continue to draw
   in-migration on jobs/infrastructure/industrial-upgrade grounds; tier-3 cities are seeing
   population outflow ACCELERATE. Demand-side support in tier-1 is a demographic fact, not a
   policy artifact. [Oxford Economics, 2026-01-28](https://www.oxfordeconomics.com/resource/house-prices-continue-to-slide-for-chinas-cities/)
2. **Land supply scarcity is structural in tier-1, especially Shanghai.** A shortage of
   developable land supply constrains new completions even as transaction volumes fall,
   propping up new-build prices where "product upgrades" (higher-spec new supply) let developers
   hold headline price even with softer volume. [Caixin commentary, 2025-12-24](https://www.caixinglobal.com/2025-12-24/commentary-why-top-tier-cities-will-lead-chinas-uneven-property-recovery-102396804.html)
3. **Inventory / vacancy imbalance is a tier-3 problem, not (mainly) a tier-1 one.** Tier-3
   cities hold roughly 50% of national housing stock value against >60% of GDP but face far
   deeper supply-demand mismatch; low vacancy in Shanghai/Dalian is offsetting weak demand there,
   while lower tiers carry the inventory overhang. [Oxford Economics](https://www.oxfordeconomics.com/resource/house-prices-continue-to-slide-for-chinas-cities/) ·
   [Stanford FSI, "A Tale of Tier 3 Cities"](https://sccei.fsi.stanford.edu/china-briefs/tier-3-cities-hotbed-trouble-chinas-property-sector)
4. **SOE developer concentration is highest in tier-1/tier-2.** State-owned developers took
   ~40% of national sales share (SCMP, 2022) and SOEs among the top-100 developers took ~67% of
   top-100 sales, "mainly because they primarily invested in first- and second-tier cities" —
   SOEs carry lower default risk than the private developers (Evergrande, Country Garden-type)
   whose distress is concentrated in lower-tier land banks. This is a supply-side credit-quality
   mechanism, distinct from demand. [SCMP, 2022](https://www.scmp.com/business/article/3216561/state-owned-firms-extend-domination-china-property-market-support-its-recovery-analysts-say) ·
   [Mingtiandi](https://www.mingtiandi.com/real-estate/research-policy/soe-developers-dominate-china-home-sales-ranks-in-2022/)
5. **Policy easing landed in tier-1 last, and asymmetrically.** All four tier-1 cities relaxed
   hukou/social-security home-purchase restrictions only from 2024 onward (Guangzhou removed ALL
   restrictions; Shenzhen removed restrictions in six districts for non-local buyers with ≥1 year
   of local tax/social-security payment; Beijing extended purchase quotas outside the fifth ring
   road) — i.e., tier-1 cities had MORE restriction to relax, and relaxing it is itself a
   demand-side lever that doesn't exist in already-unrestricted lower-tier cities.
   [english.scio.gov.cn](http://english.scio.gov.cn/chinavoices/2024-05/10/content_117178657.htm) ·
   [Bloomberg, Shenzhen](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2025-09-05/china-s-shenzhen-joins-major-cities-in-easing-home-buying-rules)

None of these five is "tier-1 is simply safer" — they compound: richer, growing buyer base
(1) + constrained new supply (2) + smaller existing overhang (3) + safer credit counterparty on
the supply side (4) + more policy lever left to pull (5).

## Q4. The official-vs-private gap: how much does NBS understate the fall, and who says so

### 4a. The mechanism claim (who says NBS understates, and why)

"Industry insiders and economists say China's official home-price indexes are likely
understating the depth of the downturn, in part because of longstanding methodologies that
struggle to capture market turning points." CBRE research is quoted specifically: China's
official approach "uses selective samples rather than data based on total market transactions,"
and "when a market goes down, the true market condition is hard to be reflected in such data."
[Asia Times, 2026-07](https://asiatimes.com/2026/07/chinas-housing-market-free-falls-as-buyers-wait-for-floor-prices/)

Academically, the same critique is made independently by Fang, Gu, Xiong & Zhou (NBER
w21112/w21346, "Demystifying the Chinese Housing Boom"): the matched-sample method fails to
control for the compositional drift of transacted units toward outer, cheaper rings, and fails
to control for developers' own pricing/discounting behavior in the new-build sector — both
biases run the SAME direction (understating true price moves, and specifically understating
declines on the way down). [NBER w21112](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w21112/w21112.pdf) ·
[CEPR VoxEU summary](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/chinas-housing-boom)

## Q5. The 4 tier-1 cities, and the "new tier-1" list of 15

**The 4 official tier-1 cities: Beijing, Shanghai, Guangzhou, Shenzhen** — this is the
NBS-anchored definition used throughout the 70-city price survey since the 2011 "Residential
Sales Price Statistical Survey Program" reform (35-city survey from 1997, expanded to 70 cities
from 2005/2011). [China Briefing](https://www.china-briefing.com/news/chinas-city-tier-classification-defined/) ·
corroborated by essentially every NBS-derived price release cited above, which all report
"first-tier cities" as exactly these four.

Classification criteria generally cited: GDP, population size, and political/administrative
status (all four are either national capital, a directly-administered municipality, or a
sub-provincial/special-economic-zone city with outsized GDP and >15 million population).
[China Briefing](https://www.china-briefing.com/news/chinas-city-tier-classification-defined/)

**The "new tier-1" (新一线) list of 15** is a DIFFERENT, commercially-produced ranking — not an
NBS or government classification — published annually by **Yicai (第一财经/CBN, formerly China
Business Network)'s New First-Tier Cities Research Institute (新一线城市研究所)**. It ranks 337
prefecture-level-and-above cities on five weighted sub-indices: Concentration of Commercial
Resources, City-as-a-Hub, Urban Residents' Activity, New Economy Competitiveness, and Future
Potential. [Yicai Global, May 2025 release](https://www.yicaiglobal.com/news/chengdu-hangzhou-13-others-rank-as-new-first-tier-chinese-cities-in-2025)

status has NOT translated into tier-1-like price resilience for at least these two cities, a
useful negative finding for anyone tempted to trade the Yicai list as a price-resilience proxy.
