# Black Money in Indian Real Estate — Size, the 2016-17 Formalisation Shock Stack, and What Happened After

Agent dossier, IN programme (Half B), principal-directed. Compiled 2026-09-11. WebSearch only —
WebFetch is EGRESS_BLOCKED for every domain and was not attempted; every figure below is a
search-snippet read, not a re-read primary page, at the india-property-plan.md §0 standard.
**Corroboration tags used throughout**: `[2-SOURCE]` = two independent outlets/searches converged;
`[1-SOURCE]` = one outlet only; `[RECALL — unverified]` = context only, not found this session.
Every figure is also read as either a **PRICE** effect or a **VOLUME/transaction-count** effect —
conflating the two is named explicitly as the standard error this brief is built to avoid. 42
searches used against the 34-42 budget. No moral or tax-planning view is offered anywhere below;
this is a market-structure question and is treated as one.

## Headline findings

- **The size of the cash component is real but never officially measured** — every number in
  circulation is an industry-consultant claim (Anarock), an old academic study (NIPFP 1995,
  Liases Foras 2012), a self-selected online survey (LocalCircles), or an anecdotal/"viral post"
  claim (farmland 75-80%). No probability-sample government statistic exists. The segment ordering
  is nonetheless consistent across every source found: **land/agricultural > resale/luxury >
  branded primary/bank-financed**, in that order, every time it is mentioned.
- **Anarock's 75-80% claim is real, sourced, and about HOUSING specifically — not real estate as a
  whole** `[2-SOURCE]`. Its own stated mechanism is compositional (branded/listed developers who
  "play by the book" now capture most demand), not a re-measurement of cash intensity, and Anarock
  sells services to precisely the developers whose formalisation it credits — a named conflict
  worth holding in mind when reading the number.
- **The 2016-17 shock stack did NOT crash national prices — it crashed LAUNCHES.** All-India nominal
  house prices (RBI HPI) kept rising through 2017-2020, just decelerating (~7-8%/yr → ~3-4%/yr);
  against ~4-5% CPI that is real-terms stagnation, not a price crash. Meanwhile top-7-city new
  launches fell ~44% over the five years after demonetisation while SALES fell only ~12% — the
  market absorbed existing inventory and let new supply retrench, a volume/supply-side adjustment,
  not a price-side one `[2-SOURCE, Anarock]`.
- **Mumbai's own flat-to-negative real-terms 2014-2021 run (established by the sibling MMR dossier,
  `research/notes/india-dossiers/mumbai-inner.md` §5) lines up exactly with this national pattern**,
  and its post-2021 re-rating (+48% cumulative MMR average, 2019→H1 2024) is a genuine regime break
  that postdates the shock stack, not a continuation of it.
- **Cash did not disappear, it concentrated where the shock stack structurally cannot reach**: land
  and standalone-plot sales are entirely OUTSIDE GST `[2-SOURCE]`; resale is GST-exempt and outside
  RERA's primary-market escrow rule; so the two laws doing the most formalisation work (RERA, GST)
  apply almost exclusively to new, under-construction, developer-sold housing. Nine years on,
  survey and enforcement evidence both still find cash endemic outside that one segment.
- **Maharashtra's own 2020-21 stamp-duty cut is the cleanest natural experiment in the record for the
  PRICE-vs-VOLUME distinction**: cutting a transaction cost (not cash) moved registrations up
  sharply (Jan-Nov 2021 +122% YoY) while prices stayed flat-to-soft (Mumbai prime -1.1% YoY, Q2
  2021) — the same shape the 2016-17 stack itself produced, evidence that friction/cost shocks in
  this market move volume far more readily than price.

## Q1. Size of the cash component, by segment

See the SEGMENT TABLE above for the full breakdown and sourcing. The compressed read: no single
source gives a uniform-methodology, contemporaneous split across primary/secondary/land/agricultural
— the table is compiled from several partial, non-uniform sources and should be read as an
**ordering** (land > resale/luxury > primary-branded), not as precise point estimates. The clearest
structural reason for the ordering is mechanical, not behavioural: RERA's escrow rule and GST's tax
net both apply **only** to new, under-construction, developer-sold product; land and resale sit
entirely outside both nets, so whatever cash the 2016-17 stack pushed out of primary housing had
nowhere structural to go except into the segments the stack does not reach.

## Q2. Anarock's 75-80% claim

Found and sourced `[2-SOURCE]`: Anarock stated, repeatedly since at least Nov 2021 and reaffirmed in
Nov 2024 retrospectives, that black money in Indian **housing** has fallen at least 75-80% since
November 2016. Anarock chairman Anuj Puri's own stated rationale is compositional: "branded, listed
players — who now attract a significant majority of housing demand to their projects — play by the
book and avoid unaccounted monies." The supporting data Anarock cites alongside the claim is a
launches/sales comparison (pre-demon 2013–Q3 2016 vs post-demon Q4 2016–Q3 2021, top 7 cities: see
timeline table) — a **supply-side** metric, not a direct re-measurement of cash intensity in
transactions. No technical methodology for the 75-80% figure itself (i.e., how cash intensity was
measured before and after) was found in any source this session. **Corroborating**: CREDAI and
NAREDCO's real-time 2016 predictions (resale/premium segment hit hardest, primary spared) point the
same direction and are consistent with Anarock's compositional story. **Contradicting, in
magnitude if not in direction**: the LocalCircles 2025 survey and the Nov 2024 "8 years post
demonetisation" coverage both still find pervasive cash use in **recent** transactions — but these
surveys are not segment-matched to Anarock's "housing" claim (they likely capture more resale/land
respondents) and are not a re-measurement of the SAME thing Anarock measured, so the two are not a
clean contradiction so much as evidence that Anarock's claim, even if directionally correct for
branded primary housing, cannot be extrapolated to the whole property market.

## Q3. Survey evidence — LocalCircles and the method problem

**The LocalCircles survey** `[1-SOURCE instrument, widely re-reported]`: ~39,000 responses across
310 districts (most recently reported Nov 2025, an earlier "One Year of Demonetisation" wave ran in
2017). Headline: **65%** (~"two in three") of people who bought property in the last three years
admitted paying part of the transaction in cash; of those, **26%** paid over half the value in cash,
19% paid 30-50%, 14% paid 10-30%. Separately, **90%** of ALL respondents (not just recent buyers)
believe black money is prevalent in real estate — a perception question, not a transaction report.

**The method weakness, named plainly rather than glossed over**: this is a self-selected
online-community panel (LocalCircles app/web users opt in to answer a public survey about admitting
to a form of tax evasion). The bias could run either way and there is no way to determine which
dominates from the source material: respondents frustrated by a bad cash-demanding experience may be
more motivated to answer and to over-report; respondents self-incriminating in an identifiable
platform may under-report the true frequency or magnitude. 39,000 responses over 310 districts is a
large N but is **not a probability sample** of Indian property buyers, has no independent
verification against any transaction record, and the survey's own framing (a "black money and
corruption" survey) likely selects respondents already primed to answer in that direction. Treat the
65-90% figures as an upper-bound-flavoured, self-reported prevalence signal, not a measured cash
share of transaction value.

## Q4. The 2016-17 shock stack, mechanism by mechanism

- **Demonetisation (8 Nov 2016)**: a pure liquidity shock — withdrew the two largest-denomination
  notes, instantly removing the ability to source or hold large undeclared cash sums. Hit cash-heavy
  resale/land/premium deals hardest; primary, bank-financed, end-user-driven markets (Bengaluru,
  Pune, Chennai per CREDAI-era reporting) saw minimal impact.
- **Benami Amendment (1 Nov 2016)**: not a cash-flow shock but an **ownership-tracing** shock — makes
  the classic vehicle for parking black money in real estate (buying in a nominee's name) newly
  confiscable without compensation and criminally punishable. Targets the land/plot segment
  specifically, since land-banking-via-proxy is the archetypal benami transaction.
- **RERA (1 May 2017)**: a **primary-market-only** structural shock — 70% of buyer collections must
  sit in an escrowed, project-specific bank account, released only against certified construction
  milestones. This is the single biggest reason a developer can no longer freely absorb cash from a
  primary buyer: the money must arrive traceably to fund the specific project.
- **GST (1 Jul 2017, restructured 1 Apr 2019)**: also **primary/under-construction-only** — land sale
  and completed/ready-to-move resale are outside its scope by design. Its formalising force is
  therefore mechanically confined to exactly the same segment RERA already targets.
- **Section 269ST (Apr 2017)**: the one measure that is genuinely **segment-blind** — a ₹2 lakh cap
  on any single cash receipt, with a 100% penalty on the receiver, applies equally to primary,
  secondary and land deals. Enforcement, however, depends on someone reporting the cash mention —
  which is precisely why the Supreme Court had to step in as recently as April 2025 to compel
  sub-registrars and courts to proactively flag such mentions to the Income Tax Department, implying
  the self-reporting design alone was not sufficient eight years on.

## Q5. What actually happened to prices and volumes, 2016-2021

See the DATED TIMELINE for the full sequencing. Compressed answer: **the market decelerated and
retrenched on new supply; it did not crash on price.** All-India RBI HPI growth ran ~7.4-7.6%/yr in
FY2017-18 (i.e., still accelerating through the RERA/GST rollout year), then moderated across
successive quarters to ~3.4% (Q1 FY2019-20) and ~2.8% (Q1 FY2020-21) — one source summarises the
2017-2020 window as "3.7% per annum" growth. **Nominal prices never went negative at the composite
level** in any figure found this session; against ~4-5% CPI that implies real-terms stagnation, which
is exactly what the sibling Mumbai dossier independently found for the most cash-exposed metro (flat
to -1.1% YoY as late as Q2 2021, prime segment). **Volumes told a sharper story, but concentrated in
SUPPLY**: top-7-city sales fell from 11.78 lakh to 10.37 lakh units across the pre-/post-demonetisation
five-year windows (~-12%), while LAUNCHES fell from 16.15 lakh to 9.04 lakh units (~-44%) over the
same span — developers pulled back on new product far more than buyers pulled back on absorbing what
already existed. Metro vs tier-2/3 and primary vs secondary both plausibly differ (CREDAI/NAREDCO
both said resale/premium would be hit hardest and end-user-driven cities like Bengaluru/Pune/Chennai
least) but no source gave a clean, separately-indexed tier-2/3 or secondary-market time series to
quantify that split for this dossier.

## Q6. Why did it NOT crash if cash was that important?

Four candidate mechanisms, weighed against the evidence gathered:

1. **Credit substitution.** Bank/HFC housing credit grew 21%/18%/19%/14% YoY across FY2016-17 to
   FY2018-19 `[1-SOURCE]` — fast enough to plausibly absorb some cash-displaced demand. But this
   cannot be the whole story: India's mortgage-to-GDP ratio is still only ~11-12% as of FY24 (vs
   ~51-82% in the US/UK, ~28-30% in China) `[2-SOURCE]`, so formal credit's absolute footprint in
   Indian housing remains small; and the credit channel itself froze in the IL&FS/NBFC crisis
   18 months into the post-demonetisation window, so it cannot be credited for smoothing the whole
   2016-2021 period — it helped early, then became a headwind.
2. **Demand shift to RERA-compliant primary product.** Anarock's own stated mechanism: branded/listed
   developers captured a growing majority of demand and "play by the book." This is a
   **compositional** shift in the mix of the market, not a shrinkage of the whole market, and is the
   best-evidenced mechanism (stated directly by the source making the 75-80% claim, and consistent
   with CREDAI/NAREDCO's contemporaneous predictions).
3. **Developer consolidation.** RERA compliance costs plus the IL&FS/NBFC credit freeze pushed
   smaller, more cash-dependent developers toward distress (debt-equity ratios up sharply for small
   and mid-size players) while larger, listed developers gained share — structurally lowering the
   average cash share of PRIMARY transactions even without touching resale or land practices at all.
4. **Cash moved rather than vanished.** GST and RERA are mechanically confined to primary
   under-construction sales (Q4); land and resale sit entirely outside both. The persistence of high
   self-reported cash use nine years on (LocalCircles 2025), continuing "on-money" enforcement raids
   (e.g., a builder group's seized data reportedly spanning 10+ years of unaccounted cash receipts
   `[1-SOURCE]`), and the still-necessary 2025 Supreme Court reporting mandate are all more
   consistent with displacement toward the segments the stack does not reach than with elimination.

**Weighing them**: mechanisms 2 and 3 (compositional shift + consolidation) are the best-evidenced —
directly stated by Anarock and consistent with independently-sourced NBFC/developer-leverage data.
Mechanism 1 (credit substitution) is real but bounded and partly self-undermining given the 2018-19
credit freeze. Mechanism 4 (displacement) best explains why national surveys still find cash
pervasive even as the branded/primary segment measurably cleaned up. The deeper reason prices did
not crash is that the **marginal price-setting transaction** was increasingly the formal,
bank-financed end-user one (least exposed to a cash shock, and the segment CREDAI/NAREDCO both said
in real time would be least affected), while the cash-exposed segments (resale, land, premium)
adjusted on **volume** — Indian real estate prices are documented as sticky (developers historically
prefer to cut launches, offer discounts/freebies, or let inventory age rather than mark down listed
prices) — consistent with the launches-fell-far-more-than-sales asymmetry above.

## Q7. Where has cash persisted, and why

Land/plots (especially agricultural), resale/secondary, and smaller towns/tier-2-3/peri-urban areas
are named across every source that discusses persistence. The structural reason, independent of the
2016-17 stack entirely: **the circle-rate/stamp-duty gap**. Where the state-notified circle
(ready-reckoner/guidance/jantri) rate is stale relative to the true market price, the legal minimum
declarable value sits below the true transaction value — under-declaring toward that floor while
paying the difference in cash saves stamp duty (charged on the higher of declared price or circle
rate) AND reduces both parties' tax exposure (Section 50C-style deemed capital gains for the seller,
Section 56(2)(x) income-from-other-sources exposure for the buyer beyond a 10% tolerance band).
None of demonetisation, the Benami amendment, RERA, GST, or Section 269ST touches stamp-duty RATES
or circle-rate REVISION FREQUENCY — both are state subjects, entirely outside the five-measure
stack, and Maharashtra's own ready-reckoner rate sat **frozen for three straight years (FY2019-20
through FY2021-22)** before a marginal ~3.9-4.4% catch-up resumed `[2-SOURCE]` — the gap the shock
stack never closed kept widening even as the stack's OTHER levers (RERA, GST, Benami) were doing
their formalising work elsewhere.

## Q8. The stamp-duty and circle-rate mechanism, and Maharashtra's natural experiment

Mechanism as above. **Maharashtra's 2020-21 cut is the cleanest available before/after in the
record**: stamp duty was cut from 5% to 2% (Sep-Dec 2020), raised to 3% (Jan-Mar 2021), and restored
to 5%+1% metro cess from April 2021 `[2-SOURCE]` — a pure transaction-COST experiment, run in the
same window the state's circle rates sat frozen. Outcome: registrations surged sharply (Mumbai
~18,000 conveyances in Dec 2020 alone; Jan-Nov 2021 volumes +122% YoY vs the same period in 2020;
full-2021 registrations exceeded 100,000, a 10-year high) `[2-SOURCE, external + internal IGR data,
mumbai-inner.md §7]`, while PRICES stayed flat-to-soft over the identical window (Mumbai prime still
-1.1% YoY in Q2 2021, Knight Frank). This is a **transaction-cost** experiment, not a cash-removal
one, but it demonstrates the same PRICE-vs-VOLUME shape the 2016-17 shock stack itself produced:
lowering (or here, raising) the friction of registering a transaction moves the NUMBER of
transactions sharply while leaving the clearing PRICE comparatively undisturbed. One credit-rating
commentary (India Ratings, contemporaneous) had predicted the cut would "do little to boost demand"
— a prior the subsequent registration surge appears to have falsified, though pandemic-deferred
demand and falling interest rates are named, un-decomposed confounds in the same window.

## Q9. What would a full removal actually hit — segment, buyer type, price vs volume

Reasoned from the evidence above, not from new search data. **By segment**: land/agricultural and
resale/premium carry both the highest reported cash share AND sit outside every net GST/RERA already
cast — a hypothetical full removal would have to work through Benami-style ownership-tracing and
269ST-style payment-channel enforcement specifically in THOSE segments, since the primary/branded/
bank-financed segment is already close to its no-cash equilibrium (Q1-Q2). **By buyer type**: the
marginal buyer who actually SETS the clearing price in the housing stock is disproportionately the
formal, credit-linked end-user — the segment least exposed to any cash shock, per CREDAI/NAREDCO's
own 2016 predictions and the data since. The marginal participant in resale/land, by contrast, is
disproportionately cash-motivated (wealth-parking, capital-gains avoidance via the agricultural-land
round-trip). **Removing cash from that second set of participants should be read primarily as a
VOLUME/transaction-count effect concentrated in resale and land — not a fresh national PRICE effect**,
because the segment that sets the visible index (formal primary) is not the segment losing its cash
channel. **The standard error to avoid, explicitly**: 2016-era commentary predicted a 20-30% PRICE
fall; what was actually observed nationally was overwhelmingly a LAUNCH/VOLUME contraction (-44%)
alongside prices that merely decelerated. Treating "prices stayed flat" as "nothing happened," or
treating the launch collapse as if it were a price collapse, are the two conflations this dossier
was built to keep apart.

## Q10. Comparative evidence — countries that formalised a cash-heavy property market

- **British Columbia, Canada** (Expert Panel — Maloney, Somerville, Unger, 2019) `[2-SOURCE across
  CBC, Globe and Mail, Business in Vancouver, official BC government release]`: estimated
  CAD 0.8-5.3bn laundered through BC real estate in 2018 alone, raising province-wide housing prices
  by an estimated **3.7-7.5%** (midpoint ~5%), with Metro Vancouver specifically cited as inflated by
  "upwards of 20%" from laundering-linked demand. This is a genuine quantified PRICE-effect precedent
  — but it is an **ex-ante panel estimate**, not a measured before/after a subsequent policy change,
  and the mechanism (foreign/domestic laundering demand into a supply-constrained market) differs
  from India's largely domestic tax-avoidance cash economy.
- **Pakistan** (FBR property valuation tables, introduced 2016, revised 2018/2019/2021/2022,
  partially reversed 2026) `[1-SOURCE, qualitative]`: a closer structural analog to raising India's
  own circle rates — FBR set official valuations above the old District-Collector rates specifically
  to shrink the tax-computation gap with true market value. Reporting characterises the effect as
  discouraging transactions (especially by non-filers, via higher withholding tax) and causing a
  "slight dip" in both trading activity and prices after each revision — a VOLUME-led adjustment,
  the same shape India's own record shows, but not independently quantified in the sources found.
  Notably, in April 2026 the FBR was forced to CUT valuation rates 10-35% in several Punjab cities
  after real-estate-lobby pushback — the same tension visible in Maharashtra's own choice to freeze
  ready-reckoner rates through 2019-22 rather than close the gap.
- **South Korea** (Real-Name Financial Transaction System, 1993, extended to asset-holding more
  broadly) `[1-SOURCE, directional only]`: retrospectively credited with reducing the underground
  economy and blocking borrowed-name asset-holding, but no source found this session gave a
  quantified real-estate price or volume before/after figure — named as a data gap, not treated as a
  quantified comparator.
- **Across all three**: no comparator produced a clean "prices crashed X%" result. Every case shows
  the same qualitative shape as India's own record — a formalisation or valuation shock hits
  VOLUME/transactions first and hardest, with price effects that are either modest (BC, single-digit
  percent) or not separately measured at all (Pakistan, South Korea).

## Closing — crash, or sideways? Answered in the principal's own terms

**PRICE: sideways.** All-India nominal house prices never fell at the composite level through the
2016-17 shock stack — growth decelerated from ~7-8%/yr to ~3-4%/yr, which against ~4-5% CPI is
real-terms stagnation, not a crash. The most cash-exposed major metro, Mumbai, independently shows
the identical shape: flat-to-slightly-negative in real terms from 2014 clear through 2021 (sibling
dossier, mumbai-inner.md §5), with no nominal crash at any point in that window.

**VOLUME: a real, but segment-concentrated, contraction — not a market-wide freeze.** Top-7-city new
launches fell ~44% over the five years after demonetisation while sales fell only ~12% over the same
window: developers retrenched on new supply far more sharply than buyers stopped buying, and existing
inventory kept clearing. The sharpest, most confidently-attributed hit landed on exactly the segment
every contemporaneous industry voice (CREDAI, NAREDCO, Anarock) predicted in real time — resale and
premium/luxury housing, the traditional cash reservoir — while end-user, bank-financed, primarily
non-metro demand (Bengaluru, Pune, Chennai per 2016-17 reporting) was explicitly described as least
affected.

**Verdict**: the 2016-17 stack removed 75-80% of the black money Anarock says was in HOUSING
specifically, concentrated almost entirely in the primary/branded segment where RERA's escrow rule
and GST's tax net actually reach — and the price level that segment sets barely moved, because the
marginal price-setting buyer there was already the formal, credit-linked end-user the shock never
touched. It did NOT crash the market, because "the market," for pricing purposes, was never as
cash-dependent at the margin as the aggregate cash-share numbers suggest. What it DID do was compress
new supply hard (the closest thing to a crash in the data, and a supply not a demand-side one) and
push whatever cash-intensity survives toward the two segments — land/agricultural and resale — that
the stack was never structurally built to reach, where the survey and enforcement evidence says it
persists essentially undiminished nine years on. A hypothetical FULL removal, run through the same
lens, would be expected to land the same way: predominantly a further VOLUME/transaction-count effect
concentrated in land, resale and tier-2/3 markets, not a fresh national price crash, because the
price-setting segment is already close to its no-cash equilibrium and the segments still exposed are
not the ones that set the index.

## Gaps and cautions

1. Every figure in this dossier is WebSearch-snippet-sourced, one level below the corroboration
   rule's preferred standard — no URL was independently opened and re-read; treat every number as
   provisional pending a direct pull, per the plan's §0 standard.
2. No official or academically-audited estimate of the cash share exists at all; every number found
   is an industry-consultant claim, an old study, a self-selected survey, or an anecdotal account.
   The SEGMENT TABLE is an ordering assembled from partial, non-contemporaneous, non-uniform sources
   — read it as a ranking, not as precise point estimates.
3. Anarock's 75-80% figure carries no disclosed measurement methodology beyond a compositional
   narrative and a launches/sales comparison (a supply metric); Anarock is commercially aligned with
   the branded/listed developers whose formalisation it credits — a named conflict, not a reason to
   discard the figure, but a reason not to treat it as audited.
4. The LocalCircles cash-prevalence figures (65-90%) come from one self-selected online-panel
   instrument with no benchmark to determine which direction its bias runs; 39,000 responses across
   310 districts is a large N but not a probability sample.
5. No segment-matched, contemporaneous, single-methodology cash-share split (primary vs secondary vs
   land vs agricultural) was found as one coherent table from one source — every comparison in this
   dossier stitches together partial reads from different sources and different years.
6. RBI HPI / NHB RESIDEX coverage (~10 major cities) does not include most tier-2/3 towns where cash
   is reported to persist most — the desk's own price evidence is systematically biased toward the
   metro/branded segment where cash has fallen the furthest, which likely makes the "sideways not
   crash" national read rosier than the true tier-2/3 picture.
7. The 2018-19 NBFC/IL&FS credit crisis and the 2020 COVID-19 shock both fall inside the "2016-2021"
   window the principal asked about and are NOT part of the cash-formalisation shock stack;
   separating their price/volume fingerprints from demonetisation's own lagged effects cannot be
   done cleanly from search snippets — this is a narrative-timeline read, not a quantified event
   study or regression.
8. All three international comparators are structurally different from India's case (BC = inbound/
   foreign laundering demand into a supply-constrained market; Pakistan = official-valuation
   catch-up, closer in shape but not independently quantified; South Korea = a 1990s all-asset
   real-name mandate with no real-estate-specific price data found) — none should be used to SIZE
   India's own effect, only to confirm the general volume-over-price shape.
9. GST's own black-money-relevant mechanics (ITC chain breaking at the land-sale/ready-property
   boundary) surfaced in the search results but were not independently verified beyond a single
   secondary characterisation; flagged as a minor, unresolved thread rather than a load-bearing claim
   in this dossier.
