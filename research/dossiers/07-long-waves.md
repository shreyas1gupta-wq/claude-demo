## 1. Findings and literature

### A. Long-run real rates

1. Schmelzing, Paul (2020), "Eight Centuries of Global Real Interest Rates, R-G, and the
   'Suprasecular' Decline, 1311–2018," Bank of England Staff Working Paper No. 845
   **[recall-verified]**. Assembles sovereign/quasi-sovereign real interest rate data across major
   economies (Italian city-states, Habsburg Spain/Low Countries, France, UK, US in succession) back
   to the 14th century. Central finding: a persistent, statistically significant **downward trend in
   global real rates averaging on the order of 1–2 basis points per year over the full 700-year
   sample** [VERIFY: exact point estimate], superimposed on very large multi-decade swings — the
   trend is centuries-long, but the swings around it, running **decades not centuries**, are the
   economically relevant object. The paper's own 2020 conclusion (pre-2022) was that this made
   "lower for longer" the modal forward case — a forecast the 2022–23 hiking cycle then visibly
   interrupted within two years: the single best worked illustration here of why even a
   rigorously-documented multi-century trend carries large, fast deviations, and why the CONTRACT's
   ±20% timing-uncertainty band on long cycles is not a throwaway caveat.

2. Jordà, Òscar; Schularick, Moritz; Taylor, Alan M. — the **Jordà-Schularick-Taylor (JST)
   Macrohistory Database** (macrohistory.net, free), documented in Jordà, Schularick & Taylor
   (2017), "Macrofinancial History and the New Business Cycle Facts," *NBER Macroeconomics Annual*
   2016, Vol. 31 (University of Chicago Press) **[recall-verified]**. Annual panel for ~17–18
   advanced economies since 1870 covering GDP, credit, house prices, and long/short interest rates —
   the single most important free data asset for this entire workstream, and the panel the CONTRACT
   explicitly directs research to pool on "where India alone offers <2 cycles" (Estimation
   Standards §9). It is also the primary underlying data source for Schmelzing's post-1870 sample
   and for most of the credit/house-price findings below.

### B. Debt supercycles

3. Reinhart, Carmen M. & Rogoff, Kenneth S. (2009), *This Time Is Different: Eight Centuries of
   Financial Folly*, Princeton University Press **[recall-verified]**. Assembles a crisis chronology
   across 66 countries and roughly eight centuries covering sovereign default, banking crises,
   currency crashes, and inflation crises. Two findings matter here: (i) **serial default** — most
   sovereigns that have defaulted once do so repeatedly across generations, a genuine,
   well-replicated "debt intolerance" finding; (ii) the "**this-time-is-different**" syndrome — the
   recurring belief, right before a crisis, that improved policy/institutions make historical
   debt/leverage limits inapplicable. This qualitative crisis chronology is large-sample (dozens of
   countries, hundreds of crisis-years) — genuinely Tier B/A by observation count, and a different,
   more defensible claim than #4.

4. Reinhart, Carmen M. & Rogoff, Kenneth S. (2010), "Growth in a Time of Debt," *American Economic
   Review Papers & Proceedings* 100(2): 573–578 **[recall-verified]**. The much narrower, much more
   famous companion result: public debt/GDP above ~90% associated with a sharp drop in median GDP
   growth (their original headline: average growth near or below zero above the threshold). This is
   the paper the CONTRACT's own instruction singles out for the honest critique below.

5. Herndon, Thomas; Ash, Michael; Pollin, Robert (2013), "Does High Public Debt Consistently Stifle
   Economic Growth? A Critique of Reinhart and Rogoff," *Cambridge Journal of Economics* 38(2):
   257–279 (first circulated as a University of Massachusetts Amherst PERI working paper, April
   2013) **[recall-verified on substance; exact journal page range — VERIFY]**. Found three distinct
   problems in the 2010 paper's Excel-based calculation: **(i) a spreadsheet coding error** that
   accidentally excluded five countries (Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark) from the
   >90%-debt bucket average; **(ii) selective exclusion** of certain high-debt/high-growth
   country-years (notably early post-WWII Australia/New Zealand/Canada, when several advanced
   economies carried very high wartime debt loads while growing strongly); **(iii) an unconventional
   equal-country weighting** scheme that let a single anomalous country-year (New Zealand, one year,
   sharply negative growth) carry the same weight as another country's twelve-plus years of ~2%
   growth. Correcting all three, Herndon-Ash-Pollin found average growth in the >90%-debt bucket was
   **positive, roughly +2.2%** — not the sharp negative "cliff" originally reported — though a
   milder negative association between higher debt and lower average growth still shows up in most
   honest reanalyses, with the causal direction genuinely ambiguous (slow growth plausibly *causes*
   high debt-to-GDP at least as much as the reverse, since GDP is the denominator). **Verdict for
   this design: the specific 90%-threshold "cliff" is discredited and must never appear as a
   parameter or a magic number (directly on point for CONTRACT §6's ban); the broader, softer
   correlation between debt trajectory and growth is a real but weak, non-threshold, likely
   reverse-causal-contaminated regularity** — usable only as a qualitative direction-of-travel input
   into a state variable, never as a hard rule.

### C. Reserve-currency and monetary-order transitions

6. Eichengreen, Barry (2011), *Exorbitant Privilege: The Rise and Fall of the Dollar and the Future
   of the International Monetary System*, Oxford University Press **[recall-verified]**. Documents
   the sterling-to-dollar reserve-currency transition (roughly 1914–1945, overlapping both world
   wars) as the one clean, completed transition in the modern floating/managed-FX era, and argues
   against the conventional wisdom that reserve status is winner-take-all — multiple currencies
   (dollar, euro, and potentially renminbi) can plausibly coexist as partial reserve assets
   simultaneously, network effects notwithstanding. Historical reserve-currency transitions took
   **decades**, driven by war finance, relative economic size, and (crucially) capital-account
   openness and rule-of-law depth of the rising power's financial markets — the last of which is the
   standing reason renminbi internationalization has been slower than raw GDP-share trends alone
   would predict.

7. Dalio, Ray (2021), *Principles for Dealing with the Changing World Order: Why Nations Succeed
   and Fail*, Avid Reader Press/Simon & Schuster **[recall-verified — Tier C practitioner, exactly as
   the CONTRACT itself names it]**. Proposes a "Big Cycle" (~100–250 years) of three interacting
   sub-cycles — a long-term debt cycle, an internal-order cycle (wealth/values-gap and political
   polarization), and an external-order cycle (great-power rivalry) — pattern-matched across the
   Dutch guilder, British pound, and US dollar reserve-currency eras, with an explicitly rising
   Chinese case as a fourth candidate. Dalio is explicit that this is historical pattern-recognition,
   not a fitted statistical model; the honest observation count is **n≈3 completed reserve-currency
   "Big Cycles"** in his own framework — well below any clock-test threshold. Useful as a structured
   narrative organizing principle for state variables that already have independent free-data
   proxies (below); **not** independently admissible as a return signal.

### D. The 15–20-year liquidity / real-estate cycle

8. Leamer, Edward E. (2007), "Housing IS the Business Cycle," NBER Working Paper 13428 (presented
   at the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City's Jackson Hole symposium) **[recall-verified]**. Shows
   residential investment is the most cyclically volatile major GDP component and, in the post-WWII
   US, **preceded 8 of the 10 recessions** studied — housing weakness is not just correlated with
   downturns but frequently leads them, operating through construction employment, mortgage-credit
   channels, and household-wealth effects. Directly on point for treating housing-credit growth as a
   leading, not coincident, state variable.

9. Glaeser, Edward L.; Gyourko, Joseph; Saiz, Albert (2008), "Housing Supply and Housing Bubbles,"
   *Journal of Urban Economics* 64(2): 198–217 **[recall-verified]**. Shows housing-supply elasticity
   (how easily new construction responds to price signals — land-use regulation, geography) is the
   key structural moderator of cycle amplitude: supply-inelastic markets get larger, longer booms
   and correspondingly sharper busts, while supply-elastic markets self-correct faster with smaller
   price swings. Mechanism: **(ii) capacity limit** — new supply takes years to permit and build
   regardless of price signal, so credit-fueled demand surges cannot be met quickly, mechanically
   generating multi-year cycle length. This is the one genuinely structural (not folk-numerological)
   argument in this section for why a real-estate cycle should run longer than a typical inventory or
   credit cycle.

10. Borio, Claudio (2014), "The Financial Cycle and Macroeconomics: What Have We Learnt?," *Journal
    of Banking & Finance* 45: 182–198 **[recall-verified]**. The BIS's own framework: a
    credit-plus-property-price "financial cycle," measured via band-pass/turning-point filtering,
    that averages **materially longer than the traditional business cycle — commonly cited in the
    8–20-year range**, with a small number of full cycles identifiable per advanced economy since
    1960s–70s data begins. **Direct overlap with dossier 03's credit-financial-cycle workstream** —
    flagged for joint reconciliation (Section 7) rather than treated as fully independent here; this
    dossier's contribution is specifically the property/real-estate leg and its multi-decade length,
    not the shorter bank-credit leg dossier 03 already owns.

11. The "18-year real estate cycle" (folk claim, marked as such per the CONTRACT's explicit
    instruction). Traceable to Homer Hoyt's *One Hundred Years of Land Values in Chicago* (University
    of Chicago Press, 1933) **[recall-verified on the book's existence and general finding; exact
    peak years — VERIFY]**, which documented a roughly 18-year spacing between Chicago land-value
    peaks. Carried forward by mid-century forecaster Roy Wenzlick and, most visibly, by Fred
    Harrison — whose book (commonly cited as *Boom Bust: House Prices, Banking and the Depression of
    2010*, 2005 **[VERIFY: exact publisher/date]**) is widely credited with anticipating the
    2008-era bust on 18-year-cycle grounds — and by Fred Foldvary, who works within Georgist
    land-value-tax theory (Henry George, *Progress and Poverty*, 1879) and is also reported to have
    flagged a 2008-adjacent downturn on similar grounds **[VERIFY: exact paper/date]**. Two honest
    cautions: **(a)** this is a heterodox Georgist tradition, not peer-reviewed mainstream
    macro-finance — a "folk claim," not implied academic legitimacy; **(b)** "successful"
    retrospective predictions like this carry their own survivorship-bias risk (the CONTRACT already
    flags this for backtests) — a forecaster naming one long approximate period will eventually be
    "right" near some multiple of it by chance, and only the hits get remembered. The mechanism
    (D#9, supply lag under credit-fueled demand) is real; the fixed "18" is not defensible as a hard
    number and must not enter the design as one — close enough in kind to CONTRACT §8's ban on
    fixed-period calendar cycles to warrant the same treatment even though not literally named there.

### E. Kuznets swings and the Kondratiev long wave

12. Kuznets, Simon (1930), *Secular Movements in Production and Prices*, Houghton Mifflin
    **[recall-verified on existence/author; this is the origin of what became known as "Kuznets
    swings"]**, later extended by Abramovitz, Moses (1968), "The Passing of the Kuznets Cycle,"
    *Economica* 35(140): 349–367 **[VERIFY: exact volume/page]**. Documented roughly 15–25-year
    ("~20-year") swings in US/UK growth rates historically correlated with long swings in
    immigration, residential construction, and capital formation — i.e., an early empirical cousin of
    the real-estate/liquidity cycle above, generated primarily from pre-WWII data. **Methodological
    caution, directly relevant to Section 1G below**: a nontrivial strand of the subsequent
    business-cycle-measurement literature has questioned whether Kuznets swings are a genuine
    periodic phenomenon or an artifact of the specific smoothing/detrending techniques used to
    extract them from noisy annual data — precisely the Slutzky-Yule mechanism (below) applied to an
    earlier generation of cycle research **[VERIFY: could not recall a single canonical
    "Kuznets-swings-are-an-artifact" citation with confidence this session; flagged as a literature
    question, not a settled fact, but the methodological risk itself is well established
    independent of this specific application]**. Recommendation: treat Kuznets swings as
    **corroborating evidence for the real-estate-cycle length prior (D above), not an independent
    state variable** — counting it separately would double-count the same underlying
    construction/credit mechanism.

13. Kondratiev (Kondratieff), Nikolai D. (1925), "The Major Economic Cycles" (Russian; English
    translation "The Long Waves in Economic Life" published in *Review of Economic Statistics*
    17(6), 1935) **[recall-verified on the existence/attribution; exact translation venue —
    VERIFY]**, adopted and popularized by Schumpeter, Joseph A. (1939), *Business Cycles: A
    Theoretical, Historical and Statistical Analysis of the Capitalist Process*, McGraw-Hill
    **[recall-verified]**, who nested Kondratiev (~50-year), Juglar (~7–11-year), and Kitchin
    (~3–5-year) cycles into a single multiplicative framework. Modern academic-adjacent successor:
    Perez, Carlota (2002), *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital: The Dynamics of Bubbles
    and Golden Ages*, Edward Elgar **[recall-verified]**, which reframes each ~50–60-year wave around
    a specific technology's installation-bubble-crash-deployment arc (canals, railways,
    steel/electricity, oil/automobiles/mass production, IT/telecom). Even granting ~5 waves over
    ~250 years (nominally clearing the ≥4 clock-test count), **each wave is driven by a different,
    non-repeating technological mechanism** — this is pattern-matching across distinct historical
    episodes assigned a common label, not one mechanism recurring with a stable period, and it is
    exactly the kind of construct CONTRACT §8 excludes by naming Elliott Wave/Gann/fixed-period
    calendar cycles as forbidden moves. **Recommendation: exclude Kondratiev/Perez-style long waves
    from the design entirely**, flagged here as a design recommendation for principal sign-off since
    it is not literally named in the CONTRACT's trap list, only close to it in kind.

### F. Demographics

14. Bloom, David E. & Williamson, Jeffrey G. (1998), "Demographic Transitions and Economic Miracles
    in Emerging Asia," *World Bank Economic Review* 12(3): 419–455 **[recall-verified]**. The
    founding empirical estimate of the "demographic dividend": attributes a substantial share
    (commonly cited as roughly one-quarter to one-third) of East Asia's post-1965 growth "miracle"
    to the mechanical effect of a rising working-age share relative to dependents, as fertility fell
    faster than mortality during the demographic transition.

15. Bloom, David E.; Canning, David; Sevilla, Jaypee (2003), *The Demographic Dividend: A New
    Perspective on the Economic Consequences of Population Change*, RAND Corporation, MR-1274
    **[recall-verified]**. The critical qualifier missing from most popular "India's demographic
    dividend" commentary: the dividend is **conditional, not automatic** — it requires the working-age
    cohort to actually find productive employment (education quality, labor-market flexibility,
    female labor-force participation, health investment); a large working-age cohort *without* job
    absorption is a dependency-ratio-shaped opportunity, not a guaranteed growth outcome, and can
    instead become a social/political liability ("youth bulge" unemployment risk). This is the
    single most important qualifier for India's case (Section 2).

16. Goodhart, Charles & Pradhan, Manoj (2020), *The Great Demographic Reversal: Ageing Societies,
    Waning Inequality, and an Inflation Revival*, Palgrave Macmillan **[recall-verified]**. Argues
    the last three-plus decades of low global inflation, low real rates, and rising inequality were
    substantially caused by a **one-off demographic dividend** — the entry of a huge, cheap,
    underemployed labor supply (China's internal migration, Eastern Europe post-1989, women's rising
    labor-force participation in the West) into the effective global workforce, which is now
    **reversing** as those cohorts age out, China's working-age population shrinks, and the labor
    supply shock cannot repeat. Their forecast: structurally higher inflation and higher real rates
    ahead as aging societies dis-save and labor bargaining power returns. **This is the direct
    intellectual counterpoint to Schmelzing's "suprasecular decline" (finding #1)** — two
    respectable, evidence-based macro-history views pointing in opposite directions for the coming
    decades' real-rate path. The honest synthesis is not to pick a winner but to note that both
    agree on the *mechanism that matters* (demographic labor-supply shifts move real rates), and
    that the 2022–2023 realized rate spike is at minimum consistent with Goodhart-Pradhan's timing
    being closer to correct than Schmelzing's own 2020 "lower for longer" framing — a second
    concrete illustration of long-wave timing uncertainty cutting against a well-evidenced trend
    within a couple of years.

17. UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs, **World Population Prospects** (biennial revision,
    free; most recent widely-cited edition as of training is **2022**, a 2024 revision likely exists
    by now **[VERIFY]**). India overtook China as the world's most populous country around **2023**
    per UN projections **[VERIFY exact crossover date]**; total population projected (2022-revision,
    medium variant) to peak around the **mid-2060s near ~1.7 billion** before declining **[VERIFY:
    exact peak year/level, likely revised since]**. India's total fertility rate (TFR) fell **below
    replacement (~2.0) nationally around 2019–2020** per SRS/NFHS-5 **[VERIFY exact year/figure]** —
    a materially faster decline than older projections assumed, mechanically **compressing** the
    dividend window versus a "to 2045–2055" framing built on higher-fertility assumptions. India's
    working-age (15–64)/dependency-ratio trough is commonly cited (Economic Survey editions) as
    **~2036–2041** nationally **[VERIFY exact year/edition]**, with substantial **regional
    divergence**: southern/western states (Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh) are already past their
    own dividend peak, aging on a schedule closer to middle-income East Asia, while northern/eastern
    states (Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Madhya Pradesh) remain younger and still rising — "India's
    demographic dividend" is a **staggered, regionally uneven** phenomenon, not one national clock
    **[medium confidence on direction; state-level dates — VERIFY]**.

### G. The statistical case against periodicity

This is the load-bearing methodological section: the formal argument for why anything with fewer
than ~4 observed complete periods must be treated as a state variable with Tier-C, reduce-only
authority and wide (≥±20%) timing uncertainty — not a milder version of the same argument, but the
actual reason the bar exists.

18. Slutzky, Eugen (1937), "The Summation of Random Causes as the Source of Cyclic Processes,"
    *Econometrica* 5(2): 105–146 (English revision of a 1927 Russian original published by the
    Moscow Conjuncture Institute) **[recall-verified]**. Demonstrates constructively that summing or
    moving-averaging a sequence of purely random, independent shocks (e.g., lottery-digit draws)
    produces a series that **looks like a smooth, quasi-periodic wave** to the naked eye and to
    naive visual cycle-spotting — with no genuine periodic mechanism anywhere in the data-generating
    process. This is the founding demonstration that "I can see a cycle in this chart" is not
    evidence of one; smoothing/filtering operations manufacture apparent cycles out of noise.

19. Yule, George Udny (1927), "On a Method of Investigating Periodicities in Disturbed Series, with
    Special Reference to Wolfer's Sunspot Numbers," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society
    A* 226: 267–298 **[recall-verified]**. Complementary mechanism: shows that a simple second-order
    autoregressive process (AR(2)) with complex characteristic roots generates **damped,
    pseudo-cyclical oscillations** indistinguishable by eye from a genuine deterministic cycle,
    without any true periodic forcing function. Together, Slutzky and Yule established the two
    canonical ways an apparent "cycle" can be entirely spurious: **filtering-induced** (Slutzky) and
    **stochastic-difference-equation-induced** (Yule) — both of which any long-wave claim built from
    smoothed multi-century series (real rates, land values, credit aggregates) must be checked
    against before being taken as a real periodic mechanism rather than persistence dressed up as a
    cycle.

20. Granger, Clive W. J. (1966), "The Typical Spectral Shape of an Economic Variable," *Econometrica*
    34(1): 150–161 **[recall-verified]**. Shows that the power spectrum of most detrended economic
    time series has a **"typical shape"**: power concentrated overwhelmingly at very low frequencies
    (the long run/trend), monotonically declining as frequency rises, with a modest bump only at the
    seasonal frequency — **no distinct peak at any "business cycle" frequency**, as a genuine
    repeating periodic mechanism would produce. Direct implication: what looks like "a business
    cycle" or "a long wave" in most macro series is better described as a **continuum of persistence
    dominated by low frequencies (a highly autocorrelated, near-unit-root process)**, not a discrete
    periodic phenomenon with an identifiable length — this is the single strongest piece of formal
    spectral evidence for the CONTRACT's own choice to rank state variables by `tau_half`
    (persistence) rather than by an assumed cycle length.

21. Nelson, Charles R. & Plosser, Charles I. (1982), "Trends and Random Walks in Macroeconomic Time
    Series: Some Evidence and Implications," *Journal of Monetary Economics* 10(2): 139–162
    **[recall-verified]**. Tests most major US macro series and finds most are better characterized
    as **difference-stationary (containing a stochastic trend / unit root)** rather than
    trend-stationary with a deterministic cyclical component around a fixed trend — reinforcing
    Granger's spectral finding from the time-domain side and further undermining any claim of a
    fixed-length deterministic cycle sitting underneath macro data.

22. Hamilton, James D. (2018), "Why You Should Never Use the Hodrick-Prescott Filter," *Review of
    Economics and Statistics* 100(5): 831–843 **[recall-verified — already the CONTRACT's own trap
    #7 citation]**. Shows the HP filter (i) introduces spurious dynamic relationships not present in
    the underlying series (a modern, filter-specific instance of the Slutzky-Yule mechanism), (ii)
    produces end-of-sample ("endpoint") values that are unreliable and revise heavily as new data
    arrives — a live problem for any real-time cycle-timing signal, and (iii) uses a smoothing
    parameter (λ=1600 quarterly) with no principled connection to the data's own statistical
    properties. Proposes a **regression-based alternative** (regress the series h periods ahead on
    its own current and recent lags, e.g. h=8 quarters, and treat the residual as the "cycle") as a
    filter free of these three defects. The CONTRACT already bans the HP filter and mandates this
    Hamilton regression filter for all cycle-extraction in this design (Trap #7); this dossier's
    contribution is establishing *why*, formally, for the long-wave block specifically, where
    filtering artifacts are the single greatest risk given how few genuine degrees of freedom
    multi-decade/century data provides.

23. **Periodogram/spectral significance with small n** (a standard result in time-series statistics,
    not a single citable paper — stated here as an argument, consistent with the CONTRACT's
    instruction that research may build its own argument with provenance). A periodogram or Fisher
    g-test for a peak at a hypothesized frequency needs **many independent realizations of the
    hypothesized period** to have statistical power to distinguish "a real periodic component" from
    "a red-noise (highly autocorrelated) process that happens to produce one long apparent swing in
    a finite sample" — informally, resolving a period of length L to reasonable confidence needs a
    sample many multiples of L long, and even then a persistent-but-non-periodic AR(1)-type process
    with a root near (but below) 1 will generate periodogram peaks at low frequencies that are
    visually and statistically hard to distinguish from a genuine cycle at conventional sample
    lengths. **Formal conclusion for this workstream**: a claimed 50–250-year long wave observed
    over 150–800 years of data yields at most 1–4 non-overlapping realizations — even before
    confronting the further problem that the *mechanism* generating each "realization" is usually
    different (gold standard vs. fiat money, agrarian vs. industrial vs. digital economy, as in
    Kondratiev/Perez above), meaning the realizations are not even draws from the same
    data-generating process. **A periodogram test is therefore not just underpowered but often not
    even well-posed for these claims.** This is the formal argument underneath the CONTRACT's own
    clock-test threshold, and the reason this dossier treats the ≥4-period bar as, if anything,
    already generous rather than overly strict — genuine statistical identification of a repeating
    mechanism at these timescales realistically needs closer to 8–10 clean, structurally comparable
    realizations, which essentially nothing in this section has.

## 2. India-specific evidence

**Public debt/GDP trajectory.** India's general government (Centre + States combined) debt/GDP
ratio is commonly cited in the **~80–85% range** in recent years, up sharply from the
high-60s/low-70s pre-COVID, driven by the FY2020–21 pandemic fiscal expansion **[VERIFY: exact
current figure and source vintage — IMF WEO/Fiscal Monitor gives one figure, RBI's own Handbook of
Statistics another, definitions differ (gross vs. net, Centre-only vs. combined)]**. The FRBM Review
Committee (chaired by N. K. Singh, report 2017) set a target general-government debt/GDP ceiling of
**~60% by FY2024–25 (40% Centre + 20% States)** **[VERIFY: exact figures/date]** — a target that
pandemic-era borrowing pushed well out of reach on the original timeline, though the FRBM Act's own
amendment history (glide-path revisions in most subsequent Union Budgets) shows the target itself
being repeatedly re-set rather than enforced as a hard rule — a small domestic illustration of the
"this-time-is-different" flexibility Reinhart-Rogoff document more broadly (finding #3), and a
reminder that a debt/GDP *ceiling* number is a policy aspiration, not a market-enforced constraint,
for a country that borrows overwhelmingly in its own currency from a captive domestic banking system
(SLR-driven G-Sec demand) — precisely the condition under which "fiscal dominance" concerns (Section
3/4 below) become live rather than academic.

**Real rates and financial repression.** India ran persistently **negative-to-low real policy
rates through much of the 2000s–early 2010s** (nominal repo rate below CPI inflation for extended
stretches), partly a financial-repression artifact of the SLR (statutory liquidity ratio) requiring
banks to hold a mandated share of deposits in government securities regardless of yield — a captive
buyer base that let the sovereign borrow cheaply relative to free-market pricing. The 2016 adoption
of a formal flexible inflation-targeting framework (Monetary Policy Committee, 4%±2% CPI target)
produced a **structural break** toward more consistently positive-to-moderate real rates thereafter
— meaning India's own real-rate history offers a genuine regime discontinuity around 2016 that
complicates any naive pooling with Schmelzing's centuries-long developed-economy series; the correct
treatment (Section 6) is to treat pre-2016 and post-2016 India as **different regimes**, not one
continuous series, exactly the same discipline dossier 06 already applied to the July-2024 gold-duty
level break.

**Reserve status and RBI's own gold accumulation.** India is a **receiver**, not an issuer, of
reserve-currency-regime effects — INR is not a reserve currency, so India experiences global
dollar-funding tightening (Fed hiking cycles, dollar strength) as an imported shock via FII debt/
equity outflows and INR depreciation (the 2013 taper-tantrum episode already documented in dossier
06 is the canonical instance). RBI's own foreign-exchange reserves (among the world's largest,
commonly cited around $600bn+ in recent years **[VERIFY: current level]**) are themselves a direct,
policy-driven hedge against a repeat of the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis — a standing
institutional memory that shapes RBI's reserve-management conservatism to this day. Separately and
more relevant to the gold-floor question specifically: **RBI has been a net buyer of gold for its
own reserves in recent years**, adding meaningfully to sovereign gold holdings through the early-to-
mid 2020s **[VERIFY: exact annual tonnage figures — recalled directionally from press commentary,
not from a primary RBI release this session]**, a free, India-specific, publicly disclosed
data point (RBI's Weekly Statistical Supplement and Annual Report both report reserve composition)
that directly complements dossier 06's WGC global-central-bank-buying proxy with an India-specific
instance of the same mechanism, rather than relying solely on the aggregate global series.

**Real estate/liquidity cycle — one observed instance, cannot clock-test domestically.** Post-1991
India offers exactly **one** clean up-cycle/down-cycle/recovery sequence: a credit- and NBFC-funded
construction boom roughly 2003–2013, a prolonged residential slump roughly **2013–2020** (worst in
NCR/Mumbai), compounded mid-slump by demonetization (Nov 2016, hit cash-heavy resale transactions),
GST (Jul 2017, restructured developer input-credit economics), RERA (2016–17 rollout, raised
developer working-capital needs), and then the **IL&FS default (Sep 2018)** and ensuing NBFC/HFC
liquidity crunch that cut off developers' main post-2008 funding channel. Recovery began roughly
**2021** (low rates, larger-home demand, inventory absorption); by 2024–2026 several markets show
renewed strength, with some commentary flagging affordability stretch by 2025–26 **[VERIFY:
magnitude/dating not independently confirmed this session]**. Trough-to-trough, this gives a length
of order **13–15 years** — inside the global 10–20-year range, but **n=1** for India alone; no
domestic clock test is possible, and the length should not be quoted as an India-calibrated
constant.

**Demographics — India's own position on the arc.** India sits, as of 2026, still inside its
national demographic-dividend window on most conventional dating (dependency-ratio trough
**~2036–2041**), but with two qualifiers from finding #17: **(a)** the window is narrower than older
"to 2045–2055" framings because realized fertility fell faster than assumed (TFR below replacement
since ~2019–2020); **(b)** the dividend is **regionally staggered** — the south already past its
peak, the Hindi-belt north/east still rising — so national aggregates mask a two-speed reality with
different state-level fiscal (pension/healthcare) and consumption implications. Per Bloom-Canning-
Sevilla (finding #15), the payoff also depends on absorbing the cohort into productive employment —
India's female labor-force participation has historically run low relative to income peers (MOSPI
PLFS-measurable), the most-cited reason a realized dividend could underperform the mechanical
demographic arithmetic.

## 3. Decay and crowding assessment

Long waves are categorically different from the crowdable anomalies the McLean-Pontiff framework
addresses (CONTRACT §5) — nobody arbitrages away a government's fiscal incentives or a country's age
structure. The relevant question per edge is not "how much has arbitrage capital eroded this,"
but "does this even qualify as a mechanism with a survival argument, or is it just narrative
pattern-matching," and, separately, "how much should the sizing be discounted for pure statistical
overfitting risk given the tiny sample."

| Edge / state variable | Survival argument | Decay/haircut applied |
|---|---|---|
| Debt trajectory / fiscal-dominance mechanism (public debt/GDP level + slope, persistence of negative real rates, active reserve-diversification) | **(iv) institutional constraint**: a sovereign's incentive to inflate away or financially-repress a large domestic-currency debt overhang is a structural political-economy fact, not a trade that gets crowded — no counterparty capital "arbitrages away" a government's own policy choice. | No McLean-Pontiff-style crowding haircut (mechanism doesn't decay under arbitrage); instead a **narrow-influence haircut**: n<2 completed modern fiscal-dominance episodes globally in the fiat era (arguably only the 1970s developed-world stagflation/inflation-away-the-debt episode qualifies cleanly), so treat as Tier C, reduce-only, narrow numeric influence cap (Section 4). |
| Reserve-currency/dedollarization tilt | **(iv) institutional constraint** (network effects/first-mover advantage in reserve-currency status are genuinely slow to arbitrage) but explicitly narrative — Dalio's own n≈3, Eichengreen's one clean completed transition. | No crowding decay applicable; instead treated exactly as dossier 06 already treats its central-bank-buying-regime input — **zero assumed persistence beyond the observed series**, Tier C by the clock test, may only add to the gold floor, never justify removing it. This dossier does not re-litigate dossier 06's own number, only supplies the mechanism argument underneath it. |
| Real-estate/liquidity cycle mechanism (supply-lag/credit-amplification, Glaeser-Gyourko) | **(ii) capacity limit**: construction permitting/build-out genuinely takes years regardless of price signal — a hard physical/regulatory capacity constraint, not an arbitrage-competable one. | No crowding decay on the mechanism itself; the **length prior specifically** gets a heavy haircut on precision (use a 10–20-year range, never an 18-year point estimate) because of the folk-numerology risk documented in finding #11 and the small-n spectral-identification problem in finding #23. India's own n=1 realization gets essentially zero independent weight beyond corroborating that the length sits inside the global range. |
| Kuznets swings | Not treated as an independent edge — subsumed into the real-estate-cycle length prior (finding #12's recommendation) to avoid double-counting the same construction/credit mechanism twice. | N/A — no separate parameter proposed. |
| Kondratiev/Perez long wave | **No defensible survival argument.** Each wave's proposed mechanism is a different, non-repeating technology-diffusion story; this is historical analogy with a common label, not one persistent mechanism. | **Recommend exclusion from the design entirely** (Section 1E), flagged for principal sign-off since it is not literally named in CONTRACT §8's forbidden list, only close to it in kind. | Long-run real-rate suprasecular decline (Schmelzing) | Mechanically nearly self-evident as a trend (opportunity-cost arithmetic across centuries), but explicitly **not** given a crowding-decay treatment because it isn't an anomaly — it's a slow-moving macro trend. | The relevant discipline is instead a **structural-break caution**: despite superficially clearing an n≥4 count if sub-periods are counted mechanically, the mechanism differs so much across 700 years (feudal fiscal systems vs. central-bank fiat regimes) that pooling them as "the same cycle" is methodologically unsound — kept Tier C/state-variable by argument, not promoted to Tier B on a technicality. ## 5. Evidence-tier recommendations

- **Debt trajectory / fiscal-dominance mechanism**: Tier C. The qualitative "serial
  default"/"debt intolerance" finding underneath it (Reinhart-Rogoff's crisis chronology) is
  large-sample and closer to Tier A/B on its own observation count (dozens of countries, hundreds of
  crisis-years via JST/Reinhart-Rogoff), but the *specific long-wave claim* — a debt supercycle with
  a repeating length — has fewer than 2 completed instances in the modern fiat-money era, hence Tier
  C for the cycle-timing use case, even though the underlying qualitative mechanism is well
  supported.
- **90%-debt-threshold growth effect**: **rejected outright, no tier** — Herndon-Ash-Pollin's
  correction is itself well-replicated and effectively settled; this must never be re-admitted as a
  parameter under any tier.
- **Reserve-currency transition / dedollarization**: Tier C. n=1 clean completed modern transition
  (sterling→dollar), one possibly-ongoing, incomplete instance (USD relative decline since the
  2000s–2010s). Identical treatment to dossier 06's own central-bank-buying input, which this
  dossier supplies the mechanism for rather than re-deriving.
- **Real-estate/liquidity-cycle mechanism (supply lag, credit amplification)**: Tier B via the
  cross-country JST/Borio panel (many country-decades of house-price and credit data support the
  mechanism); Tier C for any specific length point-estimate, and Tier C outright for India
  domestically (n=1, no clock test possible).
- **Kuznets swings**: Not separately tiered — folded into the real-estate-cycle mechanism above to
  avoid double-counting; flagged as carrying its own artifact risk (Slutzky-Yule) independent of
  that folding decision.
- **Kondratiev/Perez long wave**: Not tiered — recommended for exclusion (Section 3/4), pending
  principal override.
- **Demographic data (population, age structure, fertility)**: Tier A. Extremely large observation
  count (UN WPP + national censuses across ~200 countries and decades; India's own SRS/NFHS series
  running annually since the 1970s), among the best-measured social-science data that exists.
- **Demographic-dividend → growth/return causal translation**: Tier C. Conditional on labor-market
  absorption (Bloom-Canning-Sevilla), with no India-specific quantification of the conditional
  probability reviewed here — treat any specific bps-of-growth claim as narrative, not a fitted
  parameter.
- **Long-run real-rate suprasecular decline (Schmelzing)**: Tier C by argument (structural-break
  risk across 700 years of differing monetary regimes), despite nominally clearing an n≥4 count if
  sub-periods were counted mechanically — an explicit, written rejection of a technical
  Tier-B promotion, consistent with CONTRACT §5's "we are not slaves to the literature" license.
- **Statistical/methodological findings (Slutzky, Yule, Granger, Nelson-Plosser, Hamilton)**: Treated
  as **Tier-A methodological consensus** (decades of replication, standard graduate-econometrics
  material) directly underwriting the CONTRACT's own clock-test/`tau_half` framework — not
  "findings" requiring their own tier in the return-signal sense, but the epistemic foundation the
  whole long-wave section's tiering rests on.

## 6. Research method for the data phase

- **Debt-trajectory state variable.** Pull IMF Fiscal Monitor/WEO general-government debt/GDP
  (free) and cross-check RBI's Handbook of Statistics and Union Budget Medium-Term Fiscal Policy
  Statement (free, indiabudget.gov.in) for the Centre+State combined figure; compute a **5-year
  trend slope**, never a level threshold (Herndon-Ash-Pollin forecloses any "90%"-style magic
  number). Flag the FY2020–21 pandemic borrowing surge as an explicit **level break**, not fit
  through — same discipline dossier 06 applied to the July-2024 gold-duty cut.
- **Real-rate persistence.** Apply Hamilton's (2018) regression filter — never HP (CONTRACT trap
  #7) — to US 10y TIPS yield (FRED `DFII10`) and India's repo-minus-CPI proxy (RBI DBIE + MOSPI,
  post-2016 sample only, given the inflation-targeting structural break in Section 2). Estimate
  `tau_half` via AR(1) on the filtered gap over overlapping windows, reporting a confidence
  interval, not a point estimate — reusing dossier 03's already-established credit-cycle method
  rather than a parallel recipe.
- **Reserve-diversification / dedollarization tilt.** Pull IMF COFER (free, quarterly) for the
  global USD reserve-share trend and RBI's Weekly Statistical Supplement/Annual Report (free) for
  RBI's own gold-reserve tonnage. Treat both as exogenous conditioning state variables, not fitted
  as a regime-switch (CONTRACT trap #9's ≥10-transition rule) — identical discipline to dossier 06's
  WGC-series treatment.
- **Real-estate/liquidity-cycle proxy.** Primary: RBI House Price Index (RBI DBIE, free, quarterly
  since 2010). Supporting: RBI sectoral bank credit to housing (monthly, free) and MOSPI
  construction GVA (free). **Exclude** proprietary inventory trackers (Knight Frank, Liases Foras,
  PropEquity, ANAROCK) as non-free; substitute RBI Financial Stability Report data on
  construction/real-estate NPA and stressed-asset ratios (free, semi-annual) where an
  inventory-stress proxy is needed. Flag NHB Residex's continuity and the 2021 Census's delayed
  status as data-availability risks, with UN World Urbanization Prospects/SRS as free substitutes
  **[VERIFY current Census timeline]**.
- **Demographics.** UN World Population Prospects (free) for age-structure projections; SRS annual
  bulletins (Registrar General of India, free) and NFHS rounds (free) for India-specific fertility
  cross-checks; MOSPI PLFS (free) to track **actual** labor-force-participation realization of the
  dividend, per Bloom-Canning-Sevilla, rather than assuming automaticity. Re-pull each UN WPP
  revision at every research refresh; treat any projection beyond ~15 years out as directional
  only, with widening uncertainty.
- **Statistical hygiene for every long-wave state variable.** Apply Hamilton's filter uniformly
  (never HP). Before treating any claimed period length as a "cycle," report the number of complete
  non-overlapping realizations available and **refuse a periodogram-significance test below 4**
  (the clock test, and finding #23's formal argument) — report `tau_half` with a confidence interval
  from overlapping-window AR(1) instead, mirroring dossier 03's method so the whole program uses one
  recipe.
- **Registry discipline.** Every long-wave state variable in Section 4 must be logged in the
  versioned registry (CONTRACT §10) tagged Tier C, reduce-only, with an explicit numeric
  **influence-cap field** (the pp caps proposed in Section 4) enforced in CI, and a hard zero on any
  leverage- or beta-adding use — a registry using any of these to add risk must fail to load.
